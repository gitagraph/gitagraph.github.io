What's on your mind?

  • Anxious or worriedThe Gita treats worry as the mind being thrown about by things that come and go.
  • Unsure of my duty or what to doThe whole Gita begins here: Arjuna does not know what he should do.
  • Grieving a lossKrishna's first teaching is spoken to someone overwhelmed by grief.
  • Angry or resentfulThe Gita traces anger back to wanting: brooding on something breeds attachment, attachment breeds desire, and blocked desire turns into anger.
  • Afraid of failingYour part is the work, not the outcome.
  • Can't focusArjuna himself says the mind is as hard to hold as the wind.
  • Feeling low or without purposeThe Gita says you can lift yourself, that no sincere effort is wasted, and that no one who does good comes to a bad end.
  • Who am I?The Gita separates the body, which it calls the field, from the one who knows it.
  • Afraid of deathThe Upanishads put this fear in the mouth of a boy who walks into Death's house and asks.
  • How should I live?Speak the truth, give, hold yourself back, be kind, enjoy without grabbing: the Upanishads give short, practical answers alongside the deep ones.
  • Hard to forgiveThe Gita names forgiveness and freedom from ill will among the qualities it praises.
  • Comparing myself to othersThe Gita describes a person free from envy, content with what comes, and steady through success and failure.
  • Never satisfied, always wanting moreThe Gita describes desire as difficult to satisfy, while the Ashtavakra Gita connects craving with bondage.
  • Hurt by criticismThe Gita and the Ashtavakra Gita describe a person who remains steady through praise and criticism, honour and dishonour.
  • Ego and prideThe Gita distinguishes pride and arrogance from the mistaken sense of being the sole doer.
  • How much money is enough?These passages approach wealth from different perspectives.
  • Looking for peace of mindThe Gita connects peace with steadiness, freedom from craving, and devotion to Krishna.
  • A mistake I can't get pastThe Gita describes spiritual knowledge as capable of overcoming even grave wrongdoing.
  • Avoiding what needs to be doneThe Gita distinguishes action from inaction and describes negligence and inertia among the effects of tamas.
  • Am I ever truly alone?The Gita describes Krishna as present to all beings and as their friend.
  • I can't stop overthinking everythingAshtavakra says most of our suffering is made by anxious thinking itself, and that there is nothing you need to work out before you can rest.
  • My heart is broken. How do I move on?Ashtavakra says that the people and things we hold dear are brief, like a dream, and that coming and going is simply the nature of things.
  • I'm too attached to one personAshtavakra says that clinging has never kept anyone or anything from being lost, and the Upanishad story of Maitreyi says we love others for the sake of the Self.
  • Work pressure is wearing me outThe texts look at work pressure from the inside: Ashtavakra says restlessness can stay even when nothing is being done, and that only calm relieves the heat of duties.
  • I have a big decision to make and I'm confusedThe texts do not hand over a ready answer; they show people who are confused, who ask plainly, and who weigh advice before choosing.
  • I'm scared of what people will sayThe texts describe a person who is not shaken by praise or blame, and they speak of standing by one's own truth and one's own work even when it is imperfect.
  • I don't believe in myselfThe texts speak to self-doubt by pointing to what they say a person really is, and by saying that how one sees oneself shapes what one becomes.
  • There is constant fighting in my familyThe texts do not say who is right in a family quarrel; they describe an even mind that does not swing with each harsh word, and speech that is true without wounding.
  • I know it's wrong, but I keep doing itThe texts treat this as an old and ordinary problem: Arjuna himself asks what pushes a person to do wrong against their own wish, and Krishna names desire and anger.
  • Why do bad things keep happening to me?The texts do not explain why a particular misfortune reached a particular person, and they do not say anyone has earned it.
