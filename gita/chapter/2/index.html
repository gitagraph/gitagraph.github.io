Chapter 2 · 72 verses

Sankhya Yoga

साङ्ख्ययोग

The Gita in miniature. Krishna argues that the Self (atman) is never born and never dies, so grief for the body is misplaced; that a warrior's own duty is to fight; and that one should act without attachment to results. Closes with the portrait of the sthitaprajna, the person of steady wisdom.

गीता का सार। कृष्ण समझाते हैं कि आत्मा न जन्म लेती है न मरती है, इसलिए शरीर के लिए शोक व्यर्थ है; कि योद्धा का अपना धर्म युद्ध करना है; और कि फल की आसक्ति छोड़कर कर्म करना चाहिए। अध्याय का अंत स्थितप्रज्ञ, यानी स्थिर बुद्धि वाले मनुष्य, के वर्णन से होता है।

  1. 2.1

    To him, overcome with pity, his eyes troubled and full of tears, sinking in despair, Madhusudana spoke these words.

  2. 2.2

    Where has this weakness come from, Arjuna, at such a critical moment? It is not fitting for a noble person; it leads neither to heaven nor to honour.

  3. 2.3

    Do not give in to unmanliness, Partha; it does not suit you. Cast off this petty faintness of heart and rise, scorcher of foes.

  4. 2.4

    Madhusudana, slayer of enemies, how can I fight back with arrows in battle against Bhishma and Drona, who deserve my reverence?

  5. 2.5

    Better to live in this world even on begged food than to kill these noble elders. If I kill them, though they seek worldly gain, every pleasure I enjoy here will be stained with blood.

  6. 2.6

    Nor do we know which is better for us — that we conquer them or that they conquer us. The sons of Dhritarashtra stand before us, and if we killed them we would not even wish to live.

  7. 2.7

    My nature is overcome by the flaw of faint-hearted pity, and my mind is confused about dharma. I ask you: tell me clearly what is truly good for me. I am your disciple; I have come to you for refuge — guide me.

  8. 2.8

    I see nothing that could drive away this grief that is drying up my senses — not even gaining an unrivalled, prosperous kingdom on earth, or lordship over the gods.

  9. 2.9

    Having spoken thus to Hrishikesha, O scorcher of foes, Gudakesha said to Govinda, “I will not fight,” and fell silent.

  10. 2.10

    Then, O Bharata, between the two armies, Hrishikesha, as if smiling, spoke these words to the despairing Arjuna.

  11. 2.11

    You grieve for those who need no grief, yet you speak words that sound wise. The truly wise grieve neither for the dead nor for the living.

  12. 2.12

    Never was there a time when I did not exist, nor you, nor these kings; nor will any of us ever cease to be hereafter.

  13. 2.13

    Just as the embodied self passes through childhood, youth and old age in this body, so it passes on to another body. The steady person is not confused by this.

  14. 2.14

    Contacts of the senses with their objects, son of Kunti, give rise to cold and heat, pleasure and pain. They come and go and do not last; bear them patiently, Bharata.

  15. 2.15

    The steady person whom these do not disturb, finest of men, who stays the same in pain and pleasure, is fit for immortality.

  16. 2.16

    The unreal has no existence, and the real never ceases to be. Those who see the truth have understood the final nature of both.

  17. 2.17

    Know that what pervades all this is indestructible. No one can bring about the destruction of that which is imperishable.

  18. 2.18

    These bodies are said to come to an end, but the embodied self within them is eternal, indestructible and beyond measure. Therefore fight, Bharata.

  19. 2.19

    Whoever thinks this self is a killer, and whoever thinks it is killed — neither understands. It does not kill, and it is not killed.

  20. 2.20

    It is never born and never dies; it did not come into being, and it will not cease to be. Unborn, eternal, everlasting and ancient, it is not killed when the body is killed.

  21. 2.21

    Partha, how can a person who knows this self to be indestructible, eternal, unborn and unchanging kill anyone, or cause anyone to be killed?

  22. 2.22

    As a person discards worn-out clothes and puts on new ones, so the embodied self discards worn-out bodies and enters new ones.

  23. 2.23

    Weapons cannot cut it, fire cannot burn it, water cannot wet it, and wind cannot dry it.

  24. 2.24

    It cannot be cut, burned, wetted or dried. It is eternal, all-pervading, stable, unmoving and timeless.

  25. 2.25

    It is called unmanifest, beyond thought and unchanging. So, knowing it to be such, you should not grieve.

  26. 2.26

    And even if you think it is constantly born and constantly dies, even then, mighty-armed one, you should not grieve like this.

  27. 2.27

    For death is certain for whoever is born, and birth is certain for whoever dies. So you should not grieve over what cannot be avoided.

  28. 2.28

    Beings are unmanifest in their beginning, manifest in their middle, and unmanifest again at their end, Bharata. What is there to lament in that?

  29. 2.29

    One person sees this self as a marvel, another speaks of it as a marvel, another hears of it as a marvel — and even after hearing of it, no one truly knows it.

  30. 2.30

    The self dwelling in every body can never be slain, Bharata. Therefore you should not grieve for any being.

  31. 2.31

    Considering your own duty, too, you should not waver. For a kshatriya there is nothing better than a righteous war.

  32. 2.32

    Fortunate are the kshatriyas, Partha, who meet such a war, come of its own accord — an open door to heaven.

  33. 2.33

    But if you will not fight this righteous battle, then, having abandoned your duty and your honour, you will incur sin.

  34. 2.34

    People will tell of your disgrace forever, and for one who has been honoured, disgrace is worse than death.

  35. 2.35

    The great chariot-warriors will think you withdrew from battle out of fear, and those who held you in high regard will think little of you.

  36. 2.36

    Your enemies will say many unspeakable things, mocking your strength. What could be more painful than that?

  37. 2.37

    Either you are killed and attain heaven, or you win and enjoy the earth. So rise, son of Kunti, resolved to fight.

  38. 2.38

    Treating pleasure and pain, gain and loss, victory and defeat alike, prepare for battle. In this way you will not incur sin.

  39. 2.39

    This understanding has been explained to you in terms of Sankhya; now hear it in terms of yoga. Joined with this understanding, Partha, you will throw off the bondage of action.

  40. 2.40

    On this path no effort is lost and no reverse occurs. Even a little of this practice protects one from great fear.

  41. 2.41

    Here, joy of the Kurus, the resolute understanding is single; but the thoughts of the irresolute are many-branched and endless.

  42. 2.42

    Partha, the unwise utter flowery words; delighting in the letter of the Vedas, they declare there is nothing else.

  43. 2.43

    Full of desire and intent on heaven, they speak of many special rites that promise rebirth as the fruit of action and aim at enjoyment and power.

  44. 2.44

    Those attached to enjoyment and power, whose minds are carried away by such talk, do not develop the resolute understanding that rests in meditation.

  45. 2.45

    The Vedas deal with the three gunas; Arjuna, rise above the three gunas. Be free of the pairs of opposites, always rooted in purity, unconcerned with getting and keeping, and centred in the self.

  46. 2.46

    As much use as a small well has when water floods everywhere, so much use have all the Vedas for a brahmin who truly knows.

  47. 2.47

    Your right is to the action alone, never to its fruits. Do not let the fruit be your motive, and do not cling to inaction.

  48. 2.48

    Established in yoga, do your work, Dhananjaya, giving up attachment and staying even-minded in success and failure. This evenness is called yoga.

  49. 2.49

    Action done for its result is far inferior to the yoga of understanding, Dhananjaya. Seek refuge in that understanding; pitiable are those whose motive is the fruit.

  50. 2.50

    One joined with this understanding leaves behind both good and bad deeds here. So devote yourself to yoga; yoga is skill in action.

  51. 2.51

    The wise, joined with this understanding, give up the fruit born of action, and, freed from the bondage of birth, reach the state beyond all suffering.

  52. 2.52

    When your understanding crosses beyond the thicket of delusion, you will become indifferent to what you have heard and what is yet to be heard.

  53. 2.53

    When your understanding, now unsettled by conflicting teachings, stands firm and unmoving in meditative absorption, then you will attain yoga.

  54. 2.54

    Keshava, how is one of steady wisdom, settled in meditation, described? How does a person of steady mind speak, how does he sit, how does he move?

  55. 2.55

    Partha, when a person gives up every desire that arises in the mind and is content in the self, by the self alone, then he is called one of steady wisdom.

  56. 2.56

    One whose mind is not shaken in sorrow, who does not crave pleasures, who is free from passion, fear and anger — such a sage is called steady of mind.

  57. 2.57

    One who is unattached everywhere, who neither rejoices on meeting good nor resents meeting bad — his wisdom is firmly established.

  58. 2.58

    When he draws his senses back from their objects on every side, as a tortoise draws in its limbs, his wisdom is firmly established.

  59. 2.59

    Sense objects fall away from one who abstains from them, but the taste for them remains; even that taste fades once the Supreme is seen.

  60. 2.60

    For the turbulent senses, son of Kunti, forcibly carry off the mind even of a wise person who is striving.

  61. 2.61

    Restraining them all, one should sit disciplined, intent on me. One whose senses are under control has firm wisdom.

  62. 2.62

    When a person dwells on sense objects, attachment to them arises; from attachment comes desire, and from desire comes anger.

  63. 2.63

    Anger breeds delusion, and delusion confuses memory; when memory fails, understanding is ruined, and when understanding is ruined, a person is lost.

  64. 2.64

    But the self-controlled person, moving among sense objects with senses free from attraction and aversion and under the control of the self, attains inner calm.

  65. 2.65

    In that calm all his sorrows come to an end, for the understanding of one whose mind is serene soon becomes firmly settled.

  66. 2.66

    The undisciplined have no understanding, and no power of contemplation; without contemplation there is no peace, and without peace how can there be happiness?

  67. 2.67

    When the mind follows the roaming senses, it carries away one's wisdom, as wind carries off a boat on the water.

  68. 2.68

    Therefore, mighty-armed one, one whose senses are fully held back from their objects has firmly established wisdom.

  69. 2.69

    What is night for all beings, the self-controlled one is awake to; and that in which beings are awake is night for the sage who sees.

  70. 2.70

    As waters flow into the ocean, which is ever being filled yet stays steady within its bounds, so one into whom all desires enter in the same way attains peace — not one who chases desires.

  71. 2.71

    The person who gives up all desires and moves about free from longing, without any sense of 'mine' and without ego, attains peace.

  72. 2.72

    This is the state of Brahman, Partha; having reached it, one is no longer deluded. Remaining in it even at the hour of death, one attains the peace of Brahman.
