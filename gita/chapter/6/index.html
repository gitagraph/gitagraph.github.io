Chapter 6 · 47 verses

Meditation

ध्यानयोग

The practice of dhyana: a quiet seat, a steady posture, the mind held on the Self. Lift yourself by yourself; the mind is your friend or your enemy. Arjuna says the mind is as hard to hold as the wind; Krishna answers: by practice and detachment.

ध्यान का अभ्यास: शांत आसन, स्थिर मुद्रा, आत्मा में टिका मन। अपने द्वारा अपना उद्धार करो; मन ही मित्र है और मन ही शत्रु। अर्जुन कहते हैं कि मन को वश में करना वायु को रोकने जैसा कठिन है; कृष्ण उत्तर देते हैं: अभ्यास और वैराग्य से।

  1. 6.1

    One who does the work that ought to be done without leaning on its fruits is both a renunciant and a yogi; not the one who has merely put out the sacred fire, nor the one who simply stops acting.

  2. 6.2

    Know, Pandava, that what people call renunciation is in fact yoga, for no one becomes a yogi without first giving up self-willed intentions.

  3. 6.3

    For the sage who wants to climb to yoga, action is said to be the means; for that same person once established in yoga, calm is said to be the means.

  4. 6.4

    When a person no longer clings to the objects of the senses or to actions, and has let go of every self-willed intention, then he is called one who has mounted to yoga.

  5. 6.5

    Let a person raise himself by his own self and not let himself sink, for the self alone is one's friend, and the self alone is one's enemy.

  6. 6.6

    The self is a friend to the one who has conquered himself by himself; but for one who has not mastered himself, that very self behaves with hostility, like an enemy.

  7. 6.7

    For one who has mastered himself and is deeply at peace, the supreme Self stays steady and collected amid cold and heat, pleasure and pain, honour and dishonour alike.

  8. 6.8

    The yogi whose heart is content with knowledge and its lived realisation, who stands unshaken like an anvil, who has conquered the senses, and to whom a lump of earth, a stone and gold are all the same, is called truly joined in yoga.

  9. 6.9

    Still higher stands the one who looks with an even mind on well-wishers, friends and foes, the indifferent and the neutral, the hateful and kinsmen, and on the good and the wicked alike.

  10. 6.10

    A yogi should continually bring his mind to stillness, staying in a secluded place, alone, with mind and body restrained, free of cravings and without possessions.

  11. 6.11

    In a clean place he should set up a firm seat for himself, neither too high nor too low, covered with kusha grass, then a deerskin, then a cloth.

  12. 6.12

    Seated there, making the mind one-pointed and keeping the workings of thought and senses in check, he should practise yoga to purify himself.

  13. 6.13

    Holding body, head and neck straight and motionless, staying steady, he should gaze at the tip of his own nose without looking about in any direction.

  14. 6.14

    Calm within, free of fear, firm in the vow of celibacy, restraining the mind and fixing his thought on me, he should sit joined in yoga, with me as his highest goal.

  15. 6.15

    Constantly uniting himself in this way, with his mind under control, the yogi reaches the peace that rests in me and culminates in nirvana.

  16. 6.16

    Yoga is not for one who eats too much, nor for one who does not eat at all, Arjuna; nor for one who sleeps too much, nor for one who stays awake too long.

  17. 6.17

    For one who is measured in eating and recreation, measured in effort at work, and measured in sleep and waking, yoga becomes the remover of sorrow.

  18. 6.18

    When the well-disciplined mind settles in the Self alone, free of longing for any object of desire, then a person is called united in yoga.

  19. 6.19

    "Like a lamp in a windless place that does not flicker" — that is the image used for the yogi of controlled mind who practises union with the Self.

  20. 6.20

    That state where the mind, held in check by the practice of yoga, comes to rest, and where, seeing the Self through the self, one is content in the Self...

  21. 6.21

    ...where one knows that boundless happiness which the intellect can grasp but the senses cannot reach, and, established there, never strays from the truth...

  22. 6.22

    ...having gained which, one counts no other gain as greater, and settled in which, one is not shaken even by heavy sorrow...

  23. 6.23

    ...know that this is what is called yoga: the unlinking from all contact with sorrow. It should be practised with resolve and an undiscouraged heart.

  24. 6.24

    Giving up entirely all desires that spring from mental intention, and reining in the whole crowd of the senses from every side by the mind alone...

  25. 6.25

    ...one should gradually come to rest, with the intellect held firm by steadiness; fixing the mind in the Self, one should think of nothing else at all.

  26. 6.26

    Whenever the restless, unsteady mind wanders off, from there one should draw it back and bring it under control in the Self alone.

  27. 6.27

    Supreme happiness comes to this yogi whose mind is fully calm, whose restless passion has subsided, who is free of taint and has become one with Brahman.

  28. 6.28

    Constantly joining himself in this way, the yogi, freed of impurity, easily enjoys the boundless happiness of contact with Brahman.

  29. 6.29

    One whose self is joined in yoga sees the Self present in all beings and all beings in the Self, seeing the same everywhere.

  30. 6.30

    Whoever sees me everywhere and sees everything in me — I am never lost to that person, and that person is never lost to me.

  31. 6.31

    The yogi who, grounded in oneness, worships me as dwelling in all beings, lives in me, however he may be living.

  32. 6.32

    Arjuna, the one who, by comparing others to himself, sees pleasure or pain everywhere as the same is regarded as the highest yogi.

  33. 6.33

    Madhusudana, this yoga of evenness that you have taught — I do not see how it can stay firmly in place, because the mind is so restless.

  34. 6.34

    For the mind is restless, Krishna — turbulent, strong and stubborn. Restraining it seems to me as hard as restraining the wind.

  35. 6.35

    No doubt, mighty-armed one, the mind is restless and hard to hold. But, son of Kunti, it is brought under control through steady practice and dispassion.

  36. 6.36

    My view is that yoga is hard to attain for one who has not mastered himself; but one who has mastered himself and keeps striving can reach it by the right means.

  37. 6.37

    Krishna, what becomes of the person who has faith but lacks self-control, whose mind slips away from yoga, and who fails to reach its perfection?

  38. 6.38

    Mighty-armed one, having fallen from both, without a footing and bewildered on the path to Brahman, does he not perish like a scattered cloud?

  39. 6.39

    Krishna, please remove this doubt of mine completely; no one but you can dispel it.

  40. 6.40

    Partha, there is no ruin for him either in this world or the next; for, my friend, no one who does good ever comes to a bad end.

  41. 6.41

    One who has fallen from yoga reaches the worlds of the virtuous, lives there for many long years, and is then born into the home of pure and prosperous people.

  42. 6.42

    Or he is born into a family of wise yogis itself; but such a birth is very hard to come by in this world.

  43. 6.43

    There he regains the understanding he had developed in his former body, and from that point, joy of the Kurus, he strives again, even harder, for perfection.

  44. 6.44

    By that earlier practice he is carried forward even without willing it; even one who merely seeks to know about yoga goes beyond the ritual word of the Vedas.

  45. 6.45

    But the yogi who strives with diligence, cleansed of faults and brought to perfection over many births, then reaches the highest goal.

  46. 6.46

    The yogi is greater than ascetics, considered greater even than scholars, and greater than those devoted to ritual works; therefore, Arjuna, be a yogi.

  47. 6.47

    And of all yogis, the one who, full of faith, worships me with his innermost self absorbed in me — that one I regard as the most closely united.
