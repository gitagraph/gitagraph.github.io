Two friends

Mitra Bhed: part 3 of 8

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

Damanaka hurried back to the bull and spoke roughly. “Come here, you wicked bull. My master Pingalaka sends for you. What do you mean by bellowing over and over, afraid of nothing?” “Friend, who is Pingalaka?” “You do not know my master Pingalaka? Wait a little and you will find out. He is the lion who sits under that banyan with every animal of the forest around him.” Sanjivaka thought his life was over. “Friend,” he said, “you seem a decent sort, and you speak well. If you must take me to him, get me his promise of safety first.” Damanaka agreed and told him to wait.

To the lion he told a different tale. “Master, that is no common creature. He is the bull that carries the great god Shiva. He told me the god was pleased with him and sent him to graze beside the Yamuna, and gave him this forest to play in.” Pingalaka was afraid. “Now I understand. No eater of grass wanders bellowing through a forest like this one without a god behind him. What did you say to him?”

“I told him this forest belongs to Pingalaka, who carries the goddess Chandika. I said he had come as a welcome guest and should live with you as a brother, eating, drinking and walking in one place. He agreed to all of it, and asked only for my master's promise of safety.” “Well done,” said Pingalaka. “You spoke as if you had asked my own heart first. He has my promise. Get his promise of safety for me as well, and bring him quickly.” Damanaka went off pleased. His master was now ruled by his word.

He spoke gently to Sanjivaka this time. “Friend, I have won you the king's promise. Come without fear. Only remember our understanding once you have his favour. Do not grow proud and go your own way. I will carry the work of the kingdom as minister, with you beside me, and we will both do well. And honour everyone who stands near the king, high or low. Dantila was a great man, and he fell for failing in that.” “How was that?” asked Sanjivaka.

“It is so,” said Sanjivaka. “I will do just as you say.” Damanaka led him to the lion. Sanjivaka bowed and stood quietly before him. Pingalaka laid his clawed right paw on the bull's great humped shoulder and spoke kindly. “Are you well? How did you come to this empty forest?” Sanjivaka told him everything, and how he had been parted from Vardhamana. “Friend, do not be afraid,” said the lion. “You are safe within my arms. Live as you please, and stay close to me always. This forest is full of dangers and fierce creatures. Even large animals are not safe in it, let alone those who eat grass.”

Then Pingalaka went down to the Yamuna with all his animals, drank, and walked back into the forest. He left the work of the kingdom to Karataka and Damanaka and spent his days in talk with Sanjivaka. The company of a good person, even when it comes by chance and only once, lasts and does not grow old.

Sanjivaka had studied many branches of learning and his mind was sharp. In a few days he made the simple lion thoughtful. He drew him away from the ways of the forest and taught him the ways of the village. Every day the two of them talked alone. The other animals were kept at a distance. Even Karataka and Damanaka were not let in. And the lion no longer hunted, so the animals and the two jackals went hungry and fell ill. Servants leave a king who brings them nothing, however noble he is, the way birds leave a dry tree.

To the lion he said that this was the bull of the god Shiva, sent to graze by the Yamuna. To the bull he said that the lion carried the goddess. Each was glad to be given the other's promise of safety.

So Damanaka brought the bull before the lion. The lion welcomed him and told him to live close by, safe within his arms.

The lion and the bull became close friends and talked alone every day. The bull ate grass, and under his influence the lion gave up the ways of the forest and stopped hunting. The court went hungry, and so did the two jackals.
