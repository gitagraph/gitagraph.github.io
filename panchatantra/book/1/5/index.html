The lie to the lion

Mitra Bhed: part 5 of 8

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

Damanaka waited until Pingalaka was sitting apart from Sanjivaka. Then he went up, bowed and sat down. “Friend, why have I not seen you for so long?” asked the lion.

“My master has no use for us, so I stay away,” said Damanaka. “But I see the king's affairs going to ruin and my heart burns, so I have come on my own. Pleasant or unpleasant, a person should say what is good for someone he does not want to see fall, even unasked.”

Sanskrit verse 1.262

“Say what you have to say,” said Pingalaka. “Master, Sanjivaka means you harm. He trusts me, and he told me this in private: ‘Damanaka, I have seen where this Pingalaka is strong and where he is weak. I will kill him and rule over all the animals myself, and you will be my minister.’”

The words fell on Pingalaka like a thunderbolt. He was stunned and said nothing. Damanaka watched his face and thought, “He is bound to Sanjivaka by love.”

At last the lion found his voice. “Sanjivaka is a servant as dear to me as my life. How could he mean me harm?” “Master, a servant does not always stay a servant,” said Damanaka. “There is no one near a king who does not want the king's fortune. Those who wait on him are only the ones who cannot take it.”

Sanskrit verse 1.264

“Even so,” said Pingalaka, “my heart does not turn against him. Everyone loves his own body, whatever its faults. One who is dear stays dear, even when he does wrong.”

Sanskrit verse 1.265

“That is just the trouble,” said Damanaka. “And he is of no use to you. He is big, but he eats grass, and your enemies eat meat. He cannot help you against them. Find fault with him and kill him.”

Pingalaka would not. “I praised him before the whole court. I cannot now speak ill of him. I gave him my promise of safety, and I gave it on your word. He is my friend and I feel no anger toward him. Even a poison tree should not be cut down by the one who raised it.”

Sanskrit verses 1.267, 1.268

Damanaka pressed harder. “It is no part of a king's duty to forgive one who plots against him. And for this friendship you have let all your duty go. Your people have turned from you. Sanjivaka eats grass. You and your people eat meat, and you no longer hunt, so they have none. They will leave you, and then you are lost.

A person becomes like those who serve him and those he keeps beside him. A drop of water on hot iron vanishes without a trace. The same drop on a lotus leaf shines like a pearl. Inside a sea shell, under the star Svati, it becomes one.

Sanskrit verses 1.272, 1.273

Good people stay clear of the wrong company. No one should give shelter to a person whose nature he does not know. A louse once died for the fault of a bug.” “How was that?” asked Pingalaka.

“So you must kill him,” said Damanaka, “or he will kill you. Whoever leaves his own people and takes in outsiders comes to the end that King Kakuddruma came to.” “How was that?” asked Pingalaka.

Pingalaka asked, “What proof is there that he means me harm?” “He swore it in front of me today. ‘In the morning I will kill Pingalaka.’ You will have your proof in the morning. At the hour of your court he will come with his face and eyes red and his lip trembling. He will look about him, sit down where he should not, and stare at you cruelly. When you see that, do what is fitting.”

Damanaka went to the lion when he was alone. He said the bull had told him in secret that he meant to kill the lion and rule in his place. The lion was stunned.

The lion would not turn on a friend to whom he had promised safety. Damanaka pressed him: a king who shuts out his own people for a stranger is lost. Then he told the lion what to watch for in the morning: a red face, and a bull who sat apart.
