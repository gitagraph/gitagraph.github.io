The ending

Mitra Bhed: part 8 of 8

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

While the two jackals were still talking, the fight ended. Sanjivaka had fought the lion for a short while. Then the sharp claws struck him down, and he fell dead on the ground.

Pingalaka looked at the body and remembered how good the bull had been. His heart softened. “I have done a wicked thing in killing Sanjivaka,” he said. “Nothing is worse than betraying one who trusted you. Lost land can be won again. A good servant, once lost, cannot. And I praised him before my whole court. I do not know what I will say to them now.”

Sanskrit verses 1.454, 1.455, 1.456

While he was grieving, Damanaka came up to him, pleased. “Master, this is a weak way to behave. He ate grass and he plotted against you, and you killed him. Now you mourn him. That does not suit a king. Whoever plots against your life must die, be it father, brother, son, wife or friend. A king's way has many faces, true and false, harsh and sweet, cruel and kind. The wise do not grieve for the dead or for the living.”

Sanskrit verses 1.457, 1.459, 1.461

Pingalaka listened. He put away his grief for Sanjivaka, and he ruled the forest from then on with Damanaka as his minister. Here ends the first book, Losing Friends.

What this story shows

The friendship ended because each friend listened to the jackal and neither spoke to the other.

The lion and the bull never quarrelled. Damanaka went to each of them alone and told each that the other meant to kill him. Then he told each what to watch for. The bull, afraid, sat apart without bowing. The lion, afraid, glared. Each saw in the other exactly the sign he had been given, and took it as proof. Neither asked the other a single question.

The book also shows why it worked. Pingalaka had shut out his whole court for one friend, and those left out were hungry and bitter. Damanaka's envy grew in that gap. Karataka saw what was happening and said so, plainly and at length, and it changed nothing, because he spoke only to the one person who would not listen. The story ends as the text ends it. The bull is dead, the lion is talked out of his grief, and the jackal who caused it is made minister.

  • A friendship was lost without a quarrel. One spoken question between the lion and the bull would have ended the plot.
  • The one who carried the tale to both sides was the one who gained from it.
  • Fear made each friend act exactly as the liar had said he would.
