Losing friends

A lion, a bull and a jealous jackal

This is the first book, Losing Friends. It opens with one verse. A great friendship grew in the forest between a lion and a bull, and a greedy, slandering jackal destroyed it.

In the southern country there was a city called Mahilaropya. A merchant named Vardhamana lived there. He had earned a large fortune by honest means. One night he lay awake and thought that even a rich man should keep finding ways to earn. There is nothing, he thought, that money cannot bring about, so a sensible man works at it with care.

He weighed the ways a man can earn: begging, serving a king, farming, learning, lending money and trade. Trade seemed the best to him, and best of all was trading in goods from far countries. So he loaded a cart with goods for Mathura, took leave of his elders on a lucky day, and set out.

Two bulls born in his own house drew the cart. Their names were Sanjivaka and Nandaka. On the bank of the Yamuna, Sanjivaka stepped into deep mud. His leg was caught, the yoke broke, and he sank down.

Vardhamana was very fond of the bull. He stopped the journey for three nights. Then the men of the caravan spoke to him. The forest was full of lions and tigers, they said, and he was putting the whole caravan in danger for one bull. A sensible man does not lose much for the sake of a little.

Vardhamana saw the sense in this. He left guards with Sanjivaka and went on with the caravan. The guards were afraid of the forest too. They left the bull where he lay and followed the caravan. The next day they told the merchant a lie. “Master, Sanjivaka died. He was dear to you, so we gave him a funeral fire.” The merchant was grateful and held the last rites for his bull.

Sanjivaka's time had not come. Cool winds blew off the water of the Yamuna and revived him. Somehow he got to his feet and reached the river bank. There he ate the tips of young grass, green as emerald, and in a few days he was fat and strong, with a great hump, like the bull of Shiva. Every day he tore at the anthills with his horns and bellowed. What fate protects stands even with no one to guard it, the text says here, and what fate strikes down is lost however well it is guarded.

One day a lion named Pingalaka came down to the Yamuna to drink, with all the animals of the forest around him. From far off he heard Sanjivaka's deep bellow. His heart shook. He hid his fear, turned back without drinking, and sat down under a banyan tree with his court drawn up around him in four circles.

Two jackals always followed him. Their names were Karataka and Damanaka. They were sons of ministers and had lost their posts. Damanaka said, “Friend Karataka, our master came to the river thirsty. Now he has turned back, set his court in order around him, and sits under the banyan looking troubled. Why?” Karataka said, “That is no concern of ours. Whoever meddles in what is not his business comes to grief, like the monkey who pulled out the wedge.”

“We have the food he leaves,” Karataka went on. “That is enough. There is no reason to get mixed up in this.” Damanaka did not agree. “Is food all you want? Anyone can fill his own belly. Birds do it with their beaks. He truly lives on whom many others live. And a person who never shows what he can do is passed over. People step across fire while it sleeps inside the wood. Nobody steps across it once it burns.”

Karataka was still careful. “We hold no post. A fool who speaks to the king unasked earns disrespect, and mockery on top of it.” “Do not say that,” said Damanaka. “A person with no rank gains it by serving the king, and one with rank loses it by staying away. A king favours whoever is near him, learned or not, well born or not. Kings are like creepers. They wind themselves around whatever stands beside them.”

“What do you mean to do?” Karataka asked. “Our master is frightened today, and so is his whole court,” said Damanaka. “I mean to go to him, find out why, and put it right.” “How do you know he is frightened?” “That is not hard. Even an animal understands what it is told. A wise person works out what is left unsaid. A face, a walk and a look in the eye all show the mind inside. I will take away his fear with my wits, win him over, and have my minister's place back.”

Karataka warned him that kings are hard to please. They are like mountains, he said, rough and hard, and full of dangerous creatures. Damanaka knew it. “A clever person enters into the mood of whoever he is with, and soon has him in hand. And I will not speak out of turn.” “Then go,” said Karataka, “and may your road be a good one.” Damanaka bowed to him and set off toward the lion.

Pingalaka saw him coming and spoke to the doorkeeper. “Put the staff aside. This is Damanaka, the son of our old minister. He may come in freely.” Damanaka came up, bowed, and sat where he was shown. The lion laid his right paw on him, a paw armed with claws like thunderbolts, and spoke kindly. “Are you well? It is a long time since I saw you.”

“Your Majesty has had no use for us,” said Damanaka. “Still, there is a time to speak. A king has use for everyone, high, middling and low. Great lords need even a blade of grass, to pick their teeth or scratch an ear. How much more a person who has speech and hands. We have served your house for generations and we have stayed with you in hard times, yet we have no post. A servant, like a jewel, belongs in the right place. Nobody ties a crest jewel to his foot.”

“And if my master thinks little of me because I am a jackal, that is not right either. Silk comes from a worm, gold from a stone, a lotus from mud, and the moon from the sea. People of worth shine by what they are. Birth has nothing to do with it. I am loyal, and I am able. You should not think little of me.” Pingalaka said, “Able or not, you are the son of our old minister. Say freely whatever you wish to say.”

Damanaka asked to speak alone. A plan heard by six ears does not stay secret, he said, and one heard by four ears holds. The tiger, the leopard, the wolf and the rest understood the lion's wish and moved away at once. Then Damanaka asked, “My master came to drink. Why did he turn back and sit down here?” Pingalaka gave an awkward smile. “It is nothing.” “If it cannot be told, let it be,” said Damanaka.

Pingalaka thought that this jackal seemed worth trusting. “Damanaka, do you hear that great sound in the distance?” “I hear it, master. What of it?” “I want to leave this forest. Some creature we have never seen has come into it. That is his voice. His strength must be as great as his sound.”

“It is not right for my master to fear a mere sound,” said Damanaka. “This forest has been yours a long time. Sounds are of many kinds. Drums, flutes, lutes, cymbals and conches all make them. A sound by itself is nothing to fear. A king whose courage holds when a fierce enemy arrives does not come to defeat. I once heard of someone who was sure a thing was full of fat. He went inside it and found skin and wood.” “How was that?” asked Pingalaka.

Pingalaka said, “Look at my court. They are all shaking and want to run. How can I stand firm?” “That is no fault of theirs,” said Damanaka. “Servants take after their master. Hold on to your courage and wait here. I will go and find out what this sound is. Then you can do whatever is fitting.” “You dare to go there?” “When my master orders it, a good servant does not stop to ask what can and cannot be done.” “Then go,” said Pingalaka, “and may your road be a good one.” Damanaka bowed and went off toward the sound.

Once he had gone, the lion's fear turned on Damanaka. “I should not have trusted him with what was in my heart. He has lost his post. He may take pay from both sides and turn against me. He may even bring that creature here to kill me. The weak stay alive when they trust no one, and the strong are brought down when they do.” So he went to another spot and waited there alone, watching the path.

Damanaka reached Sanjivaka and saw that he was only a bull. He was delighted. “This has turned out well,” he thought. “I can make peace or trouble between these two as I choose, and Pingalaka will be in my hands. A healthy man has no wish for a doctor, and a king with no troubles has no wish for a minister.”

He went back. Pingalaka saw him coming, composed his face, and sat as he had before. “Did you see the creature?” “I saw him, by my master's favour.” “Truly?” “Nobody tells a lie in front of a king.” “Then you did see him,” said the lion. “The great are not angry with the small. That is why he did not strike you down. A storm does not tear up the grass that bends low before it. The mighty show their might against the mighty.”

“So be it,” said Damanaka. “He is great and we are small. Even so, if my master says the word, I will bring him into your service.” The lion let out a breath. “Can you do that?” “There is nothing that wit cannot manage. Weapons, elephants, horses and soldiers do not finish a task the way wit does.” “If you can,” said Pingalaka, “you are my minister from today. Reward and punishment are in your hands.”

Damanaka hurried back to the bull and spoke roughly. “Come here, you wicked bull. My master Pingalaka sends for you. What do you mean by bellowing over and over, afraid of nothing?” “Friend, who is Pingalaka?” “You do not know my master Pingalaka? Wait a little and you will find out. He is the lion who sits under that banyan with every animal of the forest around him.” Sanjivaka thought his life was over. “Friend,” he said, “you seem a decent sort, and you speak well. If you must take me to him, get me his promise of safety first.” Damanaka agreed and told him to wait.

To the lion he told a different tale. “Master, that is no common creature. He is the bull that carries the great god Shiva. He told me the god was pleased with him and sent him to graze beside the Yamuna, and gave him this forest to play in.” Pingalaka was afraid. “Now I understand. No eater of grass wanders bellowing through a forest like this one without a god behind him. What did you say to him?”

“I told him this forest belongs to Pingalaka, who carries the goddess Chandika. I said he had come as a welcome guest and should live with you as a brother, eating, drinking and walking in one place. He agreed to all of it, and asked only for my master's promise of safety.” “Well done,” said Pingalaka. “You spoke as if you had asked my own heart first. He has my promise. Get his promise of safety for me as well, and bring him quickly.” Damanaka went off pleased. His master was now ruled by his word.

He spoke gently to Sanjivaka this time. “Friend, I have won you the king's promise. Come without fear. Only remember our understanding once you have his favour. Do not grow proud and go your own way. I will carry the work of the kingdom as minister, with you beside me, and we will both do well.” Sanjivaka said, “I will do just as you say.”

Damanaka led him to the lion. Sanjivaka bowed and stood quietly before him. Pingalaka laid his clawed right paw on the bull's great humped shoulder and spoke kindly. “Are you well? How did you come to this empty forest?” Sanjivaka told him everything, and how he had been parted from Vardhamana. “Friend, do not be afraid,” said the lion. “You are safe within my arms. Live as you please, and stay close to me always. This forest is full of dangers and fierce creatures. Even large animals are not safe in it, let alone those who eat grass.”

Then Pingalaka went down to the Yamuna with all his animals, drank, and walked back into the forest. He left the work of the kingdom to Karataka and Damanaka and spent his days in talk with Sanjivaka. The company of a good person, even when it comes by chance and only once, lasts and does not grow old.

Sanjivaka had studied many branches of learning and his mind was sharp. In a few days he made the simple lion thoughtful. He drew him away from the ways of the forest and taught him the ways of the village. Every day the two of them talked alone. The other animals were kept at a distance. Even Karataka and Damanaka were not let in. And the lion no longer hunted, so the animals and the two jackals went hungry and fell ill. Servants leave a king who brings them nothing, however noble he is, the way birds leave a dry tree.

The two jackals, thin with hunger, talked it over. “Karataka,” said Damanaka, “we count for nothing now. Pingalaka is so taken with Sanjivaka that he has turned from his own work. The whole court has drifted away. What is to be done?” Karataka said, “A minister should tell the king his fault even when the king does not listen. And it was you who brought this eater of grass to our master. You raked out the hot coals with your own hand.” “That is true,” said Damanaka. “The fault is mine.”

“Then what should we do?” asked Karataka. “My wits will not fail me even now,” said Damanaka. “I will part Sanjivaka from our master. An arrow from an archer may kill one person, or none. A thought let fly by a clever mind can reach even the unborn. I will do it in secret, by a trick, so that neither of them knows.” “If either of them finds out,” said Karataka, “that is the end of you.” “Do not say so. Effort should not be given up even when luck has turned.”

Karataka was still afraid. “Sanjivaka is intelligent and the lion is fierce. Your wits are bold, but you cannot part those two.” “Brother,” said Damanaka, “what looks beyond me is within my reach. What force cannot do, a plan can. A pair of crows once had a black snake killed with a gold chain.” “How was that?” asked Karataka.

“If you agree,” said Damanaka, “I will go and break their friendship with my wits.” “Go, then,” said Karataka. “May your road be a good one. Do as you have decided.”

Damanaka waited until Pingalaka was sitting apart from Sanjivaka. Then he went up, bowed and sat down. “Friend, why have I not seen you for so long?” asked the lion. “My master has no use for us, so I stay away,” said Damanaka. “But I see the king's affairs going to ruin and my heart burns, so I have come on my own. Pleasant or unpleasant, a person should say what is good for someone he does not want to see fall, even unasked.”

“Say what you have to say,” said Pingalaka. “Master, Sanjivaka means you harm. He trusts me, and he told me this in private: ‘Damanaka, I have seen where this Pingalaka is strong and where he is weak. I will kill him and rule over all the animals myself, and you will be my minister.’” The words fell on Pingalaka like a thunderbolt. He was stunned and said nothing. Damanaka watched his face and thought, “He is bound to Sanjivaka by love.”

At last the lion found his voice. “Sanjivaka is a servant as dear to me as my life. How could he mean me harm?” “Master, a servant does not always stay a servant,” said Damanaka. “There is no one near a king who does not want the king's fortune. Those who wait on him are only the ones who cannot take it.” “Even so,” said Pingalaka, “my heart does not turn against him. Everyone loves his own body, whatever its faults. One who is dear stays dear, even when he does wrong.”

“That is just the trouble,” said Damanaka. “And he is of no use to you. He is big, but he eats grass, and your enemies eat meat. He cannot help you against them. Find fault with him and kill him.” Pingalaka would not. “I praised him before the whole court. I cannot now speak ill of him. I gave him my promise of safety, and I gave it on your word. He is my friend and I feel no anger toward him. Even a poison tree should not be cut down by the one who raised it.”

Damanaka pressed harder. “It is no part of a king's duty to forgive one who plots against him. And for this friendship you have let all your duty go. Your people have turned from you. Sanjivaka eats grass. You and your people eat meat, and you no longer hunt, so they have none. They will leave you, and then you are lost. A person becomes like those who serve him and those he keeps beside him. A drop of water on hot iron vanishes without a trace. The same drop on a lotus leaf shines like a pearl. Inside a sea shell, under the star Svati, it becomes one. Good people stay clear of the wrong company. No one should give shelter to a person whose nature he does not know. A louse once died for the fault of a bug.” “How was that?” asked Pingalaka.

Pingalaka asked, “What proof is there that he means me harm?” “He swore it in front of me today. ‘In the morning I will kill Pingalaka.’ You will have your proof in the morning. At the hour of your court he will come with his face and eyes red and his lip trembling. He will look about him, sit down where he should not, and stare at you cruelly. When you see that, do what is fitting.”

From the lion he went straight to Sanjivaka, walking slowly, with a worried face. The bull greeted him warmly. “Welcome, friend. It is long since I saw you. Are you well? Tell me what brings you. A guest in my house may ask for anything.” “A servant is never well,” said Damanaka. “His fortune hangs on someone else. His mind is never at rest. Those who serve kings cannot be sure even of their own lives.”

“What is it you have come to say?” asked Sanjivaka. “A minister should never give away his master's counsel,” said Damanaka. “I am doing it all the same, because I am fond of you, and because you came into this court on my word. Pingalaka has turned against you. He told me today, the two of us alone, ‘In the morning I will kill Sanjivaka and give all my animals a full meal at last.’”

“I told him it is wrong to live by betraying a friend. He grew angry. ‘Sanjivaka eats grass and we eat meat,’ he said. ‘We are enemies by nature. There is no fault in killing him.’ So I came to you. Nobody can say I broke faith with you. I have told you a closely kept secret. Do whatever seems best to you.”

The words fell on Sanjivaka like a thunderbolt. He was stunned. When he came to himself he spoke bitterly. “It is well said that a king has little affection. A fool who thinks he is the king's favourite is a bull without horns. I should never have made this friendship. Friendship and marriage belong between those equal in wealth and in family. Deer go with deer, cattle with cattle, and horses with horses.”

“If I go and try to win him back, it will not work. Anger that has a cause passes when the cause is gone. One who hates without a cause cannot be satisfied. I see what has happened. Those near him could not bear the favour he showed me, and they have turned him against me. Servants cannot bear to see their master's favour go to another. And where someone more gifted stands close, lesser gifts go unseen. A lamp glows at night. Nobody notices it once the sun is up.”

“Then you have nothing to fear,” said Damanaka. “Wicked people may have angered him, but you speak well, and you will win him round.” “That is not so,” said Sanjivaka. “Nobody can live among wicked people, even small ones. They find some way to kill. Small, clever creatures who live by tricks can do anything, as the crow and the others did to the camel.” “How was that?” asked Damanaka.

“Friend, your king is surrounded by small creatures like these,” Sanjivaka went on. “Good people cannot serve such a court. Some wicked person has turned him against me. It happens. Soft water wears away even the ground of a mountain. A whisper in the ear wears away a soft mind much sooner. A snake kills only the one it bites. A slanderer touches one person's ear and destroys another, root and all. Even so, I ask you as a friend. What should I do?”

“Go to another country,” said Damanaka. “A master like this should not be served.” “I cannot go while he is angry with me,” said Sanjivaka, “and I would find no peace elsewhere. Someone who has offended the great should not feel safe because he is far away. Their arms are long. Nothing is left for me except to fight. One who dies in battle wins heaven, and one who lives wins fame.”

Damanaka did not like this. “He has made up his mind to fight,” he thought. “If those sharp horns strike my master, great harm will come of it. I must talk him into leaving the country.” Aloud he said, “Well spoken, friend. But a servant has no business fighting his master. And whoever starts a feud without knowing the other's strength comes off worse, as the sea did against the sandpiper.” “How was that?” asked Sanjivaka.

Sanjivaka asked him once more. “Friend, how am I to know that he means me harm? All this time he has looked on me with more and more kindness. I have never seen him change.” “That is easy,” said Damanaka. “Here is your sign. If his eyes are red when he sees you, if he frowns and licks the corners of his mouth, he means you harm. If he does none of that, he is well disposed. Now let me go home. See that nobody learns what I have told you. And if you can get away at nightfall, leave this country. A person should give up the whole earth to save himself.”

Then Damanaka went to Karataka. “What have you done there?” asked Karataka. “I have sown the seed,” said Damanaka. “The rest lies with fate. I have set them against each other with lies. You will not see those two sitting and talking together again.”

Karataka was appalled. “You have done wrong. Their hearts were warm with love and they lived at ease, and you have thrown them into a sea of anger. Whoever pushes a harmless, happy creature onto the road of sorrow will know sorrow himself, life after life. And you are pleased with it. Anyone can do harm. The wind has the strength to knock a tree down. It cannot set one upright.”

“You do not understand statecraft,” said Damanaka. “An enemy, like an illness, must be put down the moment he appears, or he grows and kills. He took the minister's place, and that made him our enemy. I brought him in without a thought, with a promise of safety, and he pushed me out. So I have arranged his death, or at least his leaving. Nobody but you will know. And once he is dead we will eat him. The feud is settled, the minister's place is mine, and we are fed. Three gains at once. I do not see what you blame me for.”

When Damanaka had left him, Sanjivaka stood thinking. “What have I done? I eat grass, and I made myself the follower of one who eats meat. Where can I go now? Perhaps I should go to Pingalaka himself. I would come to him for shelter, and he may spare my life. And if I go elsewhere, some other eater of meat will kill me. Better to die by the lion. When someone measures himself against the great, even his fall has honour in it.”

So he went, slowly, with unsteady steps. As the lion's place came into view he said a verse to himself. A king's mind, with small and spiteful creatures around it, is like a house with a snake hidden inside, or a lovely lotus pool full of crocodiles. One enters it in fear.

Then he saw Pingalaka, and the lion looked just as Damanaka had said he would. Sanjivaka was startled. He drew himself in and sat down far off, without bowing. Pingalaka saw the bull behaving just as Damanaka had said he would. He believed the jackal now, and in his anger he sprang.

The lion's claws tore Sanjivaka's back. Sanjivaka gored the lion's belly with his horns, broke free, and stood ready to fight. Both were red with blood, like two flame trees in flower, and each meant to kill the other.

Karataka saw them and turned on Damanaka. “Fool, you have done wrong in setting these two against each other. You know nothing of statecraft. A minister who knows his work settles the hardest matters gently, by friendly words. If our master is hurt, your cleverness is worth nothing. If Sanjivaka is not killed, that is bad too, for he can only be killed at the risk of our master's life. And you want to be a minister. Force is the last of the means, to be used when the others have failed. If sugar calms the bile, nobody needs the bitter gourd.”

“Now harm has come to our master, or to his minister, or to both. A minister's wit is tested in mending what is broken. If you have any, find the remedy. You cannot. A mouse can knock the food basket down. It cannot lift it back up. Or perhaps the fault is our master's, for he believed you. Kings who follow low creatures and leave the path the wise point out walk into a cage of troubles with no easy way out. With you as his minister no good person will come near him, and without good people he will be lost. But there is no use in advising a fool. Wood that will not bend cannot be bent. Remember the bird Suchimukha.” “How was that?” asked Damanaka.

While the two jackals were still talking, the fight ended. Sanjivaka had fought the lion for a short while. Then the sharp claws struck him down, and he fell dead on the ground.

Pingalaka looked at the body and remembered how good the bull had been. His heart softened. “I have done a wicked thing in killing Sanjivaka,” he said. “Nothing is worse than betraying one who trusted you. Lost land can be won again. A good servant, once lost, cannot. And I praised him before my whole court. I do not know what I will say to them now.”

While he was grieving, Damanaka came up to him, pleased. “Master, this is a weak way to behave. He ate grass and he plotted against you, and you killed him. Now you mourn him. That does not suit a king. Whoever plots against your life must die, be it father, brother, son, wife or friend. A king's way has many faces, true and false, harsh and sweet, cruel and kind. The wise do not grieve for the dead or for the living.”

Pingalaka listened. He put away his grief for Sanjivaka, and he ruled the forest from then on with Damanaka as his minister. Here ends the first book, Losing Friends.

What this story shows

The friendship ended because each friend listened to the jackal and neither spoke to the other.

The lion and the bull never quarrelled. Damanaka went to each of them alone and told each that the other meant to kill him. Then he told each what to watch for. The bull, afraid, sat apart without bowing. The lion, afraid, glared. Each saw in the other exactly the sign he had been given, and took it as proof. Neither asked the other a single question.

The book also shows why it worked. Pingalaka had shut out his whole court for one friend, and those left out were hungry and bitter. Damanaka's envy grew in that gap. Karataka saw what was happening and said so, plainly and at length, and it changed nothing, because he spoke only to the one person who would not listen. The story ends as the text ends it. The bull is dead, the lion is talked out of his grief, and the jackal who caused it is made minister.

  • Whoever carries a tale against a friend usually stands to gain from it.
  • Frightened people behave in ways that look like guilt, and a liar can count on that.
  • A ruler who gives all his time to one favourite leaves the rest hungry, and hungry courtiers look for a way back in.

A merchant's bull, Sanjivaka, sank in the mud by the Yamuna. The caravan could not wait in a forest full of lions, so it went on. The guards left with him slipped away and said he had died.

Sanjivaka recovered, grew strong on the river grass, and bellowed. The lion king Pingalaka came to drink, heard the sound, and was afraid. He turned back without drinking and sat under a banyan with his court around him.

Two jackals watched him, Karataka and Damanaka, ministers' sons who had lost their posts. Damanaka saw a frightened king as his way back to a minister's place. Karataka warned him to stay out of what was not their business.

Damanaka went to the lion and learned that he feared the sound of some unknown creature. A sound by itself is nothing to fear, he said, and offered to go and look. The lion promised to make him minister if he could bring the creature into his service.

Damanaka found only a bull. He told the lion it was the bull of the god Shiva, and told the bull the lion carried the goddess. He won each a promise of safety from the other and brought them together.

The lion and the bull became close friends and talked alone every day. The lion stopped hunting, and nobody else was let in. The court went hungry, and so did the two jackals.

Damanaka had brought the bull in, and now the bull had his place. He decided to part the two friends by a trick, so that neither would know. Karataka was afraid of it and said so.

Damanaka went to the lion alone. He said the bull had told him in secret that he meant to kill the lion and rule. The lion would not believe it of a friend, so Damanaka told him what to watch for in the morning: a red face, and a bull who sat apart.

Then Damanaka went to the bull. He said the lion meant to kill him in the morning and feed the court. The bull believed that someone had poisoned the lion's mind, and saw nothing left for him except to fight.

Damanaka gave the bull a sign too: red eyes, a frown, a tongue licking the lips. Then he told Karataka the seed was sown. Karataka was appalled that two friends who loved each other had been thrown into a sea of anger.

The bull came in fear and sat far off without bowing. Each saw in the other the very sign he had been told to fear. The lion sprang, and they tore at each other.

While they fought, Karataka turned on Damanaka. A minister mends things gently, he said, and Damanaka had done this out of envy and put their own king's life in danger. He told him to keep away.

The bull fell dead under the lion's claws. The lion remembered how good his friend had been and grieved. Nothing is worse, he said, than betraying one who trusted you.

Damanaka came up pleased and told him that a king does not mourn a traitor. The lion put away his grief. He ruled on with Damanaka as his minister.

What this story shows

The friendship ended because each friend listened to the jackal and neither spoke to the other.

  • Whoever carries a tale against a friend usually stands to gain from it.
  • Frightened people behave in ways that look like guilt, and a liar can count on that.
  • A ruler who gives all his time to one favourite leaves the rest hungry, and hungry courtiers look for a way back in.
