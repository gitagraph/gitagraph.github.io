Left behind

Losing friends: part 1 of 8

This is the first book, Losing Friends. It opens with one verse. A great friendship grew in the forest between a lion and a bull, and a greedy, slandering jackal destroyed it.

In the southern country there was a city called Mahilaropya. A merchant named Vardhamana lived there. He had earned a large fortune by honest means. One night he lay awake and thought that even a rich man should keep finding ways to earn. There is nothing, he thought, that money cannot bring about, so a sensible man works at it with care.

He weighed the ways a man can earn: begging, serving a king, farming, learning, lending money and trade. Trade seemed the best to him, and best of all was trading in goods from far countries. So he loaded a cart with goods for Mathura, took leave of his elders on a lucky day, and set out.

Two bulls born in his own house drew the cart. Their names were Sanjivaka and Nandaka. On the bank of the Yamuna, Sanjivaka stepped into deep mud. His leg was caught, the yoke broke, and he sank down.

Vardhamana was very fond of the bull. He stopped the journey for three nights. Then the men of the caravan spoke to him. The forest was full of lions and tigers, they said, and he was putting the whole caravan in danger for one bull. A sensible man does not lose much for the sake of a little.

Vardhamana saw the sense in this. He left guards with Sanjivaka and went on with the caravan. The guards were afraid of the forest too. They left the bull where he lay and followed the caravan. The next day they told the merchant a lie. “Master, Sanjivaka died. He was dear to you, so we gave him a funeral fire.” The merchant was grateful and held the last rites for his bull.

Sanjivaka's time had not come. Cool winds blew off the water of the Yamuna and revived him. Somehow he got to his feet and reached the river bank. There he ate the tips of young grass, green as emerald, and in a few days he was fat and strong, with a great hump, like the bull of Shiva. Every day he tore at the anthills with his horns and bellowed. What fate protects stands even with no one to guard it, the text says here, and what fate strikes down is lost however well it is guarded.

One day a lion named Pingalaka came down to the Yamuna to drink, with all the animals of the forest around him. From far off he heard Sanjivaka's deep bellow. His heart shook. He hid his fear, turned back without drinking, and sat down under a banyan tree with his court drawn up around him in four circles.

Two jackals always followed him. Their names were Karataka and Damanaka. They were sons of ministers and had lost their posts. Damanaka said, “Friend Karataka, our master came to the river thirsty. Now he has turned back, set his court in order around him, and sits under the banyan looking troubled. Why?” Karataka said, “That is no concern of ours. Whoever meddles in what is not his business comes to grief, like the monkey who pulled out the wedge.”

“We have the food he leaves,” Karataka went on. “That is enough. There is no reason to get mixed up in this.” Damanaka did not agree. “Is food all you want? Anyone can fill his own belly. Birds do it with their beaks. He truly lives on whom many others live. And a person who never shows what he can do is passed over. People step across fire while it sleeps inside the wood. Nobody steps across it once it burns.”

Karataka was still careful. “We hold no post. A fool who speaks to the king unasked earns disrespect, and mockery on top of it.” “Do not say that,” said Damanaka. “A person with no rank gains it by serving the king, and one with rank loses it by staying away. A king favours whoever is near him, learned or not, well born or not. Kings are like creepers. They wind themselves around whatever stands beside them.”

“What do you mean to do?” Karataka asked. “Our master is frightened today, and so is his whole court,” said Damanaka. “I mean to go to him, find out why, and put it right.” “How do you know he is frightened?” “That is not hard. Even an animal understands what it is told. A wise person works out what is left unsaid. A face, a walk and a look in the eye all show the mind inside. I will take away his fear with my wits, win him over, and have my minister's place back.”

Karataka warned him that kings are hard to please. They are like mountains, he said, rough and hard, and full of dangerous creatures. Damanaka knew it. “A clever person enters into the mood of whoever he is with, and soon has him in hand. And I will not speak out of turn.” “Then go,” said Karataka, “and may your road be a good one.” Damanaka bowed to him and set off toward the lion.

Next: The king's fear

A merchant named Vardhamana set out for Mathura with a loaded cart. Two bulls born in his own house drew it, Sanjivaka and Nandaka.

On the bank of the Yamuna, Sanjivaka sank in deep mud. The caravan could not wait in a forest full of lions, so it went on. The guards left with him slipped away and said he had died.

Sanjivaka recovered, grew strong on the river grass, and bellowed. The lion king Pingalaka came to drink, heard the sound, and was afraid. He turned back without drinking and sat under a banyan with his court around him.

Two jackals watched him, Karataka and Damanaka, ministers' sons who had lost their posts. Damanaka saw a frightened king as his way back to a minister's place. Karataka warned him to stay out of what was not their business.

Damanaka went to the lion's court. He reminded the king that his family had served the court for generations, and asked to speak with him alone.

Alone with him, the lion admitted that he feared the sound of some unknown creature and wanted to leave the forest. A sound by itself is nothing to fear, said Damanaka, and he offered to go and look.

Damanaka found that the terrible creature was only a bull. He saw his chance: a king with a trouble needs a minister. The lion promised him the minister's place if he could bring the creature into his service.

To the lion he said that this was the bull of the god Shiva, sent to graze by the Yamuna. To the bull he said that the lion carried the goddess. Each was glad to be given the other's promise of safety.

So Damanaka brought the bull before the lion. The lion welcomed him and told him to live close by, safe within his arms.

The lion and the bull became close friends and talked alone every day. The lion stopped hunting, and nobody else was let in. The court went hungry, and so did the two jackals.

Damanaka had brought the bull in, and now the bull had his place. He decided to part the two friends by a trick, so that neither would know. Karataka was afraid of it and said so.

Damanaka went to the lion when he was alone. He said the bull had told him in secret that he meant to kill the lion and rule in his place. The lion was stunned.

The lion would not turn on a friend to whom he had promised safety. Damanaka pressed him: a king who shuts out his own people for a stranger is lost. Then he told the lion what to watch for in the morning: a red face, and a bull who sat apart.

From the lion Damanaka went straight to the bull. He said the lion meant to kill him in the morning and feed the court with him.

The bull was shaken. He guessed that someone near the king had poisoned his mind out of envy. He did not guess who.

The bull saw nothing left for him except to fight. That did not suit Damanaka, because those horns could wound the lion. He urged the bull to leave the country by night.

Damanaka gave the bull a sign too: red eyes, a frown, a tongue licking the lips. Then he told Karataka the seed was sown. Karataka was appalled that two friends who loved each other had been thrown into a sea of anger.

Left alone, the bull decided to go to the lion and ask for shelter. He walked slowly, afraid, and saw on the lion's face the very sign he had been told to fear.

He sat down far off without bowing, and the lion saw the very manner he had been told to expect. The lion sprang. They tore at each other, red with blood.

While they fought, Karataka turned on Damanaka. A minister mends things gently, he said, and Damanaka had put their own king's life in danger.

Karataka named the cause as well: Damanaka could not bear to see the bull in favour. One who could bring his own king to this would not spare a friend, he said, and he told Damanaka to keep away.

The bull fell dead under the lion's claws. The lion remembered how good his friend had been and grieved. Nothing is worse, he said, than betraying one who trusted you.

Damanaka came up pleased and told him that a king does not mourn a traitor. The lion put away his grief. He ruled on with Damanaka as his minister.

What this story shows

The friendship ended because each friend listened to the jackal and neither spoke to the other.

  • Whoever carries a tale against a friend usually stands to gain from it.
  • Frightened people behave in ways that look like guilt, and a liar can count on that.
  • A ruler who gives all his time to one favourite leaves the rest hungry, and hungry courtiers look for a way back in.
