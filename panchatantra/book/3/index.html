The owls’ raids and the first counsels

Kakolukiya: part 1 of 9

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

Here begins the third book, called Of Crows and Owls. Its first verse says that one who was once an enemy and comes as a friend is not to be trusted, and it points to a cave full of owls, burnt by fire the crows brought.

Sanskrit verse 3.1

In the southern country there is a city named Mahilaropya. Near it stood a banyan tree with many branches and thick leaves. Meghavarna, king of the crows, lived there with a great following, and he had made the tree his fortress. Another king, Arimardana of the owls, lived with owls beyond counting in a fortress that was a mountain cave.

Every night the owl king came and circled the banyan. Because of an old enmity he killed whatever crow he could catch, and then went away. Night after night it went on, until the tree was almost emptied of crows.

That is how it goes, the text says. One who is too lazy to check an enemy or a sickness as it spreads is slowly killed by it, and an enemy or a sickness not put down at its birth will kill even the strong once it has grown.

Sanskrit verses 3.2, 3.3

One day the crow king called all his ministers. “Our enemy is fierce and tireless, and he knows his moment,” he said. “He comes at nightfall and slaughters our people. We cannot see at night, and by day we do not know where his fortress is, so we cannot strike back. There are six courses: peace, war, moving away, holding one’s ground, seeking allies, and the double game. Tell me which fits.”

They answered, “You do right to ask, lord. A minister should speak even unasked, and when he is asked he must say what is good for the king. One who says only what is pleasant, and leaves out what will turn out well, is counted an enemy. So let us take counsel in private and settle what is to be done and why.”

Sanskrit verses 3.4, 3.5, 3.6

The first to speak advised peace. “Those who bow to the stronger and strike only when the time is right keep their fortune, as rivers do not run uphill. An enemy who is truthful, upright and noble, who has brothers behind him, who is strong and has won many battles, is one to make peace with.

Sanskrit verses 3.7, 3.8

When life is at stake, peace should be made even with the ignoble, for if life is saved everything is saved. One who has the friendship of a victor in many wars soon sees his other enemies give way.

Sanskrit verses 3.9, 3.10

Brihaspati said to seek peace even with an equal, because victory in battle is never sure, and one should not stake things on what is unsure. So war comes only after the three other means have been tried.”

Sanskrit verses 3.11, 3.12

“Two equals who fight in pride are like two unbaked pots struck together, and both break. When the weak fights the strong it is a pot against a stone. War has three fruits: land, an ally or gold. If not one of them is to be had, there should be no war. A lion that digs at a mouse hole full of stones breaks his claws, and all he can win is a mouse.

Sanskrit verses 3.13, 3.14, 3.15, 3.16

One who is attacked by the stronger and wants his fortune to last should take the way of the reed, which bends, and never the way of the snake, which strikes and is killed. Let him draw in like a tortoise and bear even blows, and rise like a black snake when the time comes.

Sanskrit verses 3.18, 3.19, 3.20

A quarrel that has arisen is better settled by other means, and one does not rush in, because victory is never sure. No rule says one must fight the strong. A cloud never goes against the wind.”

Sanskrit verses 3.21, 3.22

The second minister spoke for war. “No treaty with an enemy holds, however tightly it is made. Water puts out fire even after it has been heated. One who has neither truth nor right in him soon turns again, however well he has been joined. So he must be fought, that is my view. An enemy who is cruel, greedy, lazy, false, careless, cowardly, unsteady, foolish and scornful of fighting men is easy to uproot.

Sanskrit verses 3.23, 3.24, 3.25

And he has already humbled us. If we talk of peace now, he will only grow angrier. Soft words do harm with an enemy who can be answered only by force. No sensible person pours water on a fever that needs sweating out. Soothing words inflame an angry enemy, as drops of water do hot ghee.”

Sanskrit verses 3.26, 3.27

“A lion sets his foot on the head of an elephant far larger than himself, because he has the greater spirit. The Bharadvajas say the small can kill the great if he has the energy. Enemies who cannot be killed by strength may be killed by guile, as Bhima killed Kichaka in a woman’s dress.

Sanskrit verses 3.28, 3.29, 3.30

Enemies submit to a king whose rod is as fierce as death, and count one who bears everything as so much straw. If a man’s fire does not put down the fire of others, there was no use in his being born.

Sanskrit verses 3.31, 3.32

To the high-spirited, fortune gives no joy until she is marked with the blood of their enemies, and there is no praise for a king whose land is not wet with that blood and with the tears of the enemies’ wives.”

Sanskrit verses 3.33, 3.34

The third, Anujivi, advised going away. “With an enemy who is too strong, wicked and bound by no rule, neither peace nor war is any use. Only moving is praised. There are two kinds of moving. One is to save life and goods, and the other is the march of a conqueror.

Sanskrit verses 3.35, 3.36

A conqueror with the greater strength marches into enemy land in the month of Karttika or Chaitra and at no other time, though a sudden raid is good at any time when the enemy is in trouble or has left a gap.

Sanskrit verses 3.37, 3.38

He first makes his own place secure with brave and trusted men, sends spies ahead, and then goes. One who enters another’s realm without knowing its roads, its supplies, its water and its crops does not come home again.”

Sanskrit verses 3.39, 3.40

“So what is right for you, lord, is to withdraw. The conqueror’s march is not for us, and neither war nor peace will do with that strong and wicked one. The wise withdraw for a reason. A ram steps back in order to strike, and a lion crouches before he springs. The wise keep their purpose in their hearts and their counsel hidden, and bear things while they reckon.

Sanskrit verse 3.41

One who leaves the country at the sight of a strong enemy lives to win the earth again, as Yudhishthira did. The weak one who fights the stronger out of pride does just what his enemy wants and ruins his own house. This is a time for withdrawing. It is no time for peace or for war.” That was Anujivi’s counsel.

Near a city in the south stood a great banyan, the fortress of the crow king. The owl king lived in a mountain cave with owls beyond counting. There was an old enmity between them.

Every night the owl king came circling the tree and killed whatever crow he could catch. Little by little the banyan was emptied of crows.

The crow king called his ministers. He named six courses: peace, war, moving away, holding one’s ground, seeking allies and the double game. He asked which one fitted.

The first minister advised peace, bending like a reed before the stronger. The second advised war, saying no treaty with such an enemy would hold.

The third advised withdrawing for a time, as a ram steps back before it strikes.
