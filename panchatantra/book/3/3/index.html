How the feud began: the owl’s coronation

Kakolukiya: part 3 of 9

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

Meghavarna heard his minister out and then asked why crows and owls had always hated each other to the death. Sthirajivi said, “Child, once the swans, parrots, cuckoos, chatakas, owls, peacocks, pigeons, doves, partridges and all the other birds met in great worry and began to take counsel.

‘Our king is Garuda,’ they said. ‘He is devoted to Vishnu and gives no thought to us. He is a lord to no purpose, for he does not protect us while the fowlers’ snares take us day after day.’”

“‘A king who does not protect creatures that are frightened and always harried by others is Death in the shape of a king. Without a king to steer them rightly, a people founders like a ship at sea without a helmsman.

Sanskrit verses 3.71, 3.72

Six are to be left as one leaves a broken boat on the ocean: a teacher who does not teach, a priest who does not study, a king who does not protect, a wife who speaks harshly, a cowherd who longs for the village and a barber who longs for the forest.

Sanskrit verses 3.73, 3.74

Let us think it over and make someone else king of the birds.’ They looked at the owl, who had a dignified air, and all said, ‘This owl shall be our king. Bring what is needed for a royal anointing.’”

“Water was brought from many holy places. The hundred and eight roots were made ready and the throne was set. A map of the earth was drawn with its seven continents, its oceans and its mountains, and a tiger skin was spread. Golden jars were filled, lamps and instruments stood ready, and mirrors and other lucky things were laid out.

The bards were reciting, the brahmins were chanting the Veda together, young women were singing, and the owl’s chief queen Krikalika had been brought. The owl was just about to sit on the throne for his anointing when a crow arrived from somewhere. He wondered what this gathering of all the birds and this great festival could be.”

“The birds saw him and said to one another, ‘Among birds the crow is said to be the clever one. People say the barber is the sharp one among men, the crow among birds, the jackal among beasts with fangs and the white-robed monk among ascetics. So we should hear what he says too. Plans that have been turned over many ways with many minds and well examined by the wise do not come apart.’

Sanskrit verses 3.75, 3.76

The crow came up and asked what the great assembly and festival were for. They said, ‘The birds have no king, so all of us have settled on anointing this owl as king of the birds. Give us your opinion too. You have come at the right moment.’”

“The crow laughed. ‘This is not right. There are peacocks, swans, cuckoos, chakravakas, parrots, ducks, green pigeons, cranes and other great birds, and you are anointing this one, who is blind by day and frightful to look at. I do not agree.

His beak is hooked, his eyes are crooked, and he looks cruel and unpleasant even when he is calm. I do not like to think of that face in anger. He is fierce by nature, very harsh, cruel and rough of speech. Nothing good will come to us from making the owl our king.’”

Sanskrit verses 3.77, 3.78

“‘Besides, Garuda is our lord. There is no reason to make this day-blind one king. Even if he had merits, a second king is not approved while one lord stands. One brilliant king is good for the earth, and many are a disaster, like many suns at the end of an age.

Sanskrit verse 3.79

Garuda’s name alone makes you safe from others. When the mere name of a great lord is spoken before the wicked, safety comes at once. To speak in the name of the great brings the highest success. The hares live in peace because they spoke in the name of the Moon.’ The birds asked how that had been, and the crow told them.”

“When he had told it, the crow went on, ‘And one who wants to live should never set over himself a master who is mean, lazy, cowardly, given to vice, ungrateful, or fond of talking behind one’s back. The hare and Kapinjala went looking for justice and came before a mean lord, and both of them perished.’ The birds asked how that had been, and he told them that too.”

“Then the crow said, ‘You too, if you take this mean, day-blind creature for your lord, will go the way of the hare and Kapinjala, for you are blind by night yourselves. Now that you know, do what is fitting.’ The birds heard him and said he had spoken well. They said they would meet again and take counsel about a king, and they all flew off wherever they pleased.”

“Only the owl was left, seated on the throne and ready for his anointing, with Krikalika beside him. ‘Who is there,’ he called. ‘My anointing has still not been done.’ Krikalika said, ‘Good sir, the crow has put this obstacle in your way. The birds have all gone off in every direction, and only that one crow is still here, for some reason of his own. Get up quickly and let me take you home.’

The owl was downcast. He said to the crow, ‘Wicked one, I never did you harm, and you have wrecked my anointing. From today there is enmity between us, and between your line and mine. A wound from an arrow heals, and a cut from a sword heals. The wound of an ugly word, ill spoken, never heals.’ With that he went home with Krikalika.”

Sanskrit verse 3.111

“The crow was shaken with fear. He thought, ‘I have made an enemy for no reason. I should never have spoken as I did. Words said without regard to place and time, with no good to come of them, unpleasant and lowering to the speaker, and said without cause, are nothing but poison.

Sanskrit verse 3.112

A sensible person does not make an enemy of his own accord, even when he is strong, as no one eats poison for nothing because he has a physician. The wise never speak blame in an assembly. Even a truth should not be said if saying it brings unhappiness.

Sanskrit verses 3.113, 3.114

The wise one is he who acts only after talking a matter over many times with trusted friends and weighing it in his own mind. Fortune and good name come to such a one.’”

Sanskrit verse 3.115

“So thinking, the crow went away too. From that day there has been enmity between us and the owls, handed down from line to line.”

The king asked how the feud began. The old minister said the birds had once met, unhappy that their king Garuda gave them no thought, and had chosen the owl as their new king.

Everything was ready for the anointing, the throne, the jars and the lamps. Just then a crow arrived and asked what the festival was for.

The crow laughed at their choice. He said the owl was blind by day and harsh to look at, and that Garuda’s name alone kept them safe. He told two stories to make his point.

The birds agreed and flew away, and the owl was left on the throne with his queen. He swore enmity between his line and the crow’s. The crow went off afraid, sorry for words he need not have spoken.
