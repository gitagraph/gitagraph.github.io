Counsel to the king

Kakolukiya: part 9 of 9

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

When he had told it, Sthirajivi said, “King, as Mandavisha destroyed the frogs by the strength of his wits, so I have destroyed all our enemies. It is well said that a fire blazing in the forest burns and still spares the roots, while a wind that is soft and cool pulls up a tree, roots and all.”

Sanskrit verse 3.242

Meghavarna said, “Father, that is true. The great-souled, who have great courage, do not give up what they have begun even when they fall into trouble. That is the greatness of the great, who wear policy as their ornament: they do not let go of what is begun, however hard things become.

Sanskrit verse 3.243

The low never begin, for fear of obstacles. The middling begin and stop when an obstacle strikes them. The best are struck by obstacles a thousand times over and do not abandon what they have begun.

Sanskrit verse 3.244

You have cleared my kingdom of thorns by destroying my enemies to the last. That is the way of those who know policy. The wise do not sink when they have left no remnant of a debt, a fire, an enemy or a sickness.”

Sanskrit verse 3.245

Sthirajivi said, “Lord, the good fortune is yours, for everything you begin succeeds. Courage alone does not carry a task through. What is done with understanding is what brings victory. Enemies killed with weapons are not truly killed, and enemies killed by wit are killed indeed. A weapon kills one man’s body. Wit kills a house, its wealth and its name.

Sanskrit verse 3.246

For one who has both understanding and manly effort, success comes without strain. When a man is about to prosper, at the start of his task his mind opens out and his memory grows firm, good counsel comes to him by itself and does not fail, his reasoning strikes true, his heart rises and he takes delight in work that deserves praise.

Sanskrit verse 3.247

And a kingdom comes to one who has policy, generosity and courage. People like to be near the generous, the brave and the learned, and such a one gains merit. To the one with merit comes wealth, from wealth comes glory, to the glorious comes command, and from that a kingdom.”

Sanskrit verse 3.248

Meghavarna said, “The science of policy surely bears fruit at once. You went in among them by falling in with their wishes, and Arimardana and all his people are destroyed.” Sthirajivi said, “Even when an aim can be reached only by sharp means, it is right to begin by drawing close. The lord of the forest trees, tall and the pride of the woods, is honoured and worshipped before it is cut down.

Sanskrit verse 3.249

But there is little use, master, in speaking of a thing that is not followed at once by action, or that is hard to carry out. The words of those who cannot decide, who fear to act and who talk only for the pleasure of talking, fall out of step with what follows and become a joke in the world.”

Sanskrit verse 3.250

“Nor should the wise be careless even of small duties. Some people think, ‘I can do this, it is a small thing and needs no effort, there is no call for care,’ and they neglect it. Their minds are elsewhere, and they come to the pain of regret, which is easy to find once trouble starts.

Sanskrit verse 3.251

Today my lord has beaten his enemy and will sleep as he used to. One sleeps well in a house that has no snake, or where the snake has been caught. Where a snake is always in sight, sleep is hard to come by.

Sanskrit verse 3.252

Those who are driven by honour, pride and valour find no peace in their restless hearts until they have reached the end of the great tasks they set themselves, tasks won by long effort, blessed by those who love them and rising by policy and daring to the height of their hopes. Now that the work I began is finished, my own heart seems to rest.”

Sanskrit verse 3.253

“So now enjoy this kingdom, cleared of its thorns, for a long time. Give yourself to the care of your people, and may the umbrella, the throne and the royal fortune stay unshaken with your sons and your sons’ sons.

A king who does not win his people by protecting them and by his other merits has a kingdom as useless as the teat on a goat’s neck. A king who loves merit, turns from vice and delights in good servants enjoys royal fortune for long, with its waving fly-whisks and its white umbrella.”

Sanskrit verses 3.254, 3.255

“And do not let the pride of fortune ruin you with the thought that you have won a kingdom. A king’s fortunes do not stay. Royal fortune is as hard to climb as a bamboo pole, and it loves to fall in a moment. A hundred efforts can hardly hold it, and however well it is served it deceives in the end.

It is as scattered as the mind of a monkey, it clings no better than water on a lotus leaf, and it is as restless as the wind. It is as unsteady as friendship with the ignoble and as hard to tend as a poisonous snake.

Its glow lasts a moment, like a streak of cloud at evening. It breaks as easily as a row of bubbles, it is as thankless as the body, and it is gone as soon as seen, like a heap of treasure found in a dream.”

“The moment one is anointed king, one’s mind should be set on the troubles that may come. The jars at a king’s anointing pour out trouble along with the water.

Sanskrit verse 3.256

And no one is beyond the reach of misfortune. Think of Rama’s wandering, the Pandavas’ years in the forest, the destruction of the Vrishnis, King Nala’s fall from his throne, Arjuna as a dancing master and the fall of the lord of Lanka. Everyone here bears what time brings, and no one can shield another.”

Sanskrit verse 3.257

“Dasharatha went to heaven and was a friend of Indra. King Sagara set the ocean its shore. Prithu was born from the palm of a hand, and Manu was the son of the Sun. Mighty Time woke each of them and closed their eyes again.

Sanskrit verse 3.258

Mandhata conquered the three worlds. There was King Satyavrata, and Nahusha, who was king of the gods, and Keshava, who knew the true teachings. With their chariots and their great elephants they thought themselves seated on Indra’s throne. Great Time alone raised them, and Time took them away.

Sanskrit verse 3.259

That king, those ministers, those women and those woods and groves have all been looked upon by Death and are gone. So, now that you have won royal fortune, which trembles like the ear of an elephant in rut, enjoy it and hold to justice alone.”

Sanskrit verse 3.260

Here ends the third book, Of Crows and Owls.

What this story shows

The owls had every warning they needed, from one minister who saw clearly. They chose the pleasanter advice, and it cost them everything.

The book is built as two councils. The crow king hears five ministers argue for peace, war, withdrawal, holding fast and alliance, and then takes the sixth course, the one his oldest minister calls the double game. The owl king hears five ministers too, and four of them tell him what is kind and comfortable. Only Raktaksha tells him what is true.

The crows win by a deception, and the text reports it as a deception, carried through to a harsh end. What the book teaches is on the other side of the war: how to listen. The owls’ fall begins long before the fire, on the day a king preferred four agreeable voices to one unwelcome one. The stories inside make the same turn again and again, from the hare and the cat to the frog king on the snake.

  • Every one of the six courses is argued well, and each is right for some time and wrong for another.
  • The feud itself began with words spoken in an assembly that the speaker at once regretted.
  • The owl king lost because he would not hear the one minister who disagreed.

The king praised him for leaving no remnant of the enemy. The old minister said the good fortune was the king’s, and that understanding wins what courage alone cannot.

He told the king to sleep in peace now, as one sleeps in a house with no snake, and to care for his people. A kingdom that does not protect them, he said, is of no use.

He warned the king against the pride of fortune, which is gone like treasure seen in a dream. The great kings of old were all taken by Time. He asked him to enjoy his kingdom and hold to justice alone.
