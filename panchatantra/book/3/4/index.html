Sthirajivi’s ruse

Kakolukiya: part 4 of 9

Meghavarna said, “Father, with things as they are, tell me what we are to do.” Sthirajivi said, “Child, even so, there is one large design that lies outside the six courses. I will take it on myself and go to win this war. I mean to deceive our enemies and destroy them. Rogues with many wits and sound knowledge can cheat even the strong, as they cheated the brahmin of his goat.” Meghavarna asked how that had been, and Sthirajivi told him.

When he had told it, Sthirajivi said, “It is well said that no one in this world has escaped being taken in by the humble ways of a new servant, the talk of a guest, the tears of a courtesan and a rogue’s flood of words. And a quarrel with many is not wise, even when they are weak. One should not make enemies of the many, for a multitude is hard to overcome. Ants eat even a great snake, however he thrashes.” Meghavarna asked how that had been, and Sthirajivi told him that too.

Then Sthirajivi said, “So I have something to say here. Mark it, and do as I tell you.” Meghavarna said, “Command me. Your order will be carried out exactly.” Sthirajivi said, “Listen then, child. I have thought of a fifth means, beyond conciliation and the other three. Treat me as one who has taken the enemy’s side, and abuse me with harsh words. So that the enemy’s spies believe it, smear me with blood that you have brought, throw me down at the foot of this banyan, and go away to Mount Rishyamuka. Stay there with your people. I will win the trust of all our foes by a careful plan, and learn the inside of their fortress. Then I will come and lead you to them by day, when they are blind, and we will kill them. I have thought it through, and there is no other way for us to succeed.”

“Their fortress has no way of escape, and so it will serve only for their death. Those who know policy call a place a fortress when it has a way out. One with no way out is a prison under the name of a fortress. And you are not to pity me. Servants may be as dear as life, cared for and cherished, and still when battle comes they are to be looked on as dry firewood. One guards one’s servants like one’s own life and feeds them like one’s own body, all for the sake of the single day when the enemy is met. So you must not forbid me in this.”

Sanskrit verses 3.122, 3.123, 3.124

Having said this, he began a sham quarrel with the king. The king’s other servants saw Sthirajivi speaking wildly and rose to kill him. Meghavarna stopped them. “Stand back, all of you. I will punish this wicked friend of the enemy myself.” He got on top of Sthirajivi, struck him with light blows of his beak and drenched him with blood brought for the purpose. Then, as he had been told, he went away with his people to Mount Rishyamuka.

Meanwhile Krikalika, who was acting as the enemy’s spy, carried word of all this to the owl king: the minister’s fall, and the crow king’s leaving with all his people. At sunset the owl king set out with his ministers and his following to kill the crows. “Hurry, hurry,” he said. “A frightened enemy who is bent on flight is caught only by good luck. An enemy on the move leaves a gap, and so does one who is seeking new shelter. In the confusion of his servants he is easily mastered.”

Sanskrit verse 3.125

With these words he drew his army round the foot of the banyan on every side. Not a crow was to be seen. Arimardana perched at the tip of a branch, glad at heart, while his bards praised him. He said to his attendants, “Find out which way they went. Learn by which road the crows have fled. Before they reach a fortress we must follow and kill them. An enemy who has reached even a hedge is hard for a conqueror to kill, and one who has reached a well-stocked fortress is far harder.”

Sanskrit verse 3.126

At this point Sthirajivi thought, “If these enemies go back the way they came without learning of me, I shall have achieved nothing. Never to begin a task is the first mark of good sense, and to carry through what one has begun is the second. It is better not to begin than to break off halfway. I will make a sound and let them see me.” He gave a faint cry. All the owls heard it and came to kill him. He said, “I am Sthirajivi, minister to Meghavarna. It is Meghavarna himself who has brought me to this state. Tell your master. I have much to say to him.”

Sanskrit verse 3.127

They reported it, and the owl king, astonished, came to him at once. He asked how Sthirajivi had come to such a state. Sthirajivi said, “Lord, hear how it happened. Yesterday that wicked Meghavarna, pained by the many crows you have killed, was seized with anger and grief and set out to fight you. I said to him, ‘Master, it is not right for you to go against them. They are strong and we are weak. A weak one who wants to prosper should not even think of opposing the stronger. The one who bends like a reed is not killed, and the one who flies like a moth at a flame is plainly lost. Peace bought with a gift is the right course. At the sight of a strong enemy the wise save their lives even by giving up everything, for when life is saved wealth comes again.’”

Sanskrit verses 3.128, 3.129

“When he heard this, wicked advisers stirred him up. He suspected me of taking your side and brought me to this. Now your feet are my only refuge. There is little more to say. As soon as I am able to move, I will lead you to his dwelling and bring about the end of all the crows.”

Arimardana heard this and took counsel with his ministers, who had served his father and grandfather before him. There were five of them: Raktaksha, Kruraksha, Diptaksha, Vakranasa and Prakarakarna. He asked Raktaksha first. “Good sir, this minister of our enemy has fallen into my hands. Tell me what should be done with him.” Raktaksha said, “Lord, there is nothing to consider. He should be killed without a second thought. A weak enemy is to be killed before he grows strong, for once he has come into his full strength he is hard to conquer. People say, too, that Fortune curses the one who turns her away when she comes of herself. The moment comes once to one who waits for it. It is hard to find again when one wishes to act.”

Then the old minister set out his plan. The king was to abuse him as a traitor, smear him with blood, leave him under the banyan and take the crows away to a mountain. He would win the owls’ trust and learn the inside of their fortress.

They staged a quarrel in front of everyone. The king left his old minister lying under the tree and flew off with all his crows.

At sunset the owls came and found the banyan empty. The old crow gave a faint cry so that they would find him, and asked to speak to their king.

He told the owl king he had advised peace and been punished for it, and offered to lead the owls to the crows. The owl king asked his five ministers what to do. The first, the red-eyed one, said to kill the crow at once.
