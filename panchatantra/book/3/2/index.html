Holding the fort, allies, and Sthirajivi’s plan

Kakolukiya: part 2 of 9

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

The king heard him and turned to Prajivi. “Good sir, give your view too.” Prajivi said, “Lord, peace, war and going away, none of the three seems right to me. What seems right is to hold our ground. A crocodile in his own water drags down even a great elephant, and out of it he is worried by a dog. One who is attacked by the strong should stay in his fortress, work hard, and send from there for his friends. One who hears the enemy is coming and leaves his place in terror does not enter it again. A snake without fangs, an elephant without rut and a king without his own ground can be approached by anyone. One fighter on his own ground can face a hundred, even strong ones. So one’s own ground is not to be given up.”

Sanskrit verses 3.45, 3.46, 3.47, 3.48, 3.49

“Make the fortress strong, then, with good soldiers and stores, with wall and moat and weapons. Stay inside it, always ready to fight. If you live you will keep your kingdom, and if you die you will reach heaven. The small who hold together in one place are not hurt even by the strong, as creepers growing together are not broken by a hard wind. A great tree, strong and well rooted, can be thrown down by the wind when it stands alone. Trees that stand together, rooted on every side, are not felled even by a storm. A man by himself, however brave, is thought fair game by his enemies, and then they harm him.” That was Prajivi’s counsel, the one called holding one’s ground.

Sanskrit verses 3.50, 3.51, 3.52, 3.53, 3.54, 3.55

The king then asked Chiranjivi for his view. He said, “Lord, of the six courses the one that seems right to me is to seek an ally. Without a helper even an able and brilliant man can do little. A fire lit where no wind blows goes out by itself. Company is best, and the company of one’s own side above all. Grains of rice that have lost even their husks do not sprout. So stay here and turn to someone able, who can meet this trouble. If you leave your own place and go elsewhere, no one will help you even with a word. The wind is a friend to the fire that burns a forest, and the same wind puts out a lamp. No one is a friend to the weak.”

Sanskrit verses 3.56, 3.57, 3.58

“Nor does it have to be one strong ally. Joining with many small ones is protection too. A bamboo in a thick clump cannot be pulled up, and it is the same with a weak king who has others round him. And if the ally is a great one, there is nothing more to say. Nearness to the great lifts anyone. A drop of water on a lotus leaf takes on the beauty of a pearl. So without an ally there is no remedy, that is my view.” That was Chiranjivi’s counsel.

Sanskrit verses 3.59, 3.60

When they had all spoken, King Meghavarna bowed to Sthirajivi, his father’s old minister, a crow of great age who knew the whole science of policy. “Father,” he said, “I questioned these ministers in your presence to test them, so that you might hear it all and tell me what is fitting. Tell me now.” Sthirajivi said, “Child, each of them has spoken according to the books, and each course is useful in its own time. But this is the time for the double game. Against an enemy who is wicked and stronger, one stays on guard always and trusts neither peace nor war. An enemy who has been brought to trust, by those who do not trust him and who hold out a bait, is easily destroyed. The wise sometimes let an enemy grow whom they mean to destroy, as phlegm is fed with molasses before it is driven out altogether.”

Sanskrit verses 3.61, 3.62

“It is said that one who gives his whole trust to women, to an enemy, to a false friend, or above all to courtesans does not live. Whole-hearted dealing is for the gods, for brahmins, for one’s teacher and for oneself. Everything else is to be handled with two minds. A single mind is praised in ascetics who have mastered themselves. It is not praised in those who want fortune, and least of all in kings. If you take this double course you will stay in your own home, you will draw the enemy out with a bait, and when you see a gap in his guard you will go and kill him.”

Sanskrit verses 3.63, 3.64, 3.65

Meghavarna said, “Father, I do not even know where he lives. I have no way of learning his weak places.” Sthirajivi said, “Child, I will lay bare both his dwelling and his weak places through spies. Cows see by smell, brahmins see by the Vedas, kings see by their spies, and other people see with their eyes. A king who knows, through secret agents, the key posts on his own side and still more on the enemy’s side does not come to grief.”

Sanskrit verses 3.66, 3.67

Meghavarna asked what these posts were, how many there were, and what kind of agents were meant. Sthirajivi said, “The sage Narada spoke of this to Yudhishthira. There are eighteen such posts on the enemy’s side and fifteen on one’s own, and each is to be watched by three agents unknown to one another. Once they are known, both sides are in one’s hand. A post here means a person who holds an office. If such people are bad it is the ruin of their master, and if they are good he prospers.”

Sanskrit verse 3.68

“On the enemy’s side they are the minister, the chaplain, the general, the crown prince, the doorkeeper, the keeper of the inner rooms, the steward, the collector of revenue, the keeper of stores, the magistrate, the herald, the master of the army, the master of the elephants, the treasurer, the wardens of fort, tax and border, and the chief retainers. By setting these at odds an enemy is quickly overcome. On one’s own side they are the queen, the queen mother, the chamberlain, the garland-maker, the keeper of the bed, the head of the spies, the astrologer, the physician, the bearer of betel, the teacher, the bodyguard, the quartermaster, the umbrella-bearer and the courtesan. Through the ill will of these, ruin comes from within.”

“The physician, the astrologer and the teacher are the agents set over one’s own side. Among the enemy it is snake-charmers and madmen who learn everything. Let agents who know their work step in at these posts, as at the fords of a river, and sound the depth of the great water that is the enemy.”

Sanskrit verses 3.69, 3.70

The fourth minister said to hold the fortress. A crocodile in his own water drags down an elephant, he said, and trees that stand together are not felled by the wind.

The fifth said to seek an ally, since a fire lit where no wind blows goes out by itself.

Then the king bowed to his father’s old minister and asked for his view. The old crow said each course had its time, and that this was the time for the double game: to win the enemy’s trust and wait for a gap in his guard.

The king did not even know where the owls lived. The old minister said spies would find that out, for kings see by their spies. He listed the posts on each side that must be watched.
