The cave is burnt; Sthirajivi’s account

Kakolukiya: part 8 of 9

When Raktaksha had gone, Sthirajivi was delighted. He thought, “This is good fortune for us. Raktaksha saw far ahead, and he is gone. These others are dull of mind and will be easy for me to destroy.

It is said that a king whose old family ministers cannot see far ahead will surely come to ruin before long. It is also well said that the wise can tell enemies who wear the look of ministers: they are the ones who leave the right course and follow its opposite.”

Sanskrit verses 3.219, 3.220

With this thought he began to bring one small stick from the forest each day and add it to his nest, so that the cave could be set alight. The foolish owls did not understand that he was building up his nest in order to burn them. It is well said that one whom fate has struck makes a friend of his enemy, hates and harms his friend, and takes good for bad and bad for good.

Sanskrit verse 3.221

So, under cover of a nest, a heap of wood was made at the gate of the fortress. Then, at sunrise, when the owls were blind, Sthirajivi flew quickly to Mount Rishyamuka and said to Meghavarna, “Master, the enemy’s cave is ready to be burnt. Come with all your people, each carrying one burning stick, and drop it on my nest at the mouth of the cave. That will be the end of all our enemies.”

Meghavarna was overjoyed. “Father, tell me all that has happened to you. It is long since I saw you.” Sthirajivi said, “Child, this is no time for telling. Some spy of the enemy may report that I have come here, and then the blind one will move away somewhere else.

Hurry. When someone delays over work that should be done quickly, the gods grow angry and spoil it. When a task that is ready to bear fruit is not done at once, time drinks up the fruit. When you have come to their cave and your enemy is destroyed, I will tell you everything at leisure.”

Sanskrit verses 3.222, 3.223

Meghavarna heard him. He and his people took one burning stick each in their beaks, came to the mouth of the cave and dropped them on Sthirajivi’s nest. The owls, blind by day and shut in by the blocked gate, remembered Raktaksha’s words and died inside the cave. Having destroyed his enemies to the last one, Meghavarna went back to his fortress in the banyan tree.

There he took his seat on the throne, glad at heart, and in the full assembly he said to Sthirajivi, “Father, tell us how you passed so long a time in the midst of enemies. We are eager to hear it. They say that for the virtuous it is better to fall into a blazing fire than to keep company with enemies even for a moment.”

Sanskrit verse 3.224

Sthirajivi said, “Good sir, a servant who looks to the fruit that is coming does not feel the hardship. With the task in view, even poison tastes like nectar. There is no doubt of that for any creature.”

Sanskrit verse 3.225

“When danger is at hand, any path that serves one’s good is to be taken with a skilful mind, whether it is grand or humble. Arjuna’s arms were like the trunks of elephants, scarred by the bowstring and skilled with great weapons, and he covered them with bangles like a woman’s.

Sanskrit verse 3.226

A capable and wise person who is waiting for a better time has to live even among mean and wicked people whose words fall like thunderbolts. Mighty Bhima toiled as a cook in the house of the Matsya king, a ladle in his hand and smoke on his face.

Sanskrit verse 3.227

One who has fallen on hard times and is waiting for his hour does whatever work comes, praised or blamed, and keeps his purpose in his heart. Arjuna, whose hand was hardened by the string of his great bow, wore a dancer’s belt and danced.”

Sanskrit verse 3.228

“One who wants to succeed holds in his own fire, however spirited he is, and stays steady under what fate sends. Yudhishthira, the glorious son of Dharma, had brothers as strong as Indra, Kubera and Yama, and he still lived long and wretchedly in Virata’s house. The two sons of Madri, handsome, well born and strong, became Virata’s servants and tended his cattle.

Sanskrit verses 3.229, 3.230

Draupadi was matchless in beauty, graced with youth, born in the highest family and lovely as the goddess of fortune. Even she, when time turned against her, was ordered about as a maid by proud young women and ground sandalwood in the palace of the Matsya king.”

Sanskrit verse 3.231

Meghavarna said, “Father, to live with an enemy is like the vow of walking on a sword’s edge, I think.” Sthirajivi said, “Lord, it is so. But nowhere have I seen such a gathering of fools.

There was no one of sense among them except Raktaksha, who is very wise and without equal in his grasp of many sciences. He alone saw my mind as it really was. The other ministers are great fools who live on the mere name of minister and have no skill in the truth of things, for they did not see even this much.”

“A servant who has come over from the enemy is not to be trusted. He is still bound to the side he came from, he is like a spy, and he is a constant cause of fear.

Sanskrit verse 3.232

Enemies strike at their foes when they are off their guard, when they see an opening as they sit, lie down, travel, eat or drink. So the wise guard themselves with every care, for the self is the seat of all that life aims at, and carelessness destroys it.

Sanskrit verses 3.233, 3.234

It is well said that sickness comes to anyone who eats what is bad for him, and failures of policy come to any king with bad ministers. Fortune makes everyone proud, death takes everyone, and the pleasures of the senses torment everyone who gives in to them.

Sanskrit verse 3.235

The greedy lose their good name and the slanderer loses his friends. One who neglects his rites loses his family, one who thinks only of gain loses his sense of right, the dissolute lose the fruit of learning and the miser loses his comfort. A king whose ministers are careless loses his kingdom.”

Sanskrit verse 3.236

“So, king, what you called the vow of the sword’s edge, living among enemies, I have known at first hand. It is said that a wise person puts insult in front and honour behind and brings his purpose through, for it is folly to let the purpose be lost.

Sanskrit verse 3.237

When the time calls for it, a wise person will carry even an enemy on his shoulder. A great black snake killed many frogs in that way.” Meghavarna asked how that had been, and Sthirajivi told him.

With him gone, the old crow brought one stick from the forest each day and added it to his nest at the gate. The owls did not understand what the heap was for.

At sunrise, when the owls were blind, he flew to the mountain and told his king the cave was ready. The crows came, each with a burning stick in its beak.

They dropped the sticks on the nest at the mouth of the cave, and the owls, shut in, died inside. The crow king went back to his banyan with his enemies gone.

The king asked how he had borne so long among enemies. The old minister said that one who looks to the outcome does not feel the hardship. He recalled how Arjuna, Bhima and Draupadi once lived as servants in another king’s house.

He said only the red-eyed minister had seen through him, and the rest were fools. A wise person, he said, will carry even an enemy on his shoulder when the time calls.
