The crow at the mouse’s door

Mitra Samprapti: part 2 of 6

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

The crow had watched the whole rescue, and he was amazed. What wit this Hiranyaka had, what strength, and what a fort. The crow trusted no one and was restless by nature, and still he wanted this mouse for a friend. Even those who lack nothing should make friends, the text says, as the sea, though full, waits for the moon to rise. He came down from his tree, stood at the mouth of the burrow and called in a voice like Chitragriva’s. “Come out, Hiranyaka, come out.”

Sanskrit verse 2.28

The mouse wondered whether some pigeon was still caught. “Who are you?” “I am a crow named Laghupatanaka.” At that the mouse drew back deeper into his hole. “Go away from here at once.” “I have come on serious business,” said the crow. “I saw you free Chitragriva and it filled me with affection. Some day I may be caught too, and you could free me. Be my friend.”

“You are the eater and I am the food,” said Hiranyaka. “How can there be friendship between us? Friendship and marriage are for those equal in wealth and equal in family, the text says, and never for the well fed and the starved. Go.” “Then I will sit here at your door,” said the crow. “If you will not be my friend, I will go without food until I die.”

Sanskrit verse 2.29

“How can I make friends with an enemy?” said the mouse. “Water puts out fire, however hot the water has been made.” “We have never even met,” said the crow. “Where is the enmity?” “Enmity is of two kinds,” said Hiranyaka, “the kind one is born with and the kind that is made. The made kind ends when a kindness is done. The born kind never ends short of a death. So it is between the mongoose and the snake, the grass-eater and the beast with claws, water and fire, the dog and the cat, the rich and the poor, the hunter and the deer. None of them has killed the other, and still they torment each other. You and I are enemies by birth.”

Sanskrit verses 2.31, 2.32

“There is always a cause for friendship and a cause for enmity,” said the crow, “so a sensible person chooses friendship. Be my friend.” “Hear the whole of wise conduct,” said the mouse. “Whoever tries to make it up with a friend who has once turned bad takes death on, as a mule does when she conceives. And I cannot count on my own good qualities to keep me safe. A lion took the life of Panini, who made the grammar. An elephant crushed the sage Jaimini. A crocodile killed Pingala on the seashore. Beasts in a rage have no use for learning.”

Sanskrit verses 2.33, 2.34, 2.35

“That is so,” said the crow. “Hear this as well. The good become friends at sight. A bad man is like a clay pot, easy to break and hard to mend, and a good one is like a pot of gold, hard to break and easy to mend. The friendship of the good is like sugarcane eaten from the top, sweeter at every joint. It is like the shadow of the afternoon, small at first and growing, where the friendship of the bad is the shadow of the morning. I am one of the good. I will swear any oath to put your fear to rest.”

Sanskrit verses 2.36, 2.37, 2.38, 2.39

“I put no trust in your oaths,” said Hiranyaka. “An enemy is not to be trusted even when he has sworn peace. Indra swore an oath and then killed Vritra. An enemy who gets in by the smallest crack destroys you slowly, as water fills a boat. Do not trust the one you distrust, and do not trust too far the one you trust, for the danger that comes from trust cuts through the roots. The weak who trust no one are not caught by the strong, and the strong who trust are soon caught by the weak.”

Sanskrit verses 2.40, 2.43, 2.44, 2.45

The crow had no answer to this, and he admired the mouse all the more. “Hiranyaka,” he said, “the wise say that friendship among the good is made in seven steps, or seven words. So you are my friend already. Hear what I ask. Stay inside your fort if you cannot trust me, and talk with me there every day about what is good and bad and about fine sayings.”

Sanskrit verse 2.48

Hiranyaka thought it over. This crow spoke cleverly and truthfully, and friendship with him would be right. “You must never set foot inside my fort,” he said. “So be it,” said the crow. From that day the two of them enjoyed their talk together. The crow brought pieces of meat and other good things, and the mouse brought rice and other food by night. To give and to take, to tell secrets and to ask them, to eat together and to feed each other: these six, the text says, are the signs of affection.

Sanskrit verse 2.50

So their affection grew until it could no more be parted than a nail from the flesh. In time the mouse trusted the crow so fully that he would sit between the crow’s wings while they talked.

Sanskrit verse 2.55

The crow said he would sit at the door and starve. The mouse answered that enemies by birth cannot be friends and that no oath makes an enemy safe. The crow replied that the friendship of the good is hard to break and easy to mend.

At last the mouse agreed, on the condition that the crow never enter his burrow. They met every day, talked, and brought each other food. In time the mouse trusted the crow enough to sit between his wings.

One day the crow came in tears. There was famine, no one put out food, and snares for birds were set at every house. He meant to go south to a lake where his friend the tortoise lived.
