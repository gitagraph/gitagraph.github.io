The net

Mitra Samprapti: part 1 of 6

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

This is the second book, Winning Friends. It opens with one verse. Those who have no means, if they are wise and learned, get their work done quickly, as the crow, the mouse, the deer and the tortoise did.

Sanskrit verse 2.1

In the southern country, not far from the city of Mahilaropya, stood a great banyan tree. Birds of every kind ate its fruit, insects lived in its hollows, and travellers rested in its shade. A tree that gives comfort to so many creatures with every part of itself is worth praising, the text says, and any other is only a weight on the earth. A crow named Laghupatanaka lived in it.

Sanskrit verse 2.2

One day the crow set out toward the city to find food. He saw a man coming the other way with a net in his hand, dark and rough, with cracked feet and hair standing up, like a servant of Death. The crow was alarmed. This man was heading for the banyan, and he could not tell what it would mean for the birds who lived there. He turned back at once and warned them all. “A hunter is coming with a net and rice. He will spread the net and scatter the grains. Look on that rice as poison.”

The hunter came under the tree, spread his net, scattered white grains of rice over it and hid a little way off. The birds of the banyan remembered the crow’s words and stayed where they were, looking at the rice as if it were poison.

Just then Chitragriva, king of the pigeons, came by with a thousand followers, looking for food. He saw the rice from far off. The crow tried to stop him, but his tongue was greedy and he flew down to eat. He and his whole flock were caught. So fish in deep water meet a death they never thought of, the text says, when the tongue leads them. It adds that he was hardly to blame. When trouble is near and fate has turned, even great minds go crooked.

Sanskrit verses 2.3, 2.5

The hunter saw them caught and ran up, glad, with his stick raised. Chitragriva spoke to his pigeons. “Do not be afraid. Whoever keeps his wits in every kind of trouble comes through it. The great are the same in good times and bad, as the sun is red when it rises and red when it sets. Let us all fly up together, net and all, and get out of his sight. If we lose our heads and do not rise together, we die. Many thin threads laid side by side can bear a great weight.”

Sanskrit verses 2.6, 2.7, 2.8

They did so. The hunter ran along the ground after the net as it went through the air. “They carry it because they are of one mind,” he said, looking up. “When they start to quarrel they will fall.” The crow forgot his own meal and followed to see what would happen. The pigeons flew out of sight. The hunter gave up and turned back. What is not meant to be does not happen, he said, and when fate turns away it takes what a man already had. He had come for bird meat and lost the net that fed his family.

Sanskrit verses 2.9, 2.10, 2.11

Chitragriva saw that the hunter had gone. “He has turned back,” he said. “Now let us go, without fear, north-east of Mahilaropya. My friend Hiranyaka the mouse lives there, and he will cut these cords. When trouble comes, no one but a friend gives help, even so much as a kind word.” So the pigeons came to the mouse’s burrow. It was a fort with a thousand openings, and inside it Hiranyaka lived at ease, afraid of nothing. A king without a fort, the text says, is like a snake without fangs.

Sanskrit verses 2.12, 2.13

Chitragriva stood at the burrow and called loudly. “Friend Hiranyaka, come quickly. I am in great trouble.” From inside his fort the mouse asked who it was and what the trouble could be. “It is Chitragriva, king of the pigeons, your friend. Come quickly, the matter is serious.” Hearing the name, the mouse came hurrying out, his whole body glad. Friends who bring joy to the eyes, the text says, do not come every day even to the houses of the great.

Sanskrit verse 2.16

Then he saw Chitragriva and all his flock bound in the net, and his gladness turned to dismay. “What is this?” “You know already and still you ask,” said Chitragriva. “Whatever one has done comes back from fate at its own time and in its own way. I am bound because my tongue was greedy. Cut us loose quickly.” Hiranyaka said that a bird can see its food from a hundred leagues away and still not see the snare beside it. When he saw the sun and moon seized in eclipse, elephants and snakes and birds held captive, and the wise left poor, he thought fate strong indeed.

Sanskrit verses 2.19, 2.20, 2.21

He went to cut Chitragriva’s cords first. “No, friend,” said Chitragriva. “Free my followers first and me after them.” The mouse was annoyed. “That is not right. The master comes before the servants.” “Do not say so,” said the pigeon. “These poor birds all depend on me. They left their own families to follow me. I owe them at least this much honour. A king who honours his servants keeps them even when his money is gone. And your teeth may break while you are cutting my cords, or the hunter may come. If I were free while they were still bound, I would surely go to hell.”

Sanskrit verses 2.23, 2.25

Hiranyaka was delighted. “I know the duty of a king. I was testing you,” he said. “I will free them all first. With such care for those who serve you, and such readiness to share, you are fit to rule the three worlds.” He cut every cord. “Go home now, friend,” he said to Chitragriva, “and come to me again if trouble finds you.” The pigeons flew home and the mouse went back into his fort. One who has friends, the text says, gets even hard things done, so a person should make friends who are his equals.

Near the city of Mahilaropya stood a great banyan, home to many birds and to a crow. One day the crow saw a hunter coming toward the tree with a net. He warned the birds that the rice the man would scatter was poison.

The hunter spread his net, scattered rice and hid. The birds of the banyan kept away. A pigeon king passing with his flock flew down for the rice against the crow’s warning, and all of them were caught.

The pigeon king told his flock to fly up together, net and all. They rose as one and carried the net out of the hunter’s sight. He turned back, having lost even the net that fed his family.

The pigeons flew to the burrow of the king’s friend, a mouse, with the crow following to see what would happen. The king called and the mouse came hurrying out, glad to hear his friend. Then he saw them all bound in the net.

The mouse went to cut the king’s cords first. The king said his followers must be freed before him, since they depended on him. The mouse was delighted with this, cut every cord and sent them home.

The crow had watched the rescue and wanted this mouse for a friend. He stood at the burrow and called. The mouse drew back and told him to go, since a crow is an eater of mice.
