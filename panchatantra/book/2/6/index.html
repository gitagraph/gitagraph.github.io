The rescue

Mitra Samprapti: part 6 of 6

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

As he was speaking, the hunter came up with his bow drawn to the ear. The mouse bit through the snare in an instant. The deer ran, looking back as he went. The crow flew up into a tree and the mouse slipped into a hole nearby. The hunter’s face fell when he saw the deer gone and his work wasted.

Then he noticed the tortoise creeping slowly over the ground. “Fate has taken the deer and given me this tortoise for food,” he thought. “My family will eat today.” He wrapped the tortoise in grass, hung him on his bow, put the bow on his shoulder and set off for home.

Hiranyaka watched his friend carried away and cried out in grief. “Before I reach the end of one sorrow a second is on me. No one trusts a mother, a wife, a brother or a son as he trusts a close friend. Fate took my wealth. Why has it also taken the friend who was my rest on a weary road?

Sanskrit verses 2.183, 2.186

First I lost my wealth, then my followers, then my country, and now my friend. Yet this is how life is for every creature. The body is never far from harm, and all who meet must part. Who made this jewel, this protection when fear comes, this vessel of love and trust, the two syllables of the word friend?”

Sanskrit verses 2.188, 2.190

The deer and the crow came up weeping. “What use is this crying?” said Hiranyaka. “While Mantharaka is still in sight, let us think how to free him. One who only weeps over trouble makes the weeping longer and never reaches its end. The cure for trouble is to set about ending it and to give up despair.”

Sanskrit verses 2.191, 2.192

“Then do as I say,” said the crow. “Let Chitranga go ahead along the hunter’s path and lie down at the edge of a pond as if he were dead. I will sit on his head and peck at it lightly.

The hunter will take him for dead, drop the tortoise and run for the deer. Then you cut the grass that binds Mantharaka, and he can slip into the pond.” “A good plan,” said Chitranga. “Count Mantharaka as free already.”

They did so. The hunter saw the deer lying by a pond near the path, with the crow on him. He was pleased. “That poor deer broke the snare, got this far in his pain and died,” he thought. “The tortoise is tied fast and cannot go anywhere. I will take the deer as well.”

He put the tortoise down on the ground and ran toward the deer. At once Hiranyaka cut the grass to pieces with teeth as hard as diamond. Mantharaka crawled out of it and into the pond. Before the hunter could reach him, Chitranga sprang up and fled with the crow.

The hunter turned back, ashamed and downcast, and found the tortoise gone as well. He sat down where he was and spoke a verse to fate. “You took from me the great deer that was already caught, and the tortoise I had is lost too, by your order. I wander hungry in this forest. Whatever else you have left undone, do it. I can bear that as well.” After much lament of this kind he went home.

Sanskrit verse 2.195

When he was far away, the crow, the tortoise, the deer and the mouse came together again. Full of joy, they embraced one another and felt as if they had been born a second time. They went back to the same lake and lived there happily, passing their days in good talk.

So a sensible person should gather friends, the text says, and deal with them without deceit. Whoever makes friends in this world and is never crooked with them does not meet defeat.

Sanskrit verse 2.196

What this story shows

Four animals with nothing to their name got one another out of the hunter’s hands, each by doing the one thing he could do.

None of the four is strong. The crow can see far, the mouse can cut, the deer can run, and the tortoise can do little more than care. Each rescue in the book works because someone does his one thing at the right moment. The pigeons lift the net together. The mouse cuts the cords. The deer lies still and the crow pecks while the mouse cuts again.

The friendships are made slowly and on purpose. The mouse refuses the crow, his enemy by birth, with every argument he has, and gives way only to patience and good talk. The book also shows what friendship costs. The tortoise comes on foot to a friend in trouble and is the one who gets caught. Between the rescues the friends talk about money. The mouse grieves for his lost hoard, and the tortoise answers that wealth which was kept and never used was not his in the first place. The story ends as the text ends it. The hunter goes home hungry, and the four go back to the lake.

  • No one in this book is rescued alone. Every escape needs at least two friends doing different things.
  • The mouse trusted the crow only after long refusal, and the trust held when it was tested.
  • The friend who came out of love, without a plan, was the one who then had to be saved.

The crow made a plan. The deer lay down by a pond on the hunter’s path as if dead, and the crow perched on him and pecked lightly. The hunter saw them and was pleased.
