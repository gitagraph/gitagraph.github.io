To the lake

Mitra Samprapti: part 3 of 6

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

One day the crow came with tears in his eyes. “Friend Hiranyaka, I have lost heart with this country and I am going elsewhere.” “Why?” asked the mouse.

“The rains have failed and there is famine,” said the crow. “People are hungry and no one puts out even a scrap of offering. In every house snares have been set for birds. I was caught in one myself and got away only because my time had not come. That is why I am leaving, and why I weep.”

“Where will you go?” “In the south, deep in the forest, there is a great lake. A tortoise named Mantharaka lives there, a friend even dearer to me than you. He will give me pieces of fish, and I will pass my days in good talk with him.

I do not want to watch the birds here die in snares. Those who do not have to see their country ruined and their people perish are fortunate, the text says. Nothing is too heavy for the able or too far for the determined, and no land is foreign to the learned.”

Sanskrit verses 2.56, 2.57

“Then I am coming with you,” said Hiranyaka. “I have a great sorrow of my own.” “What sorrow?” “There is much to tell. I will tell all of it when we are there.” “I travel through the air,” said the crow. “How will you come with me?” “If you care for my life, carry me on your back. I have no other way.”

The crow was glad. “Then I am fortunate to have your company there as well. Climb on. I know the eight ways of flying, and I will bring you to the lake in comfort.”

Sanskrit verse 2.59

The mouse climbed on, and the crow flew steadily and brought him to the lake. Mantharaka saw a crow coming with a mouse on its back. He was a careful judge of time and place, so he took it for a stranger and slipped into the water.

The crow set the mouse down in a hollow of a tree on the bank, perched on a branch and called. “Mantharaka, come out. It is your friend Laghupatanaka, come after a long time and longing to see you. Come and embrace me. Sandalwood and camphor and cool snow are not worth a sixteenth part of a friend’s embrace.”

Sanskrit verse 2.60

The tortoise looked more carefully and knew him. He came out of the water with tears of joy. “Come, friend, embrace me. It has been so long that I did not know you, and so I went under the water. One should not join company with someone whose strength, family and ways are unknown.” The crow came down from the tree and they embraced.

Sanskrit verse 2.62

They sat down under the tree and told each other their news. Hiranyaka bowed to the tortoise and sat beside the crow. “This is a mouse named Hiranyaka,” the crow told Mantharaka. “He is my friend, as dear to me as a second life. His good qualities are past counting, like the drops of rain, the stars in the sky and the grains of sand. He has come to you in great weariness of heart.”

Sanskrit verses 2.64, 2.65

“What has made him so weary?” asked Mantharaka. “I asked him,” said the crow, “and he said there was much to tell and that he would tell it here. He has not told me either. Now, friend Hiranyaka, tell both of us what has turned you from the world.” And the mouse began.

The mouse said he had a great sorrow of his own and would come too. He climbed on the crow’s back. The crow flew steadily and carried him to the lake.

The tortoise saw a crow coming with a mouse and slipped under the water. The crow set the mouse in a tree hollow and called out his own name. The tortoise knew him then, came out with tears of joy, and they embraced.

They sat under the tree and the crow presented the mouse as a friend as dear as his own life. The tortoise asked what had made the mouse so weary of the world. The mouse began his story.

When the mouse had told how he lost his hoard, the tortoise spoke. The crow, he said, was a true friend, for he had carried his natural food on his back and not eaten it. He welcomed the mouse to live by the lake as in his own home.
