The deer

Mitra Samprapti: part 5 of 6

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

While they were talking, a deer named Chitranga came running into the lake, driven by hunters. The crow flew up into a tree, the mouse slipped into a clump of reeds and the tortoise went under the water. Then the crow looked again. “Come out, friend Mantharaka. It is only a thirsty deer that has come into the water.”

The tortoise judged the matter with care. “Look how he pants and keeps glancing behind him with wild eyes. Thirst did not bring him here. Hunters have frightened him. A frightened creature breathes hard, looks in every direction and finds no ease anywhere. See whether hunters are following him.”

Sanskrit verse 2.168

“You have guessed rightly,” said Chitranga. “I got away from the hunters’ arrows and reached this place with great trouble. My herd must be dead by now. I have come to you for shelter. Show me somewhere the hunters cannot reach.”

“There are two ways to get free of an enemy,” said Mantharaka. “One is to use the hands and the other is the speed of the feet. Go quickly into the thick forest before they come.” Just then the crow flew back. “The hunters have gone, Mantharaka. They have turned for home loaded with meat. Chitranga, you can come out of the water without fear.”

Sanskrit verse 2.169

So the four became friends. Every day at noon they sat in the shade of the trees by the lake and passed the time happily in good talk and fine sayings. Whoever gathers no store of good sayings, the text says, has nothing to offer when conversation is the feast.

Sanskrit verse 2.171

One day the deer did not come at the hour of their meeting. The others grew anxious. They wondered whether a lion had killed him, or hunters, or whether he had fallen into a fire or a pit, drawn on by new grass.

Those who love someone fear for them even in the garden of their own house, the text says, and far more in a wilderness full of dangers. “Hiranyaka and I are too slow to search for him,” the tortoise said to the crow. “You go and look through the forest, and see if he is alive.”

Sanskrit verse 2.173

The crow had not gone far when he saw Chitranga at the edge of a pond, held fast in a hidden snare. “Friend, what is this?” he cried in grief. At the sight of his friend the deer’s own sorrow rose higher still.

When his tears were over he said, “This is my death, friend. It is good that I have seen you. If a friend is there when life is ending, it is a comfort to both, to the one who goes and the one who lives on. Forgive whatever I said too freely in our talks. Say the same from me to Hiranyaka and Mantharaka.”

Sanskrit verses 2.175, 2.176

“Do not be afraid while you have friends like us,” said the crow. “I will bring Hiranyaka at once. And good people do not lose their heads in trouble. Rare is the one who is not carried away in good times, not cast down in bad times and not afraid in a fight.” He comforted the deer, flew back to the lake and told the others everything. Then he took the mouse on his back and flew to Chitranga again.

Sanskrit verse 2.177

The deer saw the mouse and began to hope. “The wise make true friends to get through trouble,” he said. “No one crosses it without them.” “Friend, you know the books on wise conduct and you are clever,” said Hiranyaka. “How did you fall into a snare?” “This is no time for a debate. Cut the cord on my foot before that hunter comes.”

Sanskrit verse 2.178

The mouse laughed. “Are you afraid of the hunter even now that I am here? I ask because my faith in those books is shaken when someone like you ends up like this.” “When fate takes hold, even good sense is struck down,” said the deer. “What is written on the forehead cannot be wiped away by the cleverest.”

Sanskrit verses 2.179, 2.180

While they talked, Mantharaka came slowly up to the place, his heart burning for his friend. The crow saw him first. “This is bad,” he said. “Is the hunter coming?” asked the mouse. “Never mind the hunter. Mantharaka is coming. If the hunter appears, I can fly, you can slip into a hole and the deer can run. What can a creature of the water do on dry land?”

The tortoise arrived. “You should not have come, friend,” said Hiranyaka. “Go back quickly.” “What could I do?” said Mantharaka. “I could not sit there and bear the fire of a friend’s trouble. Losing those we love and losing wealth can be borne only because friends are there, like a strong medicine. I would sooner give up my life than be parted from friends like you.”

The crow brought word that the hunters had gone home. The deer came out of the water and the four became friends. Every noon they sat in the shade by the lake and passed the time in good talk.

One day the deer did not come. The crow went to look and found him by a pond, held in a hidden snare. The deer thought it was his death and asked his friends to forgive anything he had said too freely.

The crow fetched the mouse on his back to cut the snare. Then the tortoise came slowly up, unable to sit still while a friend was in trouble. The others were alarmed, since he alone could not get away if the hunter came.

The hunter arrived with his bow drawn. The mouse cut the snare, the deer ran, the crow flew up and the mouse slipped into a hole. Only the tortoise was left, creeping over the ground.
