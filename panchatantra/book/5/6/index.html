Fate, company, and the gold-finder goes home

Aparikshit Karak: part 6 of 6

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

The wheel-bearer said, “There is no reason in that. Good and ill come to people as fate decides. Ravana had Trikuta for his fortress, the sea for his moat, demons for his soldiers, wealth from Kubera and a science written by Ushanas, and by the power of fate he was destroyed.

And the blind man, the hunchback and the three-breasted princess all came right by a wrong road, because fate was on their side.” The gold-finder asked how that had been, and the wheel-bearer told him.

The wheel-bearer tells of a blind man, a hunchback and a princess sent abroad.

When he had heard it the gold-finder said, “That is true. When fate is favourable, everything turns out well. Even so, a person should do what good people say. One who goes on in his own way alone is destroyed, as you have been. Those who share one belly and have separate necks, and eat different fruits without agreeing, are destroyed, like the bharunda bird.” The wheel-bearer asked how that had been, and the gold-finder told him.

The gold-finder tells of a bird with one belly and two heads.

The bird with two headsStep inside

When he had heard it the wheel-bearer said, “That is true. Go home, then. But do not go alone. One should not eat good things alone, think over one’s affairs alone, walk a road alone or keep watch alone among the sleeping. Even a poor sort of companion keeps one safe on the road. A crab that went along as company saved a life.” The gold-finder asked how that had been, and the wheel-bearer told him.

Sanskrit verse 5.95

The wheel-bearer tells of a brahmin who took a crab for company.

When he had heard it, the gold-finder took his leave of him and went back toward his own home.

The book ends here, inside the tale the brahmin’s wife was telling. The source does not return to her and her husband, to the judges or to the merchant Manibhadra.

Here ends the fifth book, called Acting Without Examining.

What this story shows

Almost everyone in this book has a fact. Hardly anyone stops to ask what it means.

The book begins with a man who copies what he saw without asking what lay behind it, and it never lets go of that fault. The mother with the mongoose, the scholars with the lion and the friend who walked on past the gold each act on one thing they are sure of. In every case one more question would have been enough.

It is also an odd book in its shape. The tale of the barber opens into the mongoose, the mongoose into the wheel, and the rest is two friends arguing beside the wheel about whether sense or fate decides things. Neither wins, and the book ends inside their talk without coming back out. The telling here follows that shape and says so where the source leaves a gap.

  • Seeing a thing happen is different from knowing why it happened.
  • Stopping at enough is a kind of sense that learning does not supply.
  • The two friends disagree to the end about sense and fate, and the book lets them.
