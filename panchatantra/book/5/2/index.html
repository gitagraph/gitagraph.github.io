The four treasure-seekers and the wheel

Aparikshit Karak: part 2 of 6

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

Inside the mongoose tale, the brahmin’s wife now begins a tale of her own. The book never comes back to her, to the judges or to the merchant, and everything from here to the end is told inside her tale.

The brahmin’s wife said, “Greed should be kept within measure. Too much of it leads to ruin.” Her husband asked what she meant, and she told him this tale.

Sanskrit verse 5.21

In a certain town lived four brahmin’s sons who were friends. They were worn down by poverty and talked it over together. “Poverty is a wretched thing. A forest full of tigers and elephants, with thorns to walk on, grass to sleep on and bark to wear, is better than life among one’s kin without money.

Sanskrit verse 5.22

A poor man’s master dislikes him however well he serves, his kinsmen drop him, his merits go unseen and his friends stop coming. He may be brave, handsome, well-spoken and learned in every science, and without money he gets neither fame nor respect.

Sanskrit verses 5.23, 5.24

He has the same senses, the same name, the same mind and the same voice, and without the warmth of money he is at once a different man. Let us go somewhere and make our fortune.” So they left their country, their town, their friends and their homes and set out.

Sanskrit verse 5.25

In time they came to Avanti. They bathed in the Sipra, bowed to Mahakala and were coming away when they met a yogi named Bhairavananda. They greeted him in the proper way and went with him to his lodge. He asked where they had come from, where they were going and what they wanted.

They said, “We are travellers in search of success. We are going wherever we shall find either wealth or death. That is settled. Riches that are hard to come by are won by the bold, who stake their own bodies on the chance. Fate is strong and past understanding, and human effort is strong too. No pleasure is won here without giving the body up to hardship.

Sanskrit verses 5.27, 5.28, 5.29, 5.31

So tell us some way to wealth, whether by entering a cave, by winning over a spirit, by keeping vigil in a burning ground, or by a magic wick. You are said to have wonderful powers, and we are ready for anything. Only the great can carry out what the great intend, as only the ocean can hold the fire beneath it.”

Sanskrit verse 5.34

Bhairavananda made four magic wicks for them and said, “Go toward the Himalaya. Wherever a wick falls from your hand, there is treasure for certain. Dig in that place, take the treasure and turn back.” They set out. After a time the wick fell from the hand of one of them. He dug there and found the ground full of copper.

“Take as much copper as you like,” he said. The others said, “Fool, this is no use. However much there is of it, it will not end our poverty. Get up and come on.” He said, “You go. I am going no further.” He took all the copper he wanted and turned back, the first to do so.

The three went on. Before long the wick of the one in front fell. He dug and found silver. He was delighted and said, “Take all the silver you want. We need go no further.”

The other two said, “Behind us the ground was copper, and here it is silver. Further on it will surely be gold. This will not end our poverty either. We two are going on.” He took as much silver as he could carry and turned back.

The two went on, and the wick of one of them fell. He dug with joy and found gold. He said to the other, “Take all the gold you want. There is nothing better than gold.”

The other said, “Fool, you understand nothing. First copper, then silver, then gold. After this there must be jewels, and one of those alone would end our poverty. Get up and come on. A heap of gold is only a load to carry.” He said, “You go. I will stay here and wait for you.”

So the fourth went on alone. The summer sun beat down on him, he was tormented by thirst, and he lost the track and wandered to and fro.

At last he saw a man standing on a rise, his body running with blood and a wheel whirling on his head. He hurried up and asked who he was, why he stood there with a wheel on his head, and whether there was water anywhere. As he spoke, the wheel left the man’s head and settled on his own.

He asked what this was. The other said, “It came onto my head in just the same way.” He asked when it would come off, for the pain was great. The other said, “When someone carrying a magic wick, as you were, comes here and speaks to you, it will pass to his head.”

He asked how long the other had stood there. The other asked who was king on earth now, and was told it was the king of the Vatsas. “Then I cannot count the time,” he said. “I came this way with a magic wick, driven by poverty, when Rama was king. I saw a man with a wheel on his head and asked him about it, and this happened.”

The fourth asked how he had found food and water, standing there. The other said, “The god of wealth set this terror here so that no one with such powers would carry off his treasure. Whoever comes is free of hunger, thirst and sleep, and of age and death, and feels only this pain. Now give me leave to go home.” And he went.

The friend who had found the gold grew uneasy when the other was so long in coming. He followed his footprints and came on him in the forest, sitting covered in blood and moaning with pain, with a sharp wheel turning on his head. He went up to him and asked what had happened. “It is the working of fate,” said the other. The gold-finder asked him to explain, and he told him the whole story of the wheel.

Inside that story, a brahmin’s wife tells her husband a tale of her own. Four friends, worn down by poverty, left home to make their fortune.

A yogi gave each of them a magic wick and told them to dig wherever it fell. The first found copper and turned back with it, and the second found silver and did the same.

The third found gold and said nothing could be better. The fourth was sure that jewels lay further on, and went ahead alone while his friend waited.

Lost and thirsty, he came on a man with a wheel turning on his head and asked him about it. At once the wheel passed to his own head, where it would stay until another came to ask. The man who was freed had stood there since the days of Rama.

His friend followed his tracks, found him sitting in pain under the wheel and heard the whole story.
