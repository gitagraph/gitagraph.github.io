The merchant, the golden monk and the barber

Aparikshit Karak: part 1 of 6

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

Here begins the fifth book, called Acting Without Examining. Its first verse says that what has been badly seen, badly understood, badly heard or badly looked into is not to be acted on, as the barber acted in this tale.

Sanskrit verse 5.1

In the southern country there is a city named Pataliputra, and a merchant named Manibhadra lived there. He spent his days on duty, livelihood, pleasure and the search for release, and by a turn of fate he lost his wealth. With his money gone, one slight followed another, and he sank into despair.

One night he lay thinking, “Poverty is a wretched thing. Good character, honesty, patience, courtesy, a pleasant manner and good birth all lose their shine in a man without money. His pride, his self-respect, his knowledge and his good sense all go at once. Even a sharp mind wears away day by day with the endless worry over ghee, salt, oil, rice, clothes and firewood.

Sanskrit verses 5.2, 5.3, 5.4, 5.5

The poor go unnoticed even when they live in plain sight, like bubbles that form and burst on the water. People leave the well-born, the able and the good, and crowd round the rich as round a wishing tree.”

Sanskrit verses 5.7, 5.8

He went on thinking, “I will stop eating and let go of my life. There is no use in this pointless living.” With his mind made up he fell asleep.

In his dream a treasure spirit appeared to him in the form of a Jain monk and said, “Merchant, do not give up hope. I am the Lotus Treasure, which your forefathers earned. I will come to your house in the morning in this same form. Strike me on the head with a club, and I will turn to gold that never runs out.”

He woke in the morning and went over the dream in an agony of doubt. “I cannot tell whether this dream is true or false. It must be false, since I think of nothing but money day and night. A dream that comes to one who is ill, grieving, eaten by worry, lovesick or drunk means nothing.”

Sanskrit verse 5.11

Meanwhile his wife had sent for a barber to wash his feet, and at that moment the monk appeared, just as he had been told. The merchant was overjoyed. He struck him on the head with a stick of wood that lay to hand, and the monk turned to gold and fell to the ground.

The merchant took the gold quietly into the house. He gave the barber money and clothes to content him and said, “Take these, good man, and tell no one what you have seen.”

The barber went home and thought, “So these naked monks turn to gold when they are struck on the head. In the morning I will invite a great many of them and strike them with clubs, and I shall have gold in plenty.” He could hardly get through the night.

At dawn he got up, made ready a heavy club and went to the monks’ house. He walked three times round the image of the Jina, went down on his knees with the hem of his cloth held over his mouth, and recited verses of praise in a loud voice.

Then he went to the chief monk, bowed with his knees and feet on the ground, received his blessing and said respectfully, “Reverend sir, be so good as to come to my house today on your round, with all the monks.”

The source is damaged at this point, and the chief monk’s answer is lost. The barber’s reply shows that the monk had objected.

The barber said, “Reverend sir, I know your rule. But many of your lay followers invite you. And I have just now got ready costly cloths for wrapping books, and money has been put by for the scribes who copy them. Do as the occasion requires.”

Then he went home. He got a club of hard wood ready, set the two leaves of the door in place, and at mid-morning went back and stood at the door of the monks’ house.

The text says the monks passed by the devoted laymen they knew and followed him gladly, and it quotes verses on craving here, which holds even one who has left home and owns nothing, and which stays young when hair, teeth, eyes and ears grow old.

Sanskrit verses 5.15, 5.16

He led them into his house, shut the door quietly and struck them on the head with his club. Some of them died. Others, with their heads broken, began to cry out.

The captain of the city guard heard the screaming and asked what the uproar in the city was, and sent his men to see. They ran to the house and saw the monks fleeing, covered in blood. They asked what had happened, and the monks told them what the barber had done.

The guards bound the barber and took him, with the monks who were left, to the court. The judges asked him why he had done this wicked thing. He said, “I could not help it. I saw the same thing done at the house of the merchant Manibhadra,” and he told them all he had seen.

They sent for the merchant and asked whether he had killed a monk, and he told them the whole story of the monk in his dream. Then the judges said, “Let this wicked barber, who acts without examining, be impaled.” It was done.

The judges said, “What has been badly seen, badly understood, badly heard or badly looked into is not to be acted on, as the barber acted here. Nothing should be done without looking into it first, or regret follows, as it did for the brahmin’s wife with the mongoose.” Manibhadra asked how that had been, and the judges told him.

Sanskrit verse 5.16a

The judges tell of a mother and the mongoose she reared.

A merchant lost his wealth, and with it the regard of everyone round him. One night, sunk in despair, he resolved to stop eating.

In his dream a treasure spirit came to him in the form of a monk. It promised to come to his house in the morning, and said that a blow on the head would turn it to gold.

In the morning the monk came as promised. The merchant did as he had been told, and the monk turned to gold. A barber who was in the house saw it, and was paid to say nothing.

The barber decided that every such monk would turn to gold. He went to the monks’ house, recited praises and invited them all to his home, saying he had cloth and money for their books.

At his house he attacked them, and some died. He was taken before the judges and condemned. The judges told the merchant a story about acting without looking into things.

The judges tell of a mother and the mongoose she reared.
