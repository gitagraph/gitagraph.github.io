The friendship, the wife’s demand and the escape

Labdha Pranash: part 1 of 5

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

Here begins the fourth book, called The Loss of What Was Gained. Its first verse says that one whose wits do not fail when trouble comes gets through danger, like the monkey in the water.

Sanskrit verse 4.1

On the shore of the sea stood a great rose-apple tree that was always in fruit. A monkey named Raktamukha lived in it. One day a crocodile named Karalamukha came up out of the sea and lay down on the soft sand under the tree. Raktamukha said to him, “You have come as a guest. Eat these rose-apples, which are as sweet as nectar. Whether he is dear or hated, a fool or a scholar, one who arrives when the offering is done is a guest, and a guest is a bridge to heaven. Manu said not to ask a guest his school, his clan, his learning or his family. One who honours a guest worn out by a long road reaches the highest state. When a guest leaves a house unhonoured and sighing, that householder’s ancestors and gods turn away with him.”

Sanskrit verses 4.2, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5

With that he gave him rose-apples. The crocodile ate them, enjoyed a long talk with him and went home. After that the two met every day in the shade of the tree and passed the time in talk about all kinds of learning. The crocodile took home the fruit he did not eat and gave it to his wife.

One day she asked him where he found fruit as sweet as nectar. He said, “My dear, I have a great friend, a monkey named Raktamukha, who gives it to me out of affection.” She said, “One who eats such fruit every day must have a heart made of nectar. If you care for your wife, bring me his heart. I will eat it and be free of age and death, and enjoy life with you.” He said, “My dear, do not say that. He is a brother to us, and he is the one who gives us the fruit. He cannot be killed. Give up this wrong wish. A mother bears one brother and speech bears another, and they say the brother born of speech is more than the one born of the same womb.”

Sanskrit verse 4.6

She said, “You have never gone against my word before. So it must be a she-monkey, and it is for love of her that you spend your whole day there. I see you clearly now. You have no glad word for me and give me nothing I ask. At night your sighs come fast and hot. Your arms are slack round my neck and your kisses careless. Some other loved one has my place in your heart, you rogue.” He clasped her feet, drew them onto his lap and spoke to her humbly in her anger. “I have fallen at your feet and made myself your servant. You are as dear to me as life, and there is no cause for this anger.”

Sanskrit verses 4.7, 4.8

She answered him in tears. “Your loved one sits in your heart with all her false charm, and a hundred wishes with her. There is no room for me there, so enough of this show at my feet. If she is not your loved one, kill her as I ask. And if it is a monkey, there is no reason for you to love him. I will say no more. Know that if I do not eat his heart, I have sat down to fast until I die.”

Sanskrit verse 4.9

He saw that she meant it, and he was sick with worry. “It is rightly said,” he thought, “that glue, a fool, a woman and a crab never loosen their one grip, and neither do fish, indigo dye and drink. I do not know what to do, or how he can be killed by me.” So thinking, he went to the monkey. The monkey saw him come late and troubled, and asked why he was so late that day, and why he had no cheerful word and no fine verses.

Sanskrit verse 4.10

The crocodile said, “Friend, your brother’s wife has spoken to me very harshly. She said, ‘Ungrateful one, do not show me your face. You live on your friend every day and have not repaid him even by showing him your house. There is no atonement for you. The good have laid down a penance for one who kills a brahmin, for a drunkard, a thief, a breaker of vows and a cheat, and none for the ungrateful. Bring my brother-in-law home today and repay him, or you will see me next in the other world.’ That is why I have come, and I am late because I was quarrelling with her over you. So come to my house. Your brother’s wife has decorated the courtyard, laid out clothes and jewels and hung garlands at the door, and she is waiting eagerly.”

Sanskrit verse 4.11

The monkey said, “Friend, my brother’s wife is right. A wise person keeps away from a friend who, like a weaver, is always pulling things toward himself. To give and to take, to tell secrets and to ask them, to eat at a friend’s house and to feed him: these six are the marks of affection. But I live in the forest and your house is in the water, so I cannot go there. Bring my brother’s wife here, and I will bow to her and take her blessing.” The crocodile said, “Friend, our house is on a lovely sandbank out in the sea. Climb on my back and come in comfort, with nothing to fear.” The monkey was delighted. “Then there is no reason to wait. Hurry. Here I am on your back.”

Sanskrit verses 4.12, 4.13

When they were out in deep water the monkey grew frightened. “Brother, go slowly,” he said. “The waves are drenching me.” The crocodile thought, “He is in deep water now and in my power. On my back he cannot move the width of a sesame seed. I will tell him what I intend, so that he can call on his god.” He said, “Friend, I won your trust at my wife’s word and have brought you here to kill you. Call on the god you worship.” The monkey said, “Brother, I have done no wrong to her or to you. I do not see why you should plan my death.” The crocodile said, “She has a longing to eat your heart, which is sweet with the juice of that nectar fruit. That is why.”

The monkey’s wits did not fail him. “Good sir,” he said, “you should have told me so on the shore. I always keep my heart well hidden in a hollow of the rose-apple tree. I would gladly give it to my brother’s wife. You have brought me here without it.” The crocodile was pleased. “Then give me your heart, so that this wicked wife of mine can eat it and end her fast. I will take you back to the tree.” He turned round and swam to the foot of the rose-apple. The monkey, muttering vows to many gods, reached the shore, sprang into the tree with one long leap and thought, “My life is saved. It is rightly said that one should not trust the untrustworthy, and should not trust even the trusted too far, for the danger that comes of trust cuts one off at the root. Today is like a second birthday to me.”

The crocodile called up, “Friend, give me the heart, so that your brother’s wife can eat and end her fast.” The monkey laughed and scolded him. “Shame on you, fool and betrayer of trust. No one has two hearts. Get away from under this tree and never come here again. One who tries to make friends again with a friend who has once played false is taking hold of death, like a she-mule that conceives.” The crocodile was abashed. He thought, “I was a great fool to tell him what was in my mind. If he can be brought to trust me again, I will try.” He said, “Friend, it was a joke, to test your feelings. She has no use for your heart. Come to our house as our guest.” The monkey said, “Wicked one, go. I will not come now. A hungry creature will do any wrong, and those who are wasting away have no pity. Tell Priyadarshana, good lady, that Gangadatta does not come back to the well.” The crocodile asked what that meant, and the monkey told him.

Sanskrit verse 4.15

The monkey tells of a frog king who brought a snake into his well.

A monkey lived in a rose-apple tree on the seashore. One day a crocodile came up onto the sand beneath it, and the monkey welcomed him as a guest and gave him fruit.

They met every day, and the crocodile took fruit home to his wife. She said that one who ate such fruit must have a heart of nectar, and asked for the monkey’s heart. When he refused, she said she would fast until she had it.

The crocodile went to the monkey and said his wife wished to welcome him at their home in the sea. The monkey trusted him and climbed onto his back.

In deep water the crocodile told him the truth: his wife wanted the monkey’s heart, and he had been brought out to die.

The monkey kept his wits. He said he had left his heart in a hollow of the tree, and the crocodile swam him back to fetch it. The monkey leapt into the branches and was safe.

The crocodile asked for the heart, and the monkey laughed that no one has two hearts and told him never to come back. The crocodile said it had all been a joke. The monkey answered with a story.

The monkey tells of a frog king who brought a snake into his well.

Gangadatta the frog king
