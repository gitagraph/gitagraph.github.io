The monkey’s counsel and the fight

Labdha Pranash: part 5 of 5

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

When he had heard it, the crocodile said, “No harm comes, in this world or the next, to those who give advice and wish others well. So be kind to me, ungrateful as I am, and advise me. There is no merit in being good to those who have been good to you. The good call that person good who is good to those who have wronged him.”

Sanskrit verses 4.75, 4.76

The monkey said, “Good sir, if that is how it is, go there and fight him. The verse says that if you are killed you will gain heaven, and if you live you will have your home and fame, so that either way the fighter gains.

Sanskrit verse 4.77

And it is said that the great are won by bowing, the brave by division, the low by a small gift and an equal by force.” The crocodile asked how that had been, and the monkey told him.

The monkey tells of a jackal who kept a dead elephant from four rivals.

When he had told it, the monkey said, “So you too must beat this enemy of your own kind in a fight and drive him off. If you do not, he will take root, and he will be the end of you.

Wealth is to be expected from cows, austerity from a brahmin, fickleness from women and danger from one’s own kind. And a foreign land has plenty of food of every kind and easy-going housewives, and one fault, that your own kind is against you.” The crocodile asked how that had been, and the monkey told him.

Sanskrit verse 4.82

The monkey tells of a dog who went abroad in a famine.

Chitranga the dog abroadStep inside

The crocodile heard this advice and made up his mind to fight to the death. He took leave of the monkey and went to his own place. There he fought the intruder who had entered his house, held firm in his courage and killed him. He won back his home and lived there in comfort for a long time.

It is well said that fortune which comes without effort is only the luck of the idle and is worth little, for even a deer eats the grass that fate puts in its way.

Sanskrit verse 4.84

Here ends the fourth book, called The Loss of What Was Gained.

What this story shows

The monkey is saved by one quick answer, and the crocodile is undone by one honest sentence.

The book’s whole action is over in its first part. The crocodile has the monkey on his back in deep water and tells him the plan, and the monkey says his heart is back in the tree. Everything after that is talk: the crocodile asking to be trusted again, and the monkey answering with one story after another about why he will not be.

The monkey’s stories are sharp about wives, and the telling here gives those remarks as his. What the frame itself shows is narrower. The crocodile gives up a friend at someone else’s word, against his own judgment, and loses both the friend and the wife. At the end he is told to go and fight for his own home, and that at least he does.

  • A friendship of many days was thrown away on one demand.
  • The monkey kept his life by thinking quickly, and his safety by never going back.
  • The crocodile ends with his home, and without his wife or his friend.
