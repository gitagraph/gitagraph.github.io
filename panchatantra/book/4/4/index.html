The wife’s death and the lost home

Labdha Pranash: part 4 of 5

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

While the crocodile was still talking with him, a water creature came up and said, “Crocodile, your wife was fasting, and while you stayed away she died.” The words fell on him like a thunderbolt. He cried out in his distress, “What has happened to me, unlucky as I am.

They say a house is not the home, for the wife is the home, and a house without her is no better than a wilderness. Where the loved one sits, even under a tree, that is home, and a palace without her is counted a forest. One who has neither a mother nor a sweet-spoken wife in his house may as well go to the forest, for his house is one.

Sanskrit verses 4.58, 4.59

Friend, forgive me. I have wronged you. Now that I have lost my wife I will go into the fire.”

The monkey said, “Fool, you are grieving when you have cause for joy. When such a wife dies, a festival is in order.” He then recited a string of verses against a quarrelsome wife and against women in general.

The crocodile said, “Friend, let that be. But I do not know what to do. Two misfortunes have come to me. One is the ruin of my home, and the other is the break with a friend like you. Such things come by fate.

As it is said, ‘Whatever cleverness I have shown, you have shown twice as much. You have neither the lover nor the husband.’” The monkey asked how that had been, and the crocodile told him.

The crocodile tells of a woman and a jackal who each lost two things at once.

The source does not tell how the next news reached him. By now a stronger crocodile has taken over his home, and he speaks of it in the lines that follow.

When he had told it, the crocodile lamented, “My friend has become no friend. Next, my dear wife has died. And now my house is held by another. I do not know what more is to come.

Sanskrit verse 4.71

It is rightly said that blows fall again and again on a wound, that hunger flares when the food is gone, and that enmities rise up in hard times. All this comes to people when fate is against them.”

Sanskrit verse 4.72

He wondered what to do. He could fight the intruder, or talk him out of the house with soft words, or try division or a gift. Then he thought of asking his friend the monkey, for one who acts after asking those who wish him well and are worth asking meets no obstacle in anything he does.

Sanskrit verse 4.73

So he asked the monkey in the rose-apple tree once more. “Friend, see how unlucky I am. Now even my house has been shut against me by a stronger crocodile. I have come to ask you what to do. Tell me which of the means, soft words or any of the others, fits this case.”

The monkey said, “Ungrateful wretch, I told you to stay away. I do not see why you follow me again. I will not give a fool like you so much as advice.” The crocodile said, “Friend, I am in the wrong. Remember our old affection and give me good advice.”

The monkey said, “I will not tell you. At your wife’s word you took me out to throw me into the sea, and that was not right. A wife may be dearer than all the world, and still friends and kin are not thrown into the sea at a wife’s word.

Fool, I told you before that your folly would be your ruin. One who is too proud to do what good people tell him comes quickly to ruin, like the camel with the bell.” The crocodile asked how that had been, and the monkey told him.

The monkey tells of a camel who wore a bell and trailed behind the herd.

Then a stronger crocodile took over his home. He had lost his friend, his wife and his house, and he did not know whether to fight, to plead or to bargain.

He went back to the tree and asked the monkey what to do. The monkey said he owed no advice to one who had tried to drown him at a wife’s word, and that pride had kept him from listening before.
