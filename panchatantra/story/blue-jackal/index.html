The blue jackal

Damanaka tells this to the lion Pingalaka, to warn him against keeping a stranger close while his own people are shut out.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

“Whoever casts off his own people and takes in strangers meets his death, like King Kakuddruma,” said Damanaka. “How was that?” asked Pingalaka.

A jackal named Chandarava lived in a forest. One day, driven by hunger and a greedy tongue, he went into a city.

The dogs of the city saw him. They ran at him from every side, barking, and began to tear at him with their sharp teeth. In fear for his life he ran into a dyer's house close by.

A great vat of indigo dye stood ready there. With the dogs upon him, he fell in. When he climbed out he was blue all over.

The dogs no longer knew him for a jackal, and went their ways. Chandarava got far away and set out for the forest. The blue did not come off. Indigo never lets go of what it has taken hold of.

In the forest the lions, tigers, leopards and wolves saw a creature such as they had never seen, the colour of the poison at Shiva's throat. They ran in all directions. “We do not know what he does or how strong he is. Let us get far away.”

Chandarava saw that they were afraid. “Beasts, why do you run at the sight of me? There is nothing to fear. Brahma himself made me today. He said, ‘The beasts have no king, so I anoint you lord of them all, with the name Kakuddruma. Go down to the earth and protect them.’ That is why I have come. All of you are to live in the shade of my royal umbrella. I am Kakuddruma, king in all three worlds.”

The beasts, with the lions and tigers at their head, gathered round him. “Lord, master, command us.”

He made the lion his minister. The tiger was to keep his bedchamber, the leopard his betel box, and the wolf his door. With the jackals, his own kind, he would not exchange a word. Every jackal was taken by the neck and thrown out.

So he ruled. The lions and the others killed game and laid it before him, and he shared it out among them all, as a lord should.

Time passed. One day, as he sat in his court, he heard a pack of jackals howling far away. A thrill ran through his body and his eyes filled with tears of joy. He stood up and began to howl at the top of his voice.

The lions and the others heard that high howl and knew him for a jackal. For a moment they stood with their heads down in shame. Then they said, “This wretched jackal has made fools of us. Kill him.”

He heard it and tried to run. They tore him to pieces where he stood, and he died.

“That is why I say,” Damanaka finished, “whoever casts off his own and takes in strangers meets his death.”

“But what proof is there,” asked Pingalaka, “that the bull means me harm?”

What this story shows

You can change your colour. Your voice will still give you away.

The colour was an accident, and the lie came after. Chandarava saw that the beasts were afraid of what they could not place, and he gave their fear a story: Brahma had sent him. They wanted an explanation and took the first one offered. Nobody tested it. He ruled well enough, sharing out the kill as a lord should.

What he could not do was live with his own kind near him. Any jackal would have known him, so every jackal was thrown out. That left him alone among those who would kill him if they knew. Then a far-off howl reached him, and he answered before he could think. Damanaka tells this to turn the lion against the bull, the stranger taken in. It fits Damanaka himself more closely: a jackal standing beside a king, held up by a story.

  • People who are afraid accept the first explanation they are given.
  • A false position forces you to push away those who know you best.
  • What you are comes out when you stop thinking.

The dogs chased him into a dyer's house, and he fell into a vat of indigo.

He came out blue. In the forest, the beasts ran from a creature they had never seen.

“Brahma has made me your king,” he said. The lion became his minister. The jackals were thrown out.

One day he heard jackals howling far away. He stood up and howled back.

The beasts knew him for a jackal, and tore him to pieces where he stood.
