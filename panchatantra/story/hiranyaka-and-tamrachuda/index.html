The mouse and the monk’s bowl

Hiranyaka the mouse tells this to the crow and the tortoise beside the lake. The tortoise has asked why he lost heart with the world.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

Near the city of Mahilaropya, in the south, stood a shrine of Shiva with a monastery. A wandering monk named Tamrachuda lived there. Each day he begged for food in the city. Whatever was left over he kept in his begging bowl, and at night he hung the bowl on a high peg and went to sleep. In the morning he gave that food to the workers who swept and plastered the shrine.

Hiranyaka the mouse lived close by with a large troop of mice. One day his people came to him. “Master, there is cooked food in the monastery,” they said. “It hangs in a bowl on a peg, out of our reach. Nothing is out of your reach. Take us there tonight and let us eat.”

He went that same night with the whole troop. One leap took him up into the bowl. He passed the food to his followers first and ate last. When everyone was full they went home. After that they came every night. The monk guarded the bowl as well as he could, but each time sleep took him the mouse was in the bowl again.

Then the monk found a cracked bamboo stick. Even half asleep he kept striking the bowl with it. The mouse had to pull back hungry for fear of the blow. So the nights went, the two of them at war until morning.

One day a friend of the monk came to stay. He was a wandering monk too, named Brihatsphig, on his way to the holy places. Tamrachuda rose to greet him and welcomed him with every honour. At night the two lay down on one bed of grass and began to talk of sacred things.

The guest talked, but the host’s mind was on the mouse. He kept rapping the bowl with his stick and gave empty answers. The guest grew angry. “Tamrachuda, I see you are no real friend,” he said. “You do not even care to talk with me. When a host looks away as a guest arrives, that is a house no one should visit. One monastery has come to you and it has made you proud. I am leaving tonight.”

Sanskrit verses 2.67, 2.68, 2.69

Tamrachuda was frightened. “Do not say that. I have no friend like you,” he said. “Hear why my answers were slack. There is a wicked mouse. However high I hang my begging bowl, he leaps into it and eats what is left there. Without that food even the sweeping of the shrine does not get done. So I keep striking the bowl to scare him off. And what a leaper he is. He puts cats and monkeys to shame.”

“Do you know where his hole is?” the guest asked. “Not exactly,” said Tamrachuda. “Then it surely sits over a hoard,” said the guest. “The warmth of that wealth is what makes him leap. Even the warmth of wealth raises a creature’s fire. Nothing happens without a reason. A woman once offered hulled sesame in exchange for unhulled, and there was a reason for that as well.”

Then the guest asked whether the mouse’s path was known. “It is,” said Tamrachuda. “He never comes alone. He comes and goes before my eyes with a troop past counting.” “Is there something to dig with?” asked the guest. “There is this iron spade.” “Then stay up with me until dawn,” said the guest. “We will follow their tracks along the ground.”

The mouse heard all of it and his heart sank. This man spoke with a purpose. One who had guessed the hoard would find the burrow as well. So he led his troop off the usual path and took another.

On the new path a large cat came towards them. It saw the troop and sprang into the middle of it. The mice who were left alive blamed their leader for the bad road. They ran bleeding down the old path, straight into the burrow. Hiranyaka alone went another way. It was like the deer that slips the snare, the net, the fire and the arrows, and then falls into a well.

Sanskrit verse 2.87

The guest saw the drops of blood on the ground. He followed them and came to the burrow.

This edition does not tell the digging. It goes straight to the guest’s next words, spoken when the hoard is already in the monks’ hands. Other tellings show the hoard being dug out with the spade.

“A man is bold, gets the better of others and talks big,” said the guest. “All of that is strength that comes from wealth.” The mouse heard him and it stung. He leapt for the bowl with all his might. He fell short and dropped to the floor.

Sanskrit verse 2.88

The guest laughed. “Look at this wonder,” he said to Tamrachuda. “With wealth everyone is strong. See him now, a mouse like any other mouse. Sleep without fear. What made him leap is in our hands. A snake without fangs, an elephant without its fury, a man without wealth. Each keeps only the name.”

Sanskrit verses 2.89, 2.90

The mouse lay there and thought. He could not jump a finger’s breadth now. The good in a poor man does not shine, he thought, just as things do not show without the sun. One who had wealth and lost it suffers more than one who was always poor. He saw the hoard lying under the monk’s head as a pillow. At dawn, his spirit broken, he went back to the burrow.

Sanskrit verses 2.93, 2.94

In the morning he heard his followers talking as they left. “He cannot fill our bellies any more. Follow him and all you get is cats. There is no reason to serve him.” Not one of them came to stand before him. Poverty, he thought, is another word for death. Even his own kin are ashamed of a man without a coin and hide that they know him, and his friends turn away.

Sanskrit verses 2.99, 2.104, 2.105

He could not bear to lose his wealth and keep only his life. That night he crept back to the monastery. The monk was asleep, and the mouse began to gnaw a hole in the box that held the hoard. The monk woke and struck him on the head with the bamboo. The mouse got away with what life was left in him.

Sanskrit verse 2.109

“What is meant to be mine comes to me,” Hiranyaka told the crow and the tortoise. “Even a god cannot cross that. So I do not grieve and I do not wonder. What is ours does not go to others.”

That was the mouse’s account of his joys and sorrows. They had left him weary of the world. In that state his friend the crow brought him to the lake.

What this story shows

The mouse’s boldness rested on his buried hoard. When the hoard went, the leap went with it, and so did his followers.

The story shows how much of a person’s confidence can rest on what they own. With the hoard under his burrow, the mouse could reach any bowl and feed a whole troop. Once it was taken he could not jump a finger’s breadth, though he was the same mouse.

It also shows how people treat someone who has lost. The followers who had eaten from his leap walked off the next morning and said he was no use to them. The mouse tells all this about himself and hides none of it.

  • Strength that comes from wealth leaves when the wealth leaves.
  • Those who follow for what they get walk away when it stops.
  • The guest asked why the mouse could leap so high, and that question undid the mouse.

The host explained about the mouse who could out-leap cats and monkeys. The guest said the burrow must sit over a hoard, since the warmth of wealth was what made him leap. He asked for a spade and said they would follow the tracks at dawn.

The mouse heard them and led his troop away by another path. A cat met them there and sprang among them. The mice who lived ran back down the old path to the burrow, and Hiranyaka went off alone.

The guest followed the drops of blood to the burrow. This edition does not tell the digging. When the story goes on, the hoard is in the monks’ hands.

The guest said that a man’s boldness is strength that comes from wealth. Stung, the mouse leapt for the bowl with all his might and fell short. The guest laughed and told his host to sleep without fear.
