The monkey and the wedge

Karataka the jackal tells this to Damanaka, who wants to find out why the lion king has stopped at the river and will not drink.

“Friend, what is this to us?” said Karataka. “Whoever meddles in what is not his business goes to his end, like the monkey who pulled out the wedge.”

Near a city, a merchant's son had begun to build a temple in a grove of trees. Every day at noon the builders and the other workmen went into the city to eat.

One of the craftsmen had been splitting a log of anjana wood. It lay split halfway along, held open by a wedge of acacia wood driven into the middle.

One day a troop of monkeys that lived nearby came wandering through. They began to play wherever they pleased, in the treetops, on the temple towers and along the ends of the timber.

One of them, whose death was near, sat down on the half-split log out of sheer restlessness. He took hold of the wedge with both hands and began to pull it out.

The lower part of his body hung down into the split. The wedge slipped from its place.

“What happened to him then, I have already told you,” said Karataka.

“That is why I say: do not meddle in what is not your business. We have food enough from what is left over. So what is this affair to us?”

Damanaka did not agree. Eating, he said, is not all there is to living. After a long argument he went to the lion.

What this story shows

Meddle in work that is not yours, and you may be the one who is caught.

The monkey had no use for the wedge. He was not hungry, and nobody had asked him. He pulled it because it was there and his hands were free. The craftsman knew what the wedge was holding. The monkey did not, and he was sitting in the one place where that mattered.

Karataka tells this to hold Damanaka back, and it does not work. Damanaka hears a story about a foolish monkey and does not see himself in it. People seldom do. The rest of this book follows what comes of his meddling.

  • Before you touch something, find out what it is holding in place.
  • Restlessness alone is a poor reason to act.
  • A warning works only on someone ready to hear it.
