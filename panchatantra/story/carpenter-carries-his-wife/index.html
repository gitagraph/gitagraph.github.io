The carpenter who carried his wife

The monkey tells this to say he will not be talked round after what he has seen.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

This tale is also told in Book 3, by the owl minister Raktaksha, where the carpenter is named Viravara.

In a certain town lived a carpenter whose wife was talked about by the neighbours. He wanted to find out the truth. He told himself that some things are better not looked into, the source of a river, the origin of a great family or of a sage, and a wife’s conduct. Still, he thought, what people say is usually right, for people come to know everything.

Sanskrit verses 4.49a, 4.52

So he told her, “My dear, tomorrow I am going to another village for some days. Prepare food for my journey.” She was delighted, dropped all her other work and cooked him rich food with ghee and sugar.

He left at dawn. She spent the day dressing herself with care, then went to a man she already knew and told him that her husband was away and that he should come when the household was asleep. The carpenter passed the day in the forest. At dusk he came in by the back door and hid under the bed.

The other man came and sat on the bed. The carpenter was furious and thought of killing him at once, or both of them later as they slept. Then he decided to watch what she did and hear what they said. She shut the door quietly and came to the bed. As she climbed onto it her foot touched the carpenter’s body, and she understood that her husband was there to test her.

When the man reached for her she folded her hands and said, “Sir, you must not touch me. I am a faithful wife.” He asked why she had called him, then. She said, “Listen. This morning I went to the temple of the Goddess, and a voice told me that I would be a widow within six months. I asked whether anything could save my husband.

The Goddess said that if I lay beside another man today and embraced him, the early death that hangs over my husband would pass to that man, and my husband would live a hundred years. That is why I asked you here. Do as you wish, for the Goddess’s word cannot fail.” The man, laughing to himself, did as he was invited to.

The foolish carpenter heard every word and was thrilled. He came out from under the bed and said, “Well done, faithful wife. I doubted you because of what wicked people said, and I pretended to go away in order to test you. You have done this to lengthen my life.” He embraced her and lifted her onto his shoulder.

Then he said to the other man, “Sir, my good deeds brought you here, and by your kindness I have gained a hundred years. You climb up too.” The man did not want to, and the carpenter embraced him by force and set him on his shoulder. Then he went dancing to every door in the neighbourhood.

The monkey said to the crocodile, “A fool is soothed with soft words even when the wrong is done before his eyes. I am not that carpenter. I have seen the change in you myself, and I will not go to your house.”

Sanskrit verse 4.49

What this story shows

He heard a story that flattered him and stopped trusting his own eyes.

The carpenter sets the test himself and then throws away its result. What he saw is replaced by what he is told, because what he is told makes him the object of a great sacrifice.

The monkey borrows the tale for one purpose. He has seen what the crocodile meant to do, and he will not let friendly words undo that knowledge.

  • Seeing a thing for oneself is no use if a pleasing explanation is accepted afterward.
  • The carpenter is fooled by a story in which he is the one being cared for.
  • The monkey refuses to be soothed after what he has seen.

A carpenter had heard talk about his wife and wanted to test her. He said he was going to another village for some days, and she gladly cooked him food for the road.

He spent the day in the forest, and at dusk he came in by the back door and hid. The other man arrived. The wife noticed that her husband was there and understood that she was being tested.

So she told the man, in her husband’s hearing, that the Goddess had promised her husband a hundred years of life if she embraced another man that day. She said this was her only reason for calling him.

The carpenter believed every word. He came out, praised her as a faithful wife, lifted her and the other man onto his shoulders and danced through the neighbourhood. The monkey says he is not that carpenter.
