The tortoise and the geese

The sandpiper hen tells this to her mate, who would not move their eggs away from the tide.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

“Whoever will not act on the words of friends who wish him well is lost,” said the sandpiper hen, “like the foolish tortoise who fell from the stick.” “How was that?” asked her mate.

Sanskrit verse 1.344

A tortoise named Kambugriva lived in a lake. He had two friends, the geese Sankata and Vikata, who loved him dearly. Every day they came to the shore, and the three of them talked of the lives of gods and sages. At sunset the geese flew home to their nest.

Then a time came when no rain fell, and little by little the lake dried up. The geese grieved for their friend. “Friend, there is only mud left in this lake. How will you live? Our hearts are troubled for you.”

“Without water there is no living here for me,” said Kambugriva. “Even so, let us think of a way. Courage is not to be dropped in a bad hour. A sailor whose ship has broken up at sea still means to reach the shore.”

Sanskrit verse 1.345

“Bring a strong rope, or a light stick, and find a lake with plenty of water. I will hold the stick in the middle with my teeth. You two take the ends, and carry it and me to that lake.”

“We will do it, friend,” said the geese. “But you must keep a vow of silence the whole way. If you do not, you will fall from the stick.”

So it was done. As they flew, Kambugriva saw a town far below. The townspeople looked up and were amazed. “Look, look. Two birds are carrying something round, like a wheel.”

Kambugriva heard the noise. “What is all this shouting?” he began to say. The words were half out when he fell. The townspeople cut him to pieces.

Other tellings end this story differently. This is how this edition has it.

“That is why I say,” the hen finished, “whoever will not act on the words of friends who wish him well is lost.”

What this story shows

One moment of talk can undo a careful plan.

The plan was the tortoise's own. He thought of the stick, and he understood the one rule that came with it. He was neither careless nor slow. A shout from below, and the wish to know what it was about, were enough. The story shows how a rule you fully accept can still slip when something tugs at your attention, and how little room some rules leave: half a sentence was all it took.

The sandpiper hen tells this to her mate after the sea has taken the eggs. She had warned him about the tide, and he had laughed. The fit is rough. The tortoise agreed with his friends and failed for a moment; the sandpiper never agreed at all. She is angry, and the verse about friends who wish you well is the one she wants him to hear. In this edition the tale ends with the townspeople, and it is told without comment.

  • The rule that keeps you safe is hardest to keep when someone is shouting.
  • Friends who carry you can only do it on their terms.
  • Know which of your rules allow no exceptions at all.

A tortoise named Kambugriva lived in a lake. Two geese were his dear friends.

No rain fell, and the lake dried to mud. “How will you live?” the geese asked.

“I will hold a stick in my teeth,” said the tortoise. “You carry the ends.” “Then keep silent,” said the geese, “or you will fall.”

They flew over a town. “Look,” the people shouted. “Two birds are carrying something like a wheel.”

“What is all this shouting?” he began to say, and fell. The townspeople cut him to pieces.

What this story shows

One moment of talk can undo a careful plan.
