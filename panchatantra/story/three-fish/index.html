The three fish

The sandpiper hen tells this to her mate, after the sea has taken the eggs he would not move.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

“Plan-ahead and Quick-wit both came through and prospered,” said the sandpiper hen. “What-will-be was lost.” “How was that?” asked her mate.

Three fish lived in a pond. Their names told what they were. Anagatavidhata was Plan-ahead. Pratyutpannamati was Quick-wit, who finds a way when the moment comes. Yadbhavishya was What-will-be.

One evening some fishermen passed the pond and stopped to look. “This pool is full of fish, and we have never tried it,” they said. “We have our food for today, and it is already dusk. We will come back here at dawn.”

Their words fell on Plan-ahead like a thunderbolt. He called all the fish together. “You heard what the fishermen said. We must go to some nearby lake, tonight. The weak have two choices before a strong enemy: get away, or get behind a wall. At dawn those men will come and empty this pond. We should not stay here another moment.”

“You are right,” said Quick-wit. “I want the same. Let us go somewhere else. Cowards who are afraid of strange places die where they were born. They say, this is my father's well, and go on drinking its bitter water.”

What-will-be laughed out loud. “This is poor advice from both of you. Are we to leave the lake of our fathers and grandfathers because of something a few men said? If our time is up, death will find us in the other lake too. What fate protects is safe without a guard. What fate strikes down is lost, however well it is guarded. I am not going. You two do as you think best.”

Plan-ahead and Quick-wit saw that his mind was made up. They left that night with their families.

At dawn the fishermen came. They dragged the pond with their nets and left it without a single fish. What-will-be was taken with the rest.

Other tellings have the second fish stay behind and escape by a trick. In this edition he leaves with the first.

“That is why I say,” the hen finished, “Plan-ahead and Quick-wit prosper, and What-will-be is lost.”

What this story shows

Waiting to see what happens is also a choice, and often the costly one.

What-will-be does not talk like a fool. He speaks of fate, of the home of his fathers, of not jumping up at a few words from strangers. Each of these can be true. Added together they come to one thing: he does not have to move. The story shows how easily the wish to stay where you are can pass itself off as calm. Plan-ahead heard the same words and took them as news.

The sandpiper hen tells this after the sea has taken the eggs. She had asked her mate to move the nest, and he had laughed. He sees at once which fish she means, and he does not like it. His answer is to swing the other way and declare war on the sea. The story warns against doing nothing. That does not make every kind of doing wise.

  • When the warning is clear, the time to move is before morning.
  • “It has always been ours” says nothing about whether a place is still safe.
  • Fate is easy to quote when you would rather stay where you are.

Three fish lived in a pond: Plan-ahead, Quick-wit and What-will-be.

At dusk some fishermen stopped at the pond. “We will come back at dawn,” they said.

“We must leave tonight,” said Plan-ahead, and Quick-wit agreed. What-will-be laughed. “If our time is up, death will find us anywhere. I am not going.”

The two left that night with their families. What-will-be stayed.

At dawn the fishermen dragged the pond with their nets. What-will-be was taken with the rest.
