The camel who wore a bell

The crocodile asks for advice, and the monkey answers that he was warned once already.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived a carpenter named Ujjvalaka, who was very poor. He thought, “This poverty in my house is a wretched thing. Everyone else here is busy at his own work, but there is no call for mine. People here have old houses that have stood for generations, and no one needs a carpenter.” So he left the place.

On his way through a forest, in a thicket as dark as a cave, he came at sunset on a she-camel that had strayed from her herd and was in the pains of birth. He took her and her newborn calf and turned back toward home.

At home he tied the she-camel with a rope. Then he took a sharp axe, went to the hillside and cut a great many tender young shoots, which he carried back on his head and set before her. She ate them little by little.

On that food she grew sleek, and her calf grew into a big camel. The carpenter took the milk every day and kept his family on it. He was so fond of the young camel that he tied a large bell round its neck.

Then he thought, “There is no need for any harder trade. Looking after these camels has made my family comfortable.” He said to his wife, “My dear, this is a good business. If you agree, I will borrow some money and go to the Gurjara country to buy young camels. Look after these two carefully until I come back with another she-camel.”

He went, bought a she-camel and brought her home. In time he had so many camels and calves that he made a large herd and hired a herdsman, who was paid one calf a year and had milk to drink every day. The carpenter lived in comfort on his camels.

The young camels went every day to graze in the woods near the town. They ate tender creepers as they pleased, drank at a large lake and came home at their ease in the evening. The first young camel, the one with the bell, was full of himself and always trailed along behind.

The others said, “He has no sense. He strays from the herd and comes along behind us ringing his bell. If he walks into the jaws of some fierce beast, that will be the end of him.”

One day, as he wandered through the forest, a lion heard the bell and came toward it. He saw the herd of camels going home after drinking, and one camel still behind, playing and browsing on the creepers.

When that one came out of the trees and looked round, he could see no path and did not know the way. He walked on slowly, the bell sounding loudly. The lion followed the sound, crouched out of sight and waited. When the camel came near, he sprang, seized him by the neck and killed him.

The monkey said to the crocodile, “One who is too proud to do what good people tell him comes quickly to ruin, like the camel with the bell. I warned you before of where your folly would lead.”

Sanskrit verse 4.74

What this story shows

The bell was a mark of fondness, and it told the lion where to wait.

The camel is not stupid about anything except himself. He is the first, the favourite, the one with the bell, and he takes that to mean the herd’s rules are not for him.

The others say plainly what will happen. The monkey’s use of the tale is short: he gave the crocodile good advice once, pride kept the crocodile from taking it, and he sees no reason to give more.

  • A favour that marks someone out can also expose him.
  • The warning came from his own companions and was ignored out of pride.
  • The monkey declines to advise someone who would not listen before.

A poor carpenter left his town to look for a living. In a forest at sunset he found a she-camel that had just given birth, and he took her and her calf home.

He fed her on tender shoots and kept his family on her milk. He tied a bell round the neck of the young camel he was fond of, and in time he owned a whole herd.

The young camels grazed in the woods each day. The one with the bell was proud and always trailed behind, and the others said that some fierce beast would get him.

One evening he strayed and lost the path, his bell ringing as he went. A lion heard it, followed the sound and lay in wait.

The lion sprang, and that was the end of the camel. The monkey says pride kept the crocodile, too, from taking good advice.
