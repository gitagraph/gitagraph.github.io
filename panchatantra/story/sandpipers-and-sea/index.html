The sandpipers and the sea

Damanaka tells this to the bull Sanjivaka, to talk him out of fighting the lion and into leaving the forest.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

“What war can there be between a servant and his master?” said Damanaka. “Whoever starts a feud without knowing the enemy's strength comes off worse, as the sea did with the sandpiper.” “How was that?” asked the bull.

Sanskrit verse 1.337

A pair of sandpipers lived on a stretch of the seashore. When the season came, the hen was ready to lay. “Dear,” she said to her mate, “my time is near. Think of some safe place where I can lay my eggs.”

“This shore is lovely,” said the sandpiper. “Lay them right here.” “On the day of the full moon the tide comes up over this place,” she said. “It drags away even great elephants. Let us look for somewhere far from the water.”

He laughed. “That is no way to talk, my dear. What is the sea, that it should harm my children? Nobody wakes a sleeping lion, and nobody walks into Death's own house and dares him to do his worst. Lay your eggs here and be easy. A man who leaves his home for fear of being beaten gives his mother nothing to be proud of.”

Sanskrit verses 1.339, 1.340, 1.342

The sea heard him. “What pride in this worm of a bird,” it thought. “They say the sandpiper sleeps with his feet in the air, in case the sky should fall. Everyone carries some greatness of his own invention. I am curious to see what he is worth. What will he do to me if I take his eggs?”

Sanskrit verse 1.343

The eggs were laid. One day, when the hen had gone to find food, the sea came up as if it were only the tide, and carried them off.

She came back and found the place empty. “Fool,” she wept. “I told you the tide would take the eggs. I said we should go far away. You were too proud and too stubborn to listen. Whoever will not act on the words of friends who wish him well is lost, like the foolish tortoise who fell from the stick.”

Sanskrit verse 1.344

“How was that?” asked the sandpiper. And she told him.

“There is another saying,” she went on. “The one who plans ahead and the one who thinks fast both come through. The one who says, what will be will be, is lost.” “How was that?” he asked. And she told him that story too.

“My dear, do you take me for one who says, what will be will be?” said the sandpiper. “Watch what my wit can do. I will dry up this wicked sea with my beak.”

“What quarrel can you have with the sea?” said the hen. “It is no use being angry with it. Anger in someone who has no power only hurts himself. A pot that is heated too far burns its own sides.”

Sanskrit verse 1.353

“Do not say that, my love. Those who have spirit can take on the great, however small they are. The elephant is huge, and a small goad rules him. The dark is wide, and one lamp ends it. Whoever has fire in him is the strong one. Size proves nothing. With this beak I will turn all that water into dry ground.”

Sanskrit verse 1.358

“The Ganga pours into the sea every day with nine hundred rivers behind her, and so does the Indus,” she said. “Eighteen hundred rivers keep it full, and your beak carries a single drop. How will you dry it up? Why say what nobody can believe?”

“Not losing heart is the root of good fortune,” he said. “My beak is like iron. The days and nights are long. Why should the sea not run dry?”

Sanskrit verse 1.359

“If you must go to war with the sea,” she said, “then call the other birds and do it with friends beside you. Many together are hard to beat, even when each one is weak. A rope is twisted out of grass, and it can tie up an elephant.”

Sanskrit verse 1.361

“A sparrow, a woodpecker, a fly and a frog once stood together against an elephant, and the elephant met his end.” “How was that?” he asked. And she told him.

Sanskrit verse 1.362

“So be it,” said the sandpiper. “I will dry up the sea with all my friends around me.” He called the herons, the cranes, the peacocks and the rest. “The sea has wronged me. It has stolen my eggs. Think of a way to dry it up.”

The birds talked it over. “We cannot dry up the sea, so why labour for nothing? A weak one who marches on a towering enemy comes home like an elephant with broken tusks.

Sanskrit verse 1.371

But we have a lord, Garuda. Tell him the whole insult. A wrong done to his own kind will make him angry, and he will settle the score. And if he looks the other way, you are no worse off. It eases the heart to tell one's trouble to a master who has power.”

Sanskrit verse 1.372

So all the birds went to Garuda with long faces and eyes full of tears, and cried out before him. “Injustice. Injustice. You are our protector, and still the sea has carried off the eggs of the sandpiper, who has done no wrong. The race of birds is finished. The sea will kill the rest of us whenever it likes.

A king is family to those who have none and eyes to those who cannot see. A king who does not protect his people carries a share of every wrong done among them.”

Sanskrit verses 1.375, 1.377

Garuda felt their grief as his own, and anger filled him. “These birds are right,” he thought. “Today I will go and dry up that sea.”

While he was thinking this, a messenger came from Vishnu. “Garuda, Lord Narayana has sent me to you. He is going to Amaravati on the business of the gods. Come at once.”

Garuda answered with wounded pride. “What does the Lord want with a poor servant like me? Go and tell him to find someone else to carry him. Give him my respects. A wise servant does not serve a master who does not know his worth. Nothing grows from salt land, however well it is ploughed.”

Sanskrit verse 1.381

“Garuda, you have never spoken of the Lord like this,” said the messenger. “Tell me how he has slighted you.” “The sea is the Lord's own resting place,” said Garuda, “and it has stolen the eggs of our sandpiper. If the Lord does not punish it, I am no servant of his. Tell him that is my decision. Go quickly.”

The messenger carried this back. The Lord understood that Garuda was angry in the way of one who loves. “I will go myself and bring him with honour,” he thought. “A servant who is loyal, able and well born must never be slighted. A king, when he is pleased, gives his servants only wealth. Honour them, and they give him their lives.”

Sanskrit verses 1.382, 1.383

He went at once to Garuda's home at Rukmapura. Garuda saw the Lord at his door. He lowered his face in shame and bowed. “Lord, the sea has grown proud because it shelters you. It carried off my servant's eggs and so insulted me.

Out of regard for you I held back. Otherwise I would have turned it into dry land this very day. Nobody strikes even a dog, for fear of its master. A servant should do nothing that lowers his lord, even if it costs him his life.”

Sanskrit verse 1.384

“You are right,” said the Lord. “When a servant does wrong, the penalty falls on his master, and so does the shame. Come. We will take the eggs back from the sea and do right by the sandpiper. Then we will go on to Amaravati.”

Sanskrit verse 1.385

The Lord rebuked the sea and set a fire arrow to his bow. “Wicked one, give back the sandpiper's eggs, or I will turn you into dry land.”

The sea was afraid, and gave the eggs back. The sandpiper carried them to his wife.

“That is why I say,” Damanaka finished, “whoever starts a feud without knowing the enemy's strength comes off worse. And so a person should never give up trying.”

The bull heard him out, and asked one thing more. “Friend, how am I to know that the lion means me harm? All this time he has looked on me with growing affection. I have never once seen him change. Tell me the sign, so that I can strike to save my own life.”

What this story shows

Before you wrong someone small, find out who stands behind them.

The sandpiper is wrong about almost everything. He will not move the nest, he boasts, and the eggs are lost. His plan to empty the sea with his beak could never have worked. He gets the eggs back because he does not stop, and because in the end he listens: to his wife, who tells him to call his friends, and to the birds, who send him to their lord. The complaint climbs from a small bird to Garuda and from Garuda to Vishnu. At each step someone decides that a wrong done to those beneath him is a wrong done to him. The sea had measured the sandpiper and nobody else.

Damanaka tells this to make the bull run. His point is the first half of the verse: do not take on someone stronger than you. The story he picks says more than he wants. The weak side wins, and his own last line is that a person should never give up trying. The bull does not leave. He asks how to tell when the lion will strike, so that he can strike first. The hen deserves a second look too. She is right at the start and is laughed at. She is right again in the middle, and the eggs come home because of her advice. The verse remembers only the sandpiper.

  • Listen the first time to the one who knows the tide.
  • A cause too big for you may be the right size for your friends.
  • Before you slight someone, count who will take it personally.
