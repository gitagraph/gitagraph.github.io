The iron scale and the mice

Karataka tells this to Damanaka, to say that one who has ruined his master will not spare a friend.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

“If you can bring our master to this,” said Karataka, “what would you not do to someone like me? Keep away from me. Where mice eat an iron scale that weighs a thousand, a hawk may well carry off a boy.” “How was that?” asked Damanaka.

Sanskrit verse 1.442

In a certain town lived a merchant's son named Jirnadhana. He had lost his money and made up his mind to go abroad. “A man who stays on, poor, in the place where he once lived well by his own efforts is the lowest of men,” he thought. “People look down on the one who used to walk proudly there and now speaks humbly.”

Sanskrit verses 1.443, 1.444

In his house was a scale made of a thousand weights of iron, handed down from his forefathers. He left it in the keeping of a merchant of the town and set out.

He travelled abroad for a long time, wherever he pleased. Then he came home and went to the merchant. “Sir, give me back the scale I left with you.”

“Your scale is gone,” said the merchant. “The mice ate it.”

“Then it is no fault of yours, sir, if the mice ate it,” said Jirnadhana. “That is how this world is. Nothing in it lasts. Now I am going to the river to bathe. Send your boy Dhanadeva with me to carry my bathing things.”

The merchant was uneasy about his theft and did not like to refuse. “Child,” he said to his son, “your uncle here is going to the river to bathe. Take his bathing things and go with him.” It is well said that nobody does a kindness out of devotion alone. There is fear behind it, or greed, or some purpose. And where someone is far too gracious for no reason, be on your guard.

Sanskrit verses 1.445, 1.446

The boy took the bathing things and went off happily with the guest. Jirnadhana bathed. Then he put the boy in a cave by the river, closed its mouth with a great rock, and hurried back.

“Guest, where is my boy, who went with you to the river?” asked the merchant. “A hawk carried him off from the bank,” said Jirnadhana.

“Liar. Can a hawk carry off a boy? Give me back my son, or I will go to the king's court.”

“Lover of truth, a hawk does not carry off a boy, and mice do not eat a scale made of a thousand weights of iron. If you want your son, give me my scale.” Quarrelling like this, the two went to the king's court.

There the merchant cried out at the top of his voice. “Injustice. Injustice. This thief has stolen my child.” “Give the merchant his son,” the judges said to Jirnadhana.

“What can I do? A hawk carried the boy off from the river bank, before my own eyes.” “That is not the truth,” they said. “Is a hawk able to carry off a boy?”

“Hear me, sirs,” said Jirnadhana. “Where mice eat an iron scale that weighs a thousand, O king, a hawk may carry off a boy. There is no doubt of it.” “How is that?” they asked.

Sanskrit verse 1.447

Then the whole story was told before the court. The judges laughed. They made the two men see reason, and each was satisfied: one got his scale and the other got his son.

“That is why I say,” Karataka finished, “where mice eat an iron scale, a hawk may carry off a boy.”

“Fool, you did all this because you could not bear to see the bull in favour.”

What this story shows

A lie can be answered in its own coin.

Jirnadhana does not argue with the lie. He agrees with it, politely, and says that nothing in this world lasts. Then he tells a lie of exactly the same shape and waits. The merchant is the one who has to say out loud that such things do not happen, and with that he has answered himself. The court laughs and each man gets back what was his. Nobody is punished, and nobody gains. The story is fond of this. It is also plain that the lever was a child shut in a cave behind a rock, and that the merchant only sent him because his own theft had made him afraid to refuse.

Karataka uses the verse in a different way. Where one impossible thing is done, he says, expect another: a jackal who can bring his king to the edge of death will not spare a friend. So he tells Damanaka to keep away from him. Then he names the cause, which nobody in the book has said so plainly: Damanaka did all this because he could not bear to see the bull in favour.

  • You do not always have to prove a lie false. You can show where it leads.
  • Too much politeness with no reason behind it is worth a second look.
  • A fair ending gives each side back its own and no more.

Jirnadhana left his great iron scale in a merchant's keeping and went abroad.

When he came back, the merchant said, “Your scale is gone. The mice ate it.”

“Then it is nobody's fault,” said Jirnadhana, and asked for the merchant's son to carry his bathing things. At the river he shut the boy in a cave behind a rock.

“A hawk carried your son off,” he told the merchant. “Can a hawk carry off a boy?” “Can mice eat iron?” They went to the king's court.

The court laughed. One man got his scale back, and the other his son.

What this story shows
