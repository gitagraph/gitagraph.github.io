The lion's court and the camel

The bull Sanjivaka tells this to Damanaka, after Damanaka has told him the lion means to kill him.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

“One cannot live among the wicked, even small ones,” said the bull. “They find some way and they kill. Many small clever creatures who live by deceit can make wrong look right, as the crow and the others did to the camel.” “How was that?” asked Damanaka.

In a forest lived a lion named Madotkata. A leopard, a crow and a jackal were his followers. One day, as they wandered, they came on a camel named Krathanaka, who had strayed from his caravan.

“I have never seen such a creature,” said the lion. “Find out whether it belongs to the forest or the village.” “Lord, it is a village animal called a camel,” said the crow. “It is food for you. Kill it.”

“I do not kill one who has come to my house,” said the lion. “Even an enemy who arrives trusting and unafraid must not be harmed. Promise him safety and bring him to me.”

So they all reassured the camel and brought him. He bowed and told how he had lost his caravan. “Krathanaka,” said the lion, “do not go back to the village and the misery of carrying loads. Stay in this forest with me, without fear, and eat the tips of the grass, green as emerald.” The camel agreed, and lived among them in comfort, thinking no harm could come from any side.

Then one day Madotkata fought a great wild elephant. The tusks hurt him badly. He lived, but he was too weak to walk a single step. Without him to hunt, the crow and the others went hungry and suffered.

“Go and find some animal,” said the lion. “Even as I am, I will kill it and feed you.” The four went out and found nothing.

The jackal took the crow aside. “Why wander any further? Krathanaka is here, trusting our lord. Let us kill him and live.” “Well said,” answered the crow. “But our lord has promised him safety.” “I will speak to our lord so that he does the killing himself. Wait here.”

The jackal hurried to the lion. “Lord, we have been through the whole forest and found nothing. We are too hungry to move. And you need the right food to recover. If you give the order, today's meal can be Krathanaka's flesh.”

The lion was furious. “Shame on you, you vile creature. Say that once more and I will kill you on the spot. I gave him my promise of safety. The wise say that no gift, of cows or land or food, is as great as the gift of safety.”

“Lord, to kill him after such a promise would be a fault,” said the jackal. “But if he offers his life to you himself, out of devotion, there is no fault. If he does not, kill one of us. What are our lives for, if they are not given for you? When the head of a house is lost, the house is lost.” “If that is so,” said Madotkata, “do as you think best.”

The jackal ran back to the others. “Our lord is very low. Who will protect us without him? Let us go and offer him our own bodies, and so repay what we owe him.” They all came before the lion with tears in their eyes, bowed and sat down.

“Did you find anything?” asked the lion. “Nothing, lord, though we went everywhere,” said the crow. “So eat me today and live. A servant who gives his life for his lord wins heaven.”

“You are too small,” said the jackal. “Your flesh would not keep our lord alive. But you have shown your devotion, and you will be praised in both worlds. Stand aside.” Then he bowed. “Lord, eat me today.”

“Well spoken,” said the leopard, “but you are small too. And you fight with claws, as our lord does. Your kind is not to be eaten. Stand aside.” Then he bowed. “Lord, let my life keep yours today. Give me a home in heaven and fame on earth.”

The camel thought, “They have all made fine speeches, and our lord has killed none of them. I will say what the moment calls for too, and these three will answer me in the same way.”

“Leopard, that is true,” he said, “but you also fight with claws. How can our lord eat you? Stand aside.” He came forward and bowed. “Lord, you cannot eat these. Keep yourself alive with my life.”

The moment he had said it, the jackal and the leopard tore open his sides, and Krathanaka died. Those small clever creatures ate him, every one of them.

“That is why I say,” the bull finished, “many small clever ones who live by deceit can make wrong look right.”

“So I see it now. This king of yours keeps low company. Someone close to him has turned him against me.”

What this story shows

A promise can be kept in words and broken all the same.

Nobody in this story breaks the lion's promise openly. The jackal finds a way round it. If the camel offers himself, the promise is not broken, so the jackal arranges a scene in which offering yourself looks safe. Three offers are made and three are politely turned down. The camel takes this for a custom of the court and joins in. The others were playing parts that had been agreed. He copied them in good faith, and his was the only offer that was accepted.

The lion comes out of it badly too. He refused with real anger, and then said, do as you think best. That was enough. The bull tells this story to the very jackal who is doing the same thing to him. He has understood the method exactly, and he still cannot see who is using it.

  • Before you copy what others are doing, find out whether it is a performance.
  • “Do as you think best” is also a decision.
  • Knowing how a trick works does not tell you who is playing it.

A lion and his followers, a leopard, a crow and a jackal, found a camel who had lost his caravan.

The lion gave him a promise of safety. “Stay in this forest without fear,” he said.

The lion was wounded by an elephant and could not hunt. The jackal and the crow began to plan.

One by one they offered the lion their lives. Each time, another found a reason to refuse.

The camel thought he would be answered the same way. He stepped forward and offered his life.

The jackal and the leopard tore him open at once.

What this story shows

A promise can be kept in words and broken all the same.

  • Before you copy what others are doing, find out whether it is a performance.
  • “Do as you think best” is also a decision.
  • Knowing how a trick works does not tell you who is playing it.
