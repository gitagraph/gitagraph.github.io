The bird whose droppings were gold

Raktaksha tells this to the owl king and his ministers. The crow Sthirajivi is being fed at their gate and growing strong, and Raktaksha calls them all fools for it.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

On one side of a certain mountain stood a great tree. A bird named Sindhuka lived in it, and gold came forth in his droppings.

One day a hunter came that way. The bird let fall his droppings right in front of him. The hunter saw them turn to gold as they fell, and he was amazed. He thought, “I have been given to snaring birds since I was a child, and eighty years have gone by. Never once have I seen gold in a bird’s droppings.” With that thought he set a snare in the tree.

The foolish bird, suspecting nothing, came and sat where he had sat before, and at once he was caught. The hunter took him out of the snare, put him in a cage and carried him home.

Then he began to think. “I do not know what to do with this dangerous bird. If someone finds out what he is and tells the king, my life will surely be at risk. I had better present the bird to the king myself.” And that is what he did.

The king saw the bird, and his eyes and his face opened like lotuses. He was delighted. “Guards,” he said, “watch over this bird with care. Give him whatever he likes to eat and drink.”

Then a minister said, “This bird is of no use. It has been accepted on nothing more than the word of a hunter, and a hunter’s word is not to be trusted. Gold does not come from the droppings of a bird. Let it be released from the cage.”

On the minister’s word the king set the bird free. He flew up and perched on the high arch of the gate, let fall droppings of gold, and recited this verse: “First I was the fool, next the bird-catcher, then the king and his minister. It is a whole ring of fools.” Then he went off through the sky, as free as he pleased.

Sanskrit verse 3.216

“That is why I say,” Raktaksha finished, “that it is a whole ring of fools.”

What this story shows

Each of them had the bird in hand and let it go, the bird included. It names them all from the top of the gate.

Everyone in the story makes one mistake, and each mistake is different. The bird trusts a place where it has just been seen. The hunter is afraid to keep what he has found. The king takes advice against his own eyes, and the minister rules out what he has not looked at.

Raktaksha begins the list with himself. He has given his king good advice and has failed to make it heard, and he counts that as folly too. The bird’s verse is funny, and it is also a warning to the owls. They are holding something valuable and dangerous, and they are being talked into treating it as nothing.

  • The bird sat down again where it had just shown what it was worth.
  • The hunter gave the bird away out of fear of being found with it.
  • The minister judged by what usually happens and never tested the claim.

A bird lived in a tree on a mountain, and its droppings turned to gold. An old hunter saw it happen and could hardly believe it. He set a snare in the tree.

The bird came back to the same branch and was caught. The hunter put it in a cage and took it home.

He was afraid to keep such a thing, in case the king heard of it, so he presented the bird to the king himself. The king was delighted and ordered it to be well guarded and fed.

A minister said that no bird drops gold and that a hunter’s word was worth nothing. On his advice the king let the bird go.

The bird flew to the top of the gate and let fall droppings of gold. It called them all fools, itself first, and flew away. The owl minister tells this to call his own king and court a ring of fools.
