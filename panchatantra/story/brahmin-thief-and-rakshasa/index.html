The brahmin, the thief and the rakshasa

Vakranasa, the fourth minister, tells this to Arimardana. He too wants the crow spared, and says that enemies who fall out with each other end up helping us.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived a poor brahmin named Drona. What he was given in alms was all his wealth. Fine clothes, ointments, perfumes, garlands, ornaments, betel and such comforts never came his way. His hair, beard, nails and body hair grew long, and cold, heat, wind and rain had dried up his body.

A patron took pity on him and gave him a pair of calves. The brahmin reared them from the time they were small on ghee, oil, fodder and whatever else he could beg, and they grew sleek and strong.

A thief saw them and decided at once to steal the brahmin’s pair of cattle. That night he took a rope to tie them with and set out.

Halfway there he saw someone on the road. The teeth were sharp and set far apart. The bridge of the nose stood high, the eyes bulged and were red at the corners, the bent body was knotted with sinews, the cheeks were sunken, and beard, hair and body were tawny as a well-fed sacrificial fire. The thief was badly frightened and asked who he was.

Then the thief told his own business. “I am a thief, and my work is cruel. I mean to steal a poor brahmin’s pair of cattle.” At this the other, who was a rakshasa, trusted him and said, “Good sir, I eat only once in three days. Tonight I shall eat that very brahmin.”

The two went to the place and stood out of sight, waiting for the right moment. When the brahmin was asleep the rakshasa moved to eat him. The thief saw it and said, “Good sir, this is not fair. Let me take the pair of cattle first. After that you may eat this brahmin.”

The rakshasa said, “The noise of the cattle might wake the brahmin, and then all my trouble would be wasted.” The thief said, “And if anything at all goes wrong when you go to eat him, I shall not be able to take the cattle either. So I take the cattle first, and then you eat the brahmin.” Each kept saying that he should go first, the dispute grew into a quarrel, and the noise of it woke the brahmin.

Then the thief said to him, “Brahmin, this rakshasa wants to eat you.” The rakshasa said, “Brahmin, this thief wants to steal your pair of cattle.”

Hearing this, the brahmin got up and was on his guard. He saved himself from the rakshasa by fixing his mind on the mantra of his chosen deity, and he saved his cattle from the thief with a raised club.

“That is why I say,” Vakranasa finished, “that even enemies do us good when they quarrel with each other. The thief gave the brahmin his life, and the rakshasa gave him his pair of cattle.”

Sanskrit verse 3.190

What this story shows

Each wanted the other to wait. Their quarrel woke the one person both of them wanted asleep.

Drona has almost nothing, and the two calves he reared on begged food are all his wealth. Two dangers come for him on the same night, one for his cattle and one for his life. They travel together easily enough, until the moment comes to settle who goes first.

Neither will wait, each for a sensible reason of his own, and each then gives the other away out of spite. Vakranasa takes from this that a quarrel among enemies is a gift. The brahmin still had to get up and act: a prayer for the one and a raised stick for the other.

  • The thief and the rakshasa agreed on everything except the order.
  • Each gave the other away to the brahmin once the quarrel turned sour.
  • The brahmin met each danger with the means that fitted it.

A poor brahmin, thin and ragged, was given a pair of calves by a kind patron. He reared them on begged food until they were sleek and strong.

A thief saw the cattle and set out one night with a rope to steal them. On the road he met a fearsome figure. It was a rakshasa, on his way to eat that same brahmin.

They went together and hid until the brahmin slept. Then each wanted to go first. The rakshasa feared the cattle would wake the brahmin, and the thief feared losing the cattle if the rakshasa failed.

Their quarrel grew loud and the brahmin woke. The thief told him the rakshasa meant to eat him, and the rakshasa told him the thief meant to steal his cattle.

The brahmin got up on his guard. He kept the rakshasa off with a prayer and the thief off with a raised club. The owl minister tells this to say that enemies who quarrel can end up doing us good.
