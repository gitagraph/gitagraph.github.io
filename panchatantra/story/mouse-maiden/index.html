The mouse who became a maiden

Raktaksha tells this to the other ministers. The crow Sthirajivi has asked for a fire to enter, saying he wants to be reborn as an owl. Raktaksha answers that no one leaves their own kind.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

A sage named Shalankayana, rich in austerities, had gone to bathe in the Ganga. As he stood worshipping the sun, a hawk seized a mouse nearby in its sharp claws. The sage saw it and his heart melted with pity. Crying “Let go, let go,” he threw a piece of stone at the hawk.

The blow stunned the hawk. It lost hold of the mouse and fell to the ground. The mouse, shaking with fear and not knowing what to do, cried “Save me, save me,” and sat down close to the sage’s feet.

When the hawk came to itself it said to the sage, “Sage, it was not right of you to strike me with a stone. It seems you have no fear of doing wrong. Hand that mouse over to me. If you do not, great sin will fall on you.”

The sage answered, “Lowest of birds, the lives of living creatures are to be protected. The wicked are to be punished, the good honoured, teachers revered and the gods praised. You are talking nonsense.”

The hawk said, “Sage, you do not know the fine points of the law. When the Creator made all creatures, he made their food as well. As grain is appointed for you, so mice and the like are appointed for us. It is not right to blame me for wanting my own food.

There is no fault in eating what is appointed as one’s food. The fault lies in eating what is not, and so the order of things should not be upset. What is forbidden to one kind is food for another. To eat one’s proper food is good, and only forbidden food is a great wrong. So you have no ground for punishing me.”

Sanskrit verses 3.200, 3.201, 3.202

“Nor is this the way of sages. Among them, freedom from craving and from enmity is what is praised. The best is one who is the same to enemy and friend, to whom a clod, a stone and gold are alike, and whose mind is even toward well-wishers and strangers, foes and kin, the good and the sinful. A yogi should stay in solitude and keep his mind in check at all times.

Sanskrit verse 3.203

By this act you have fallen from your austerity. As the saying goes, one called ‘Let go, let go’ and fell, a second called ‘Do not let go’ and fell, and the sight of both falling shows that silence serves every purpose.” Shalankayana asked how that had been, and the hawk told him.

The sage heard it and laughed. “Foolish bird, that was the law of the first age. In that age wrong arose even from talk about a wrongdoing, and that is why the two cloths fell, for words spoken out of turn. This is the Kali age. Here everyone is sinful, so wrong does not stick unless the deed is done.

In other ages wrong passed from one being to another. In the Kali age it stains only the one who does it. In the first age it spread through sitting, lying, travelling, meeting and eating together, like a drop of oil on water. So stop this idle talk and go, or I will curse you.”

Sanskrit verse 3.206

When the hawk had gone, the mouse said to the sage, “Holy one, take me to your hermitage. If you do not, some other wicked bird will kill me. I will pass my days there on a handful of the food you give.”

The sage was courteous and kind. He wondered how he could carry a mouse in his hand, for people would laugh. He decided to turn her into a girl and take her that way, and so she became a girl.

When his wife saw the sage coming with a girl, she asked him where the girl had come from. He said, “This is a mouse. She was asking for shelter in fear of a hawk, and I have brought her to your house in the form of a girl. Look after her with care. Later I will make her a mouse again.”

His wife said, “Holy one, do not do that. You are her father by right. The one who begets, the one who performs the initiation, the one who gives learning, the one who gives food and the one who saves from fear are remembered as the five fathers. You have given her life. And I have no child. So she shall be my daughter.”

Sanskrit verse 3.208

So it was done. The girl grew day by day like the crescent moon in the bright fortnight. She served the sage and his wife, and soon she was close to womanhood. Shalankayana saw this and said to his wife, “My dear, this girl is nearly grown. She should not stay on in my house unmarried.

They say the girl chooses the bridegroom, her mother looks for wealth, her father for learning, the relations for good family, and everyone else for the sweets. The wise give a girl in marriage after weighing seven things: family, character, protectors, learning, wealth, looks and age. The rest need not be considered.”

“So, if he pleases her, I will call the blessed Sun and give her to him. A bridegroom the girl does not like should not be given her, however handsome he is, by anyone who wishes her well.” His wife said, “There is nothing wrong in that. Let it be done.”

Sanskrit verse 3.214

The sage called the Sun. By the power of the Vedic verses that summoned him, the Sun came at once and said, “Holy one, tell me quickly why I have been called.” The sage said, “Here is my daughter. If she chooses you, marry her.” He showed the Sun to the girl and asked whether this blessed one, the lamp of the three worlds, pleased her.

She said, “Father, he burns too fiercely. I do not want him. Call someone greater than he is.” The Sun heard this. He knew she was a mouse and had no wish for her, and he said to the sage, “Holy one, the Cloud is greater than I am. When he covers me, even my name is not known.”

The sage called the Cloud and asked the girl whether he pleased her. She said, “He is dark in colour and dull by nature. Give me to someone greater than this.” The sage then asked the Cloud whether anyone was greater than he. The Cloud said, “The Wind is greater than I am. When the Wind strikes me I go off in a thousand pieces.”

The sage called the Wind and asked his daughter whether the Wind seemed the best match for her. She said, “He is strong, but he is restless. Call someone greater still.” The sage asked the Wind whether anyone was greater than he. The Wind said, “The Mountain is greater than I am. Strong as I am, he stops me and holds me.”

The sage called the Mountain, showed him to the girl and asked whether he should give her to him. She said, “Father, he is hard and unbending. Give me to another.” The sage asked the Mountain, king of mountains, whether anyone was greater than he. The Mountain said, “The mice are greater than I am. They bore through my body by force on every side.”

On hearing this the sage called a mouse and showed him to her. “Daughter, here is the king of the mice. If he pleases you, what is fitting shall be done.” She looked at him and knew he was of her own kind. A thrill ran over her whole body, and she said, “Father, make me a mouse and give me to him, so that I can keep house in the way laid down for my own kind.”

The sage made her a mouse again and married her to the mouse, as she wished.

“That is why I say,” Raktaksha finished, “that the mouse passed over the Sun, the Cloud, the Wind and the Mountain as husbands and went back to her own kind. One’s own kind is hard to go beyond.”

Sanskrit verse 3.199

What this story shows

She turned down the Sun, the Cloud, the Wind and the Mountain, each for a fault. At the sight of a mouse she was glad at once.

The story has two halves. In the first a sage and a hawk argue about whose rule applies: the duty to protect a life or a hunter’s right to his food. The hawk argues well, and the sage answers him and keeps the mouse. Neither is made a fool.

In the second half the girl is asked what she wants, and each great suitor honestly names someone greater. The chain ends at a mouse. Raktaksha uses this against Sthirajivi: a crow will stay a crow whatever body he takes. Read by itself, the story is gentler. She was happy among her own, and the sage, who could do anything, let her choose.

  • The sage and his wife took in a creature in danger and raised her as a daughter.
  • Each suitor told the truth about who was stronger than himself.
  • The girl was asked her wish at every step, and it was honoured.

The hawk said mice were its appointed food and a holy man should not take sides. The sage laughed, said that rule belonged to another age and sent the hawk away. The mouse begged to go with him to his hermitage, where no other bird could kill her.

Not wanting to carry a mouse in his hand, the sage turned her into a girl. His wife, who had no child, said the girl should be their daughter.

The girl grew up in their care. When it was time for her to marry, the sage resolved to give her only to one she liked, and he began with the greatest he knew.

He called the Sun and asked his daughter if she would have him. She said he burned too fiercely. The Sun said the Cloud was greater, for the Cloud could cover him.

He called the Cloud. She said he was dark and dull. The Cloud said the Wind was greater, for the Wind scattered him.

He called the Wind. She said he was strong and never still. The Wind said the Mountain was greater, for the Mountain stopped him.

He called the Mountain. She said he was hard and unbending. The Mountain said the mice were greater, for they bored through him on every side.

The sage called a mouse, and at the sight of him she was glad at once. She asked to be made a mouse again and to be given to him, and the sage did as she wished. The owl minister tells this to say that no one leaves their own kind.
