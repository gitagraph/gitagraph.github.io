Dantila and the sweeper

Damanaka tells this to the bull

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a city called Vardhamana there lived a merchant named Dantila. He dealt in goods of every kind and was the leading man of the whole town. He handled the town's business and the king's business too, and both the townspeople and the king were pleased with him. Nobody had seen or heard of anyone so able.

That is rare, the text says. Whoever serves the king's interest comes to be disliked by the people, and whoever serves the people's interest is dropped by the king. It is hard to find someone who can do right by both.

In time Dantila held a wedding in his house. He invited all the townspeople and the king's household, fed them, and honoured them with gifts of cloth. After the wedding he brought the king himself to his home, with the women of the palace, and paid him honour.

Among the guests was Gorambha, a servant who swept the king's house. He sat down in a place that was not meant for him. Dantila had him taken by the neck and put out.

From that day Gorambha could not sleep for the insult. He lay sighing and thinking how to cost the merchant the king's favour. Then he thought, “There is no use in wearing myself thin. I can do nothing to him.” A man who is angry and can do no harm has no shame, the text says. A chickpea may leap in the pan, and it will not crack the pan.

Then one morning, at dawn, the king lay dozing and Gorambha was sweeping near the bed. He said aloud, “The nerve of that Dantila, to embrace the king's own queen.”

The king sat up at once. “Gorambha, is that true? Has Dantila embraced the queen?” “Master,” said Gorambha, “I was up all night gambling, and sleep came over me as I worked. I do not know what I said.”

The king was stung with jealousy. He thought, “This man goes where he likes in my house, and so does Dantila. He must have seen something and let it slip. What a person wants or sees or does by day, he says in his sleep.” He brooded on it for a long time, and thought the worst of the queen.

From that day the king turned his face from Dantila. He was even stopped from entering the palace gate. Dantila could not understand it. He had done no wrong to the king or to anyone near him, even in a dream. “It is well said,” he thought. “Nobody has ever seen or heard of a king who was a friend.”

One day he was held back at the gate, and Gorambha saw it. He laughed and said to the doorkeepers, “Careful, doorkeepers. This is Dantila, the king's favourite, who hands out punishment and reward as he pleases. Stop him and you will be taken by the neck and thrown out, as I was.” Dantila heard it and understood. “This is Gorambha's doing,” he thought. “Even a low-born fool, if he serves the king, is honoured everywhere.”

He went home troubled. That evening he sent for Gorambha, gave him a pair of fine cloths, and said, “Friend, I did not have you put out in anger that day. You had sat down in front of the Brahmins, in a place that was not yours, and so you were shamed. Forgive it.”

To Gorambha the cloths were like the kingdom of heaven. “Merchant, I forgive you,” he said. “And in return for this honour you will see what my wits can do, and you will have the king's favour again.” He went away content. A scoundrel is like the beam of a balance, the text says. A little lifts him and a little brings him down.

The next morning Gorambha was sweeping while the king dozed. He said aloud, “Our king has no sense. He eats cucumbers while he is on the privy.”

The king was astonished. “Gorambha, what nonsense is this? I spare you only because you are a servant of the house. Have you ever seen me do such a thing?” “Master, I was gambling all night, and sleep came over me as I swept. I said something, and I do not know what. Forgive a man who was overcome by sleep.”

The king thought it over. “Never in my life have I eaten a cucumber on the privy. This fool has said an impossible thing about me. Then what he said of Dantila was the same. I did wrong to take that good man's honour from him. Such a thing could not be true of a man like him. And without him the king's business and the town's business have both gone slack.”

So he sent for Dantila, gave him jewels and clothes from his own person, and put him back in his post. “That is why I say,” Damanaka finished, “whoever in his pride fails to honour the king's people, high, middling and low, falls from his place as Dantila did.”

What this story shows

A small man with the king's ear undid a great one, and a small courtesy undid the harm.

Dantila was the ablest man in the city, and it did not protect him. He shamed a sweeper in front of others, and the sweeper happened to work beside the king's bed. One muttered sentence, taken back at once, was enough. The king never asked Dantila anything. He let his jealousy answer for him.

The repair was just as small. A pair of cloths and an apology turned the same sweeper into an ally, and a second muttered sentence showed the king how little the first was worth. Damanaka tells this to the bull as a warning about the king's people. It also describes Damanaka himself, who stands near a king and has been slighted.

  • People close to power can do harm far beyond their rank.
  • A suspicion that is never tested hardens into a verdict.
  • An insult given in public is remembered long after the one who gave it has forgotten.
