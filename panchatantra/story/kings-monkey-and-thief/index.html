The king's monkey, and the thief

Karataka tells these two tales to Damanaka as the fight ends, to say that a fool beside a king is worse than an enemy.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

“Fool, even your help has turned to harm,” said Karataka. “Better a clever enemy than a foolish friend. A monkey killed his king, and a thief saved the Brahmins.” “How was that?” asked Damanaka.

Sanskrit verse 1.450

A certain king had a monkey who was devoted to him. The monkey waited on him in person, went freely even into the inner rooms of the palace, and was trusted completely.

One day the king was asleep and the monkey was fanning him. A fly settled on the king's chest. The monkey drove it off with the fan, and it came back. He drove it off again, and it came back again.

The monkey was restless by nature, and a fool. He lost his temper, picked up a sharp sword and struck at the fly. The fly flew away. The blade split the king's chest in two, and the king died.

“So a king who wants a long life should not keep a fool to attend on him,” said Karataka. “And there is the other tale.”

In a certain city there was a Brahmin who was very learned and, by some turn of an earlier life, a thief. Four Brahmins came to that city from another country to sell their goods. He saw them and thought, “How can I get hold of their money?”

He went to them and quoted fine sayings from the books, and spoke sweetly, until they trusted him. Then he began to serve them. It is well said that brackish water is cool and a rogue is pleasant to talk to.

Sanskrit verse 1.451

While he waited on them, the four sold everything and bought jewels of great value. In front of him they hid the jewels in the flesh of their thighs, and made ready to go home.

The rogue was dismayed. “None of that wealth has come my way. I will go with them, poison them somewhere on the road, and take every jewel.” He wept before them. “Friends, you are leaving me behind, alone. My heart is tied to you. At the very word parting it has no peace. Be kind, and take me along as your companion.”

They were moved, and the five set out together. On the road they passed through a settlement of robbers. The crows there began to call, “Robbers, run, run. Men worth a lakh and a quarter are going by. Kill them and take it.”

The robbers heard the crows and came running. They beat the Brahmins with clubs, stripped them and searched them, and found nothing. “Travellers,” they said, “the crows have never once been wrong. You have wealth on you somewhere. Hand it over, or we will kill every one of you, cut you open and look.”

The thief thought, “When they kill these four and find the jewels, they will kill me as well. Then let me offer myself first. I carry nothing, and it may set the others free. Why fear death? It lets nobody off for being afraid. Today or in a hundred years, it is certain.”

Sanskrit verse 1.452

“Robbers,” he said, “if that is how it is, kill me first and look.” They did. They found nothing on him, and they let the other four go.

“That is why I say,” Karataka finished, “better a clever enemy than a foolish friend.”

While the two jackals were still talking, the fight ended. The bull, torn by the lion's claws, fell to the earth and died.

What this story shows

A devoted fool can do more harm than an enemy with sense.

The two tales pull in opposite directions on purpose. The monkey loved the king, was trusted everywhere, and wanted only to guard his sleep. The thief had planned to poison four men for their jewels. The king died of his servant's care, and the four lived because of a man who meant them harm. What decided each ending was whether the one holding the sword could think. The thief's good deed is told plainly. He reckons that he will be killed either way, and that dying first may at least count for something. The text lets a mixed motive stand and still gives him the credit.

Karataka tells this as his last word to Damanaka: a fool at the king's side is worse than an enemy with sense. It is a fair description of a monkey with a sword. It fits Damanaka less well, who is no fool and knew what he was doing. And while Karataka is still speaking, the bull dies. Five stories of good advice have been told beside a fight that neither jackal stepped in to stop.

  • Loyalty does not make someone safe to trust with a sword.
  • Judge help by what the helper is able to foresee, as well as by how much they care.
  • Good can come from a bad man's reckoning. It is still good for those it saves.

A king had a monkey he trusted completely. It fanned him while he slept.

A fly settled on the king's chest and would not be driven off. The monkey lost his temper and picked up a sword.

He struck at the fly. The fly flew away. The blade came down on the king's chest, and the king died.

The second tale. A learned Brahmin was also a thief. With fine sayings and sweet words he won the trust of four Brahmins, to get at their money.

They turned their goods into jewels and hid them on their bodies. He meant to poison them on the road, so he wept and begged to travel with them.

In a robbers' village the crows called, “Rich men are passing.” The robbers beat and searched the five and found nothing. “The crows are never wrong,” they said. “Hand it over, or we kill you all and cut you open.”

The thief reckoned that they would kill him too, once they found the jewels. Better to die first, with nothing on him, and perhaps free the others. “Kill me first and look,” he said.

They did. They found nothing on him, and let the other four go.

What this story shows

A devoted fool can do more harm than an enemy with sense.

  • Loyalty does not make someone safe to trust with a sword.
  • Judge help by what the helper is able to foresee, as well as by how much they care.
  • Good can come from a bad man's reckoning. It is still good for those it saves.
