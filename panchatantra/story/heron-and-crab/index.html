The heron and the crab

The jackal tells this to the two crows, to show them that the small can bring down the one who preys on them.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a forest there was a large lake, full of water creatures of every kind. A heron lived there. He had grown old and could no longer catch fish.

Thin with hunger, he sat on the bank and wept. His tears fell to the ground like a string of pearls.

A crab came up with some of the other creatures. He was sorry to see it. “Uncle,” he said, “why are you not eating today? You only sit here with wet eyes, sighing.”

“You have seen truly, child,” said the heron. “I have lost all wish to eat fish. I have sat down to fast until I die. Even when fish swim close, I leave them alone.”

The crab asked what had brought him to this. “I was born in this lake and I grew old in it,” said the heron. “Now I have heard that a drought of twelve years is about to begin.” “Who told you so?” asked the crab.

“An astrologer,” said the heron. “Saturn is about to cut through the Cart of Rohini. The learned Varahamihira has said that when that happens, no rain falls on the earth for twelve years.”

Sanskrit verse 1.233

“This lake has little water as it is. It will soon be dry, and all those I grew up with and played with will die. I cannot bear to watch that. Everywhere else the creatures of small waters are being carried to deeper ones by their own kin. The crocodiles and the river dolphins are leaving by themselves. Only here does nobody worry. That is why I weep. Not one of you will be left, even for seed.”

The crab carried these words to the others. The fish, the turtles and the rest came to the heron in fear. “Uncle, is there any way we can be saved?”

“Not far from here there is a deep lake covered with lotuses,” said the heron. “It would not dry up in twenty-four years without rain. If one of you climbs on my back, I will take you there.”

They trusted him. Calling him father, uncle and brother, they pressed round him, each saying, “Me first.”

So the heron carried them off on his back, one at a time. Not far from the lake there was a rock. He threw each one down on it and ate at his leisure. Then he came back and cheered the others with made-up news and messages from the ones who had gone. In this way he ate every day.

One day the crab spoke up. “Uncle, I was the first to speak kindly with you. Why do you take the others and leave me? Save my life today.”

The heron thought, “I am tired of fish. Today this crab will be my relish.” He took the crab on his back and set off for the rock.

From far off the crab saw a hill of bones beside the rock. He knew them for fish bones. “Uncle,” he asked, “how far is this lake? You must be worn out with my weight.”

The heron thought, “This is a dull creature of the water. On land he can do nothing.” He smiled. “Crab, where would there be another lake? This is how I live. Call on your god. I am going to throw you on that rock and eat you too.”

He was still speaking when the crab's two claws closed on his neck, soft and white as a lotus stalk. The heron died.

The crab took the heron's neck and made his slow way back to the lake. The creatures crowded round. “Crab, why have you come back? And where is Uncle? What is keeping him? We are all ready and waiting.”

The crab laughed. “Fools. That liar cheated every one of them. He threw them on a rock near here and ate them. My time had not come, so I saw what that betrayer meant to do. Here is his neck. Be calm now. From today every creature in this water is safe.”

“That is why I say,” the jackal finished, “a heron ate many fish, and then died of his greed in the grip of a crab.”

Sanskrit verse 1.232

What this story shows

Greed that never stops will one day pick the wrong victim.

The heron's lie worked because it was shaped like kindness. He wept, he fasted, he said he could not bear to watch his friends die. The fish did not check any of it. They were frightened, he offered a way out, and each of them asked to go first. Nobody came back, and still nobody asked why. The made-up messages were enough.

The heron was undone by wanting more. He had a full belly and a safe arrangement, and he took the crab for a change of taste. The crab did what the fish had not done. He looked at where he was being taken and believed what he saw. The story shows a liar who stopped being careful, and one passenger who kept his eyes open.

  • When you are frightened, check the helpful stranger twice.
  • If no one who left has come back, find out why.
  • Greed rarely knows when it has had enough.

An old heron sat on the bank of a lake and wept. He could no longer catch fish.

“A drought of twelve years is coming,” he told the creatures of the lake. “I cannot bear to watch you die.”

He offered to carry them to a deeper lake. One at a time, they climbed on his back.

Not far away there was a rock. He threw each one down on it and ate.

One day the crab asked to go. From far off he saw a hill of fish bones beside the rock.

“There is no other lake,” said the heron. The crab's two claws closed on his neck.

The crab went slowly back with the heron's neck. “Every creature in this water is safe now.”
