The weaver who asked for a second head

The wheel-bearer tells this, agreeing that a friend’s advice should be heard.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived a weaver named Mantharaka. One day, as he was working, every wooden part of his loom broke. He took an axe and went to look for wood, and his wandering brought him to the seashore, where he saw a great sissoo tree. He thought, “This is a big tree. If I cut it down I shall have wood for many looms.” He raised his axe.

A spirit who lived in the tree said, “This tree is my home, and you must spare it. I am very comfortable here, with the cool wind off the waves to refresh me.” The weaver said, “I cannot help it. Without wood my family goes hungry. Move somewhere else quickly, for I am going to cut it.”

The spirit said, “I am pleased with you. Ask for whatever you wish, and spare this tree.” The weaver said, “Then I will go home and ask my friend and my wife, and come back. Give it to me then.” The spirit agreed.

The weaver set off home, well pleased. At the edge of the village he met his friend the barber and told him, “Friend, a spirit has granted me a wish. Tell me what to ask for.”

The barber said, “Ask for a kingdom. You will be king and I will be your minister, and we shall both be happy in this world and the next. A king who is always giving wins fame here, and by its power he reaches heaven and rivals the gods.”

Sanskrit verse 5.59

The weaver said, “That is so. Still, I will ask my wife.” The barber said that the books were against taking counsel with a wife, and quoted verses to say so. The weaver said, “I must ask her all the same. She is devoted to me, and I do nothing without asking her.”

He hurried home and said, “My dear, a spirit has granted us a wish today, and I have come to ask you what it should be. My friend the barber says I should ask for a kingdom.” She said, “My lord, barbers have no judgment. Do not do as he says. A sensible man takes no counsel from bards, flatterers, low people, barbers, children or beggars.

Sanskrit verse 5.64

And kingship is one long chain of troubles. With peace and war, marching and camping, alliances and double dealing, it never gives a man a happy hour. From the moment a king is anointed his mind is given over to trouble, for the coronation jars pour out misfortune along with the water.

Sanskrit verse 5.65

Think of Rama’s exile, of the sons of Pandu in the forest, of the end of the Vrishnis and of Nala cast out of his kingdom, and do not wish for a throne. A kingdom is a thing for which brothers and sons wish a king dead. Keep well away from it.”

Sanskrit verses 5.66, 5.67

The weaver said, “You are right. Tell me what to ask for, then.” She said, “You weave one cloth every day, and it pays for all we need. Ask for a second pair of arms and a second head, so that you can weave two cloths at once, one in front and one behind.

The price of one will keep the house as before, and with the price of the other you can do something more. You will live in comfort, well thought of among your own people, and gain both worlds.” He was delighted. “Well said, my faithful wife. That is what I will do. My mind is made up.”

He went back to the spirit and said, “If you will give me what I want, give me a second pair of arms and a second head.” At once he had two heads and four arms. He set off home in high spirits. The people took him for a demon, struck him with sticks and stones, and he died.

The wheel-bearer said to the gold-finder, “That is true. One who has no sense of his own and does not do what a friend tells him comes to ruin, like the weaver Mantharaka.”

Sanskrit verse 5.58

What this story shows

He was offered anything and never once asked himself what he wanted.

The weaver gets two pieces of advice and has no idea of his own. One friend wants to be a minister. His wife wants the household to run a little better. Each answer fits the person who gives it.

The verse blames him on two counts, having no judgment and not heeding his friend. The barber quotes verses against listening to wives, and the wife has her own against listening to barbers; those are their views. What the tale shows is a man who could have had anything and chose a way to do twice the same work.

  • Each adviser asked for what suited the adviser.
  • The wish he chose made him look like a monster to his own neighbours.
  • The fault the verse names first is having no judgment of his own.

A weaver whose loom had broken went to cut down a great tree by the sea. A spirit who lived in it offered him any wish if he would spare the tree, and the weaver went home to ask what to wish for.

His friend the barber told him to ask for a kingdom. His wife said kingship was nothing but trouble, and that a second head and pair of arms would let him weave two cloths a day. He took her advice.

The spirit granted it, and he set off home with two heads and four arms.

The villagers took him for a demon and killed him. The wheel-bearer says that one with no judgment of his own, who will not heed a friend, comes to ruin.
