The carpenter who hid under the bed

Raktaksha tells this to the other ministers once the owl king has decided to spare the crow. He says they have been soothed by soft words while the harm is done in front of them.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

In a certain town lived a carpenter named Viravara. His wife was called Kamadamani. She was unfaithful to him, and people talked about her. The carpenter made up his mind to test her. He knew of her conduct only from what people said, and it is said that what is neither seen nor heard in the Vedas and the learned books is still known to the world, wherever under the sky it happens.

Sanskrit verse 3.195

Having settled this, he said to his wife, “My dear, in the morning I am going to another village. I shall be there some days. Make me some food fit for the journey.” She was glad to hear it. In her eagerness she left all her other work and cooked a meal rich in ghee and sugar.

At dawn the carpenter got up and left the house. When she knew he had gone, she dressed and adorned herself with a smiling face and somehow got through the day. Then she went to the house of a man she had known before and said, “That wretched husband of mine has gone to another village. Come to our house when people are asleep.”

The carpenter spent the day in the forest. At dusk he came into his own house by the back door and hid quietly under the bed. Meanwhile the man, whom the text calls Devadatta, arrived and sat down on the bed.

The carpenter saw him and was filled with rage. He wondered whether to get up and kill him then, or to kill the two of them later as they slept. Then he decided to wait, to see first what his wife would do and to hear what she said to the man.

Just then she shut the door of the house quietly and got onto the bed. As she did, her foot touched the carpenter’s body. She thought, “This must be that wretched carpenter, testing me. I shall have to show some cleverness.”

While she was thinking, Devadatta reached out for her. She folded her hands and said, “Noble sir, you must not touch my body. I am a faithful and most virtuous wife. If you do, I shall curse you and you will be burnt to ash.” He asked why, in that case, she had sent for him.

“Listen closely,” she said. “This morning at dawn I went to the temple of Chandika to see the goddess. Suddenly a voice spoke from the sky: ‘Daughter, there is nothing I can do. You are devoted to me, yet by the decree of fate you will be a widow within six months.’

I said, ‘Goddess, you know of the danger, so you must know its cure. Tell me if there is some way for my husband to live a hundred years.’ She said, ‘Child, there is a way and there is none, for the cure lies with you.’”

“I said, ‘Goddess, if it can be done even with my life, command me and I will do it.’ The goddess said, ‘If today you get onto one bed with a man who is not your husband and embrace him, the early death that hangs over your husband will pass to that man, and your husband will live another hundred years.’

That is why I asked you here. Do whatever you mean to do. The word of a goddess does not fail, that is certain.” Devadatta laughed to himself and did as he was asked.

The foolish carpenter heard her words and thrilled with joy. He came out from under the bed and said, “Well done, faithful wife. Well done, joy of the family. Wicked people’s talk had filled my heart with doubt. To test you I pretended to go to another village and lay hidden under this bed.

Come, embrace me. You are first among wives devoted to their husbands, for you kept your holy vow even in another man’s company. You did this to lengthen my life and turn away my early death.” Saying this, he embraced her lovingly.

He lifted her onto his shoulder and said to Devadatta, “Noble sir, it is by my good deeds that you came here. By your kindness I have gained a life of a hundred years. You too must embrace me and climb onto my shoulder.” Devadatta did not want to, and the carpenter embraced him all the same and set him on his shoulder by force.

Then he danced, saying, “Foremost of those who keep the holy vow, you too have done me a kindness,” and more of the same. He set them down from his shoulder, and wherever he went, round the doors of his relations’ houses, he praised the virtues of them both.

“That is why I say,” Raktaksha finished, “that a fool is calmed by soft words even when the wrong is done before his eyes. The carpenter carried his own wife and her lover on his head.”

Sanskrit verse 3.193

What this story shows

The carpenter saw everything he had feared, and then let a story talk him out of it.

The carpenter sets his test with care, and it works. He sees the man arrive and hears his wife send for him. Everything after that rests on his wanting to be wrong. Her story is thin, and he takes it up with joy and carries it from door to door.

Raktaksha tells it in anger. The owls have the crows’ minister in their hands, and they are being won over by his own account of himself. The story is not kind to anyone in it. What Raktaksha takes from it is narrow and sharp: what one has seen should weigh more than what one is told afterwards.

  • The carpenter’s plan worked, and he saw what he came to see.
  • His wife’s story succeeded because he wished it to be true.
  • Raktaksha’s warning is to trust what one has seen over a pleasing account of it.

A carpenter heard people talk about his wife and decided to test her. He told her he was going to another village for some days.

He spent the day in the forest, came home by the back door at dusk and hid under the bed. His wife had sent for another man, and the carpenter saw him arrive.

Her foot touched her husband under the bed, and she knew he was there. So she told the man a story for her husband to hear. A goddess, she said, had warned her that her husband would die young unless she embraced another man that day.

The foolish carpenter believed every word. He came out from under the bed, called his wife the most faithful of women and thanked the man for saving his life.

He lifted them both onto his shoulders and danced from door to door, praising the two of them. The owl minister tells this to say that a fool is calmed by soft words even when the wrong is done before his eyes.
