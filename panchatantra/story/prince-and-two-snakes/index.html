The prince, the princess and the two snakes

Prakarakarna, the fifth minister, tells this to Arimardana. He holds that the crow should live, because those who protect one another come through and those who expose one another are lost.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain city there was a king named Devashakti. A snake had made its home in his son’s belly, as if in an anthill, and day by day the prince wasted away in every limb. Good physicians treated him in many ways with the medicines the best books prescribe, and he did not recover. In despair the prince left for another country.

There, in a certain city, he lived by begging and spent his days in a great temple.

The king of that city was named Bali, and he had two daughters, both grown. Every day at sunrise they came to their father’s feet and bowed. One of them would say, “Victory to you, great king, by whose favour every happiness is had.” The other would say, “Great king, enjoy what has been allotted to you.”

Hearing this, the king grew angry. “Ministers,” he said, “give this ill-spoken girl to some stranger from abroad. Then she can enjoy what has been allotted to her.”

The ministers agreed, and they gave the girl, with a few attendants, to the prince who was living in the temple. She accepted him gladly as her husband and honoured him as she would a god. Then she took him with her and went to another country.

On the outskirts of a distant town, on the bank of a tank, she left the prince to look after their camp and went with her attendants to buy ghee, oil, salt, rice and other things.

When she came back from the market, the prince was asleep with his head resting on an anthill. A snake had put its hood out of his mouth and was taking the air. From that same anthill another snake had come out and was doing the same.

The two saw each other and their eyes went red with anger. The snake of the anthill said, “You wretch, you torment this prince, who is handsome in every limb.” The snake in the mouth said, “And you, wretch, are fouling the two jars full of gold inside this anthill.” So each laid bare the other’s secret.

The snake of the anthill spoke again. “Wretch, it seems nobody knows the cure for you. A drink of old boiled sour gruel with black mustard, and you would perish.” The snake of the belly said, “And it seems nobody knows the cure for you. Hot oil, or very hot water, and you would perish.”

The princess was standing hidden behind a bush. She heard all their talk, full of secrets as it was, and did exactly what they had said. She made her husband whole and well and gained a great treasure, and then she set out for her own country. Her father, her mother and her people received her with honour. She had the good that was allotted to her, and she lived in happiness.

“That is why I say,” Prakarakarna finished, “that creatures who do not guard one another’s secrets perish, like the snake of the anthill and the snake of the belly.”

Sanskrit verse 3.191

What this story shows

Each snake knew what would destroy the other, and said it aloud in anger. The princess was listening.

Two people who have been cast out meet in this story. The prince left home sick and without hope. The princess was given away for saying that everyone lives by what is allotted to them. She takes the husband she is given gladly, and the story ends by saying she received exactly her allotted share.

The turn comes from the snakes. Nobody could reach either of them while each kept quiet. Their anger at the sight of each other makes them trade secrets, and a listener behind a bush needs nothing more. Prakarakarna’s point to the owls is to guard one another’s weak places. The princess, for her part, simply paid attention and acted.

  • The second daughter told her father a plain truth and was punished for it.
  • The snakes were safe until they began to shame each other.
  • The princess listened, remembered and did what was needed.

A king’s son was wasting away because a snake had made its home in his belly, and no physician could cure him. In despair he went to another country, where he lived on alms in a great temple.

The king of that city had two daughters. One praised him every morning as the giver of all happiness. The other said only that he should enjoy what was allotted to him, and it made him angry.

He had the second daughter married to the beggar prince, to live on her own allotted share. She took him gladly as her husband and set out with him for another land.

One day she came back from the market and found him asleep with his head on an anthill. A snake had put its head out of his mouth, and another had come out of the anthill. The two quarrelled, and each said aloud what would destroy the other.

The princess, hidden behind a bush, heard it all and did exactly as they had said. Both snakes were destroyed. Her husband was whole and well, and the jars of gold from the anthill were hers.

She went home with her husband, and her father, mother and people received her with honour. She had exactly what was allotted to her. The owl minister tells this to say that those who give away one another’s secrets are lost.
