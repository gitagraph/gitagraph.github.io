The jackal, the lion and the young camel

Damanaka tells this to Karataka

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain forest lived a lion named Vajradamshtra. Two servants followed him everywhere: a jackal named Chaturaka and a wolf named Kravyamukha.

One day the lion came upon a camel in a thicket. She had strayed from her herd and was about to give birth. He killed her, and when he tore her open a small calf came out alive. The lion and his servants ate their fill of the mother.

The calf he took home, out of affection. “Little one,” he said, “you have nothing to fear from me or from anyone else. Go where you like in this forest. Your ears stand up like spikes, so your name is Shankukarna.”

So the four of them lived together and passed their days in talk. Shankukarna grew up, and he never left the lion's side for a moment.

Then the lion fought a wild elephant in rut. Its tusks wounded him so badly that he could not walk. He grew weak with hunger. “Find me some animal,” he told the three. “Even as I am, I can kill it, and that will end your hunger and mine.”

They searched the forest until dusk and found nothing. Chaturaka thought, “If Shankukarna were killed, we would all eat for days. But the master will not kill him. He is a friend, and he lives under the master's protection. I will have to bring the master round by my wits.”

He went to the young camel. “Shankukarna, the master is wasting away for want of food. If we lose him, we are all lost. I have something to ask of you for his sake.” “Tell me quickly,” said Shankukarna. “Whatever it is, I will do it without a second thought. To do my master good is worth a hundred good deeds.”

“Then give him your body, on loan,” said Chaturaka. “You will get it back doubled, a body twice the size, and the master will live.” “If that is how it is,” said Shankukarna, “tell him I offer it. Let the master's need be met. Only let the god of justice stand as surety for the bargain.”

They all went to the lion. “Lord, we found no animal, and the sun has set,” said Chaturaka. “Shankukarna here offers you his body, to be repaid to him twice over, with the god of justice as surety, if you will promise it.” “If that is so, very good,” said the lion. “Let the god of justice be surety for the bargain.”

As soon as he had spoken, the wolf and the jackal tore open the camel's sides, and Shankukarna died.

The lion said to Chaturaka, “I am going to the river to bathe and say my prayers. Keep careful watch here until I come back.” And he went.

Now Chaturaka wanted the whole camel for himself. He said to the wolf, “Kravyamukha, you are starving. Eat some of this meat before the master returns. I will see that he does not blame you.” The wolf had taken only a few mouthfuls when Chaturaka said, “The master is coming. Leave it and stand well back, so he does not guess.”

The lion came back and saw that the camel's heart was gone. He scowled. “Who has dared to eat from this before me? I will kill him too.”

Kravyamukha looked at Chaturaka. Chaturaka laughed. “You ate the meat without a thought for me, and now you look to me. Enjoy the fruit of what you have done.” The wolf feared for his life and ran off to a far country.

Just then a caravan of loaded camels came along the road. The camel at its head wore a great bell on its neck. The lion heard the sound from far away. “Go and see what that dreadful noise is,” he said to the jackal. “I have never heard anything like it.”

Chaturaka went a little way into the trees and came hurrying back. “Master, run. Run, if you can.” “Why do you frighten me? What is it?”

“It is the god of justice, and he is angry with you. He says, ‘This lion killed my camel before its time. I will take from him a thousand camels for the one.’ He has hung a bell on the leader's neck, and he comes with the dead camel's fathers and grandfathers to collect the debt.”

The lion looked into the distance, left the dead camel where it lay, and ran for his life. And Chaturaka ate the camel's meat, a little at a time, for many days. “That is why I say it,” said Damanaka. “A clever one gains his end at another's cost and is never found out.”

Sanskrit verse 1.399

What this story shows

The young camel died of his own loyalty, and the jackal counted on it.

Shankukarna owes the lion everything he knows. The lion killed his mother and then raised him, and the calf grew up grateful. When the jackal asks for his body, he agrees at once and asks only that the god of justice witness the bargain. The lion, who promised him safety, lets the promise go as soon as it is dressed up as a loan. Nobody in the story forces anyone. Each step is agreed to.

Then the jackal turns on the other two. The wolf is tempted into a small theft and frightened away with it. The lion is frightened away with the very oath he swore, when a caravan bell is made to sound like the god coming to collect. This is the second camel in this book to be talked into dying for a lion. Damanaka tells the story to Karataka as proof that such plans work, at the moment he is describing his own plan to have the bull killed and eaten.

  • Loyalty is easiest to exploit in those who feel they owe the most.
  • A promise can be undone by renaming what is being asked.
  • Someone who cheats one partner will cheat the others next.

A lion named Vajradamshtra lived in a forest with two servants: the jackal Chaturaka and the wolf Kravyamukha.

The lion killed a camel that was about to give birth, and her calf came out alive. Out of affection he took the calf home, promised him safety, and named him Shankukarna.

The calf grew up at the lion's side. Then a wild elephant wounded the lion so badly that he could not hunt. The servants searched all day and found no food.

Chaturaka wanted the camel killed, and knew the lion would not break his promise. So he asked Shankukarna to lend the master his body, to be repaid twice over. The camel agreed at once, and asked only that the god of justice be surety.

The lion accepted the bargain, and so felt free of his promise. The wolf and the jackal tore the camel open, and he died.

The lion went to the river to bathe and left Chaturaka on guard. Chaturaka wanted the whole camel for himself. He urged the hungry wolf to eat a little, and promised to clear him with the master.

The lion came back, saw the heart gone and swore to kill whoever had eaten first. The wolf looked to Chaturaka, who laughed at him. The wolf ran for his life.

A caravan passed with a bell on its lead camel. Chaturaka told the lion it was the god of justice, come to collect a thousand camels for the one. The lion fled, and Chaturaka ate the camel alone for many days.
