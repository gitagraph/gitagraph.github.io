The two clever fish and the frog

The wheel-bearer tells this to answer his friend, who has just praised sense above learning.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain pond lived two fish named Shatabuddhi and Sahasrabuddhi, which is to say Hundred-wit and Thousand-wit. A frog named Ekabuddhi, One-wit, became their friend. The three used to sit at the water’s edge enjoying good talk and then go back into the water.

One day, as they sat talking, some fishermen came to the pond. They looked it over and said to one another, “This pool is full of fish and the water is low. We will come back in the morning.” Then they went home.

The fish were downcast and took counsel together. The frog said, “Shatabuddhi, you heard what the fishermen said. Tell us today what is best, to run or to stand fast.”

Sahasrabuddhi laughed. “Friend, do not be afraid. There is no need to fear at the mere sound of words. The plans of snakes, of rogues and of all who mean harm come to nothing, and that is why the world goes on. They may not come at all. If they do, I will save you and myself by my wits, for I know many ways of moving through water.”

Sanskrit verse 5.44

Shatabuddhi said, “You have spoken well. You are Thousand-wit indeed. Nothing is out of reach of the wit of the wise. By wit Chanakya brought down the Nandas, sword in hand as they were. Where the wind cannot go and the rays of the sun cannot go, the wit of the wise goes quickly.

Sanskrit verses 5.45, 5.46

We cannot leave the place where we were born, which has come down to us from our fathers, for the sake of a few words. Heaven itself has no happiness like that of one’s birthplace, poor as it may be. We must not go. I will save you by my wits.”

Sanskrit verse 5.47

The frog said, “Friends, I have only one wit, and it tells me to run. I am going to another pond this very day, and my wife with me.” And that night he went.

In the morning the fishermen came and took creatures of every kind, fish, turtles, frogs and crabs. Shatabuddhi and Sahasrabuddhi fled with their wives and kept themselves safe for a long time by twisting and turning as they knew how. In the end they fell into the net and were killed.

In the afternoon the fishermen set off home, well pleased. One of them carried Shatabuddhi on his shoulder because he was heavy, and Sahasrabuddhi was carried dangling. The frog had come up to the edge of his pool and saw them go by. He said to his wife, “Look, my dear. Hundred-wit rides on a head and Thousand-wit hangs down. I, One-wit, play in clear water.”

Sanskrit verse 5.48

The wheel-bearer said to the gold-finder, “You say that sense is better than learning. My view is that sense is not the whole answer either.”

What this story shows

The frog’s one wit was to know what he could not do.

The fish are not stupid. They really are clever swimmers, and they last a long time among the nets. Their cleverness is what keeps them there, because they are sure it will be enough.

The wheel-bearer tells this against his friend’s argument. If learning without sense fails, he says, sense can fail too. The two friends go on disagreeing for the rest of the book, and the stories take turns.

  • Confidence in one’s own skill kept the fish where the danger was.
  • The frog had one plan, the plainest one, and used it at once.
  • The teller’s point is that cleverness is no guarantee either.

Two fish named Hundred-wit and Thousand-wit lived in a pond with their friend the frog One-wit. One day fishermen looked the pond over and said they would come back in the morning.

The frog asked whether to run or stay. The two fish said their many tricks would save them, and that they would not leave their birthplace at the sound of a few words. The frog said he had only one wit, which told him to run, and he left that night with his wife.

In the morning the fishermen netted the pond. The two fish dodged for a long time and were caught in the end. The frog watched from his new pool as they were carried past.

He told his wife that Hundred-wit and Thousand-wit were being carried off while One-wit played in clear water. The wheel-bearer says that sense is not the whole answer either.
