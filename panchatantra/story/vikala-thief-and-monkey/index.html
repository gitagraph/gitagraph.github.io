The demon, the thief and the monkey

The gold-finder tells this to explain why he will not stay beside the wheel.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain city lived a king named Bhadrasena, who had a daughter named Ratnavati, a girl with every mark of good fortune. A demon wanted to carry her off. He came every night and troubled her, but she was guarded by charms and he could not take her away. At the hour when he was near she would shake and feel ill.

One midnight the demon was standing in a corner of her room when the princess said to her friend, “See, this Vikala torments me every night at this hour. I wish there were some way to keep the wretch off.” She meant the evil hour itself, which is called vikala.

The demon thought, “So there is another like me, named Vikala, who comes every night to carry her off and cannot do it either. I will take the form of a horse and stand among the horses, and see what he looks like and how strong he is.” He did so.

That same night a horse thief got into the palace. He looked over all the horses, judged the demon to be the best of them and mounted him. The demon thought, “This must be Vikala. He has taken me for a thief and has come to kill me in his anger.”

While he was wondering what to do, the thief put a bit in his mouth and struck him with the whip, and the demon bolted in terror. When they had gone a long way the thief pulled on the bit to stop him, and the horse only went faster.

The thief thought, “No horse ignores the bit like this. It must be a demon in the shape of a horse. If I see a patch of sandy ground I will throw myself off. I shall not live otherwise.”

As he called on his god, the horse passed under a banyan tree. The thief caught hold of a hanging root and clung to it. So the two were parted, and each was overjoyed to find himself still alive.

A monkey who was a friend of the demon lived in that banyan. He saw the demon running in fright and called out, “Friend, there is nothing to run from. That is a man, and men are your food. Eat him.” The demon took his own shape again and turned back, doubtful and unsteady on his feet.

The thief knew it was the monkey who had called him back. In his anger he took the monkey’s tail, which was hanging down beside him, into his mouth and bit hard. The monkey decided that this must be something stronger than the demon. He was too frightened to say a word and sat with his eyes shut in pain.

The demon saw him sitting like that and said, “From the look on your face, monkey, Vikala has got hold of you. Whoever runs will live.” And he was gone.

Sanskrit verse 5.84

The gold-finder said to the wheel-bearer, “So let me go home. You stay here and taste the fruit of the tree of greed.”

What this story shows

A demon, a thief and a monkey each take the next one for something worse.

Everyone in the tale is frightened of the wrong thing. The demon fears a name, the thief fears his horse, and the monkey fears a man with strong teeth. Each acts sensibly on what he believes, and the result is a chain of escapes.

The gold-finder takes the last line for himself. He cannot help his friend, the sight of the wheel frightens him, and he means to leave while he can.

  • A word overheard and misread set the whole chase going.
  • Each of the three ran from something he had only guessed at.
  • The gold-finder uses the tale as his reason to go.

A demon came every night to trouble a princess and could not carry her off. One night she complained to her friend of Vikala, the evil hour. The demon took Vikala for a rival stronger than himself, and hid among the horses in the shape of a horse to get a look at him.

A horse thief chose him as the best horse and rode off on him. The demon thought his rider was Vikala and bolted in terror, and the thief guessed he was on a demon. Passing under a banyan, the thief caught a hanging root and swung clear.

A monkey in the tree, the demon’s friend, called to him to come back and eat the man. The thief seized the monkey’s tail, which hung beside him, in his teeth. The monkey took him for something worse than a demon and sat silent with his eyes shut.

The demon looked at the monkey’s face, decided that Vikala had caught him too, and fled. The gold-finder says he will leave in the same way while he can.
