The old snake who carried the frogs

Sthirajivi tells this to Meghavarna after the owls have been destroyed. The king has asked how he could bear to live among enemies, and this is his answer: the snake let himself be ridden and waited.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

Near Mount Varuna, in a certain place, lived an old black snake named Mandavisha, Weak-Venom. He thought to himself that he must find some easy way to live. So he went to a pond that was full of frogs and sat there as if he had given up all striving and was at peace.

A frog at the edge of the water saw him sitting so. He called him uncle and asked why he was not going about for his food that day as he used to.

“Friend,” said the snake, “an unlucky creature like me has no wish for food. This evening, just at dusk, I was out looking for a meal and saw a frog. I made ready to catch him.

He saw me and, in fear for his life, slipped in among some brahmins who were busy reciting the Veda. I could not tell where he had gone. Mistaking it for him, I bit the toe of a brahmin’s son, which was in the water at the edge of the pond. The boy died at once.”

“His father, in his grief, cursed me. ‘Wretch, you have bitten my son, who had done no wrong. For this fault you shall become a mount for the frogs, and you shall live on what they are pleased to give you.’ So I have come here to carry you.”

The frog told this to all the other frogs. They were delighted, and all of them went and reported it to Jalapada, Water-Foot, the king of the frogs. He thought it a great wonder. Surrounded by his ministers, he came hurrying out of the pond and climbed onto Mandavisha’s hood. The rest climbed onto the snake’s back in order of rank. Those who found no place on top ran along behind him.

To please them, Mandavisha showed off many different paces. Jalapada enjoyed it greatly and said, “Riding an elephant, a horse, a chariot or a litter is nothing to me beside riding Mandavisha.”

Sanskrit verse 3.239

The next day Mandavisha crawled very slowly, on purpose. Jalapada saw it and asked why he was not carrying him as well as before. Mandavisha said, “Lord, I have had no food today and have no strength to carry you.” The king said, “Then eat the small frogs.”

At this a thrill ran through Mandavisha’s whole body. He said quickly, “That is exactly the brahmin’s curse on me. I am glad to have your leave.” From then on he ate frogs without a break, and in a few days he had grown strong. He laughed to himself and said, “These frogs of every kind have come to me by a trick. With me eating them, they will not last long.”

Sanskrit verse 3.240

Jalapada’s mind was so clouded by Mandavisha’s made-up words that he understood none of it.

Meanwhile another large black snake came to that place. He saw Mandavisha being ridden by frogs and was astonished. “Friend,” he said, “you are carrying those who are our food. This is the wrong way round.” Mandavisha said, “I know all about it, how the frogs ride on me. I am waiting a while, like the brahmin who was blind from ghee.” The other snake asked how that had been, and Mandavisha told him.

“That is why I say,” Sthirajivi finished, “that when the time calls for it a wise person will carry even an enemy on his shoulder. A great black snake killed many frogs in that way.”

Sanskrit verse 3.238

What this story shows

The frog king enjoyed the ride so much that he gave his own people away to keep it going.

Mandavisha is old and cannot catch his food, so he offers the frogs something they have never had: their enemy as a servant. His story of the curse is made up from start to finish. The frogs believe it because it flatters them.

The turn is the king’s own order. Jalapada wants a faster ride, and he pays for it with the small frogs. Sthirajivi tells this of himself, as the one who bore insult and waited, and the text shows the deceit plainly as deceit. Read from the pond, it is a story about a ruler who trades his people for his own pleasure and does not notice.

  • The snake’s tale worked because it gave the frogs a reason to feel grand.
  • The frog king himself gave leave for the small frogs to be eaten.
  • Another snake saw at once that the arrangement was upside down.

An old snake, too weak to hunt, came to a pond full of frogs and sat there as if he had given up the world. A frog asked why he was not looking for food.

The snake said he had bitten a brahmin’s son by mistake, and that the father had cursed him to become a mount for frogs. He had come, he said, to carry them.

The frogs told their king, who thought it a great wonder. He climbed onto the snake’s hood, the others climbed on behind by rank, and the snake showed off his paces. The frog king said no ride had ever pleased him so much.

The next day the snake crawled slowly and said he was too hungry to carry anyone. The frog king told him to eat the small frogs.

From that day the snake ate frogs as he pleased and grew strong, and the pond grew quiet. The frog king, taken in by his talk, understood nothing.

Another snake came by and asked why he let his own food ride on him. He said he was only biding his time. The old crow minister tells this of himself, for he too carried his enemies and waited.
