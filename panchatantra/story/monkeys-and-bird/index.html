The monkeys and the bird

Karataka tells this to Damanaka while the lion and the bull are fighting, to say that Damanaka is past teaching.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

“What is the use of advising a fool?” said Karataka. “It brings harm and no good. Wood that will not bend cannot be bent, and a razor does nothing to a stone. Learn from Suchimukha: do not teach one who is no pupil.” “How was that?” asked Damanaka.

Sanskrit verse 1.417

A troop of monkeys lived on a mountainside. One winter a bitter wind set them shivering, and rain and sleet beat down on them in sheets. They could find no relief.

Some of them gathered gunja berries, which are red and look like sparks. They stood in a ring and blew on them, hoping for fire.

A bird named Suchimukha saw them working for nothing. “You are fools, all of you,” he said. “Those are gunja berries. There is no fire in them. Why wear yourselves out? They will not keep off the cold. Look for a place out of the wind, a cave or a hollow in the hill. The clouds are still piled high.”

An old monkey answered him. “Fool, what is this to you? Go away. A sensible creature does not talk to a gambler who has just lost, or to anyone whose work keeps going wrong.”

Sanskrit verse 1.418

Suchimukha paid no attention. He went on at the monkeys without a pause. “Why all this useless trouble?”

He would not stop. One of the monkeys, angry that his work had come to nothing, caught him by the wings, dashed him against a rock, and killed him.

“That is why I say,” Karataka finished, “wood that will not bend cannot be bent.”

What this story shows

Good advice, pressed on those who do not want it, can cost the adviser.

Suchimukha was right about everything. The berries were berries, the cold was coming, and a cave was the answer. He was killed for it all the same. He opened by calling the monkeys fools, he was told to go, and he kept talking. They were cold, their plan was failing, and someone would not stop pointing it out. The story shows what advice looks like from the other side: to people who are already failing, a correct voice that will not stop is one more misery, and it is the one within reach.

Karataka tells this to Damanaka while the lion and the bull are fighting. His point is that Damanaka cannot be taught. Then he goes on teaching him, with four more stories. Karataka is Suchimukha here, right and unable to stop. Damanaka, unlike the monkey, lets him talk and changes nothing.

  • Being right does not make advice welcome.
  • When you are told to stop, the lesson is over, whoever was right.
  • People who are cold and failing do not need to hear that they are fools.
