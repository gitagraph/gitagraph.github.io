The jackal cub reared by a lioness

The king tells this to the potter to explain why he must go.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

A lion and a lioness lived in a certain place, and the lioness gave birth to two cubs. Every day the lion killed deer and brought them to her.

One day he found nothing, and the sun set while he was still roaming. On his way home he came on a jackal cub. He saw that it was only a baby, so he carried it carefully between his teeth and gave it to the lioness alive.

She asked whether he had brought them any food. “My dear,” he said, “today I found no creature but this jackal cub. I did not kill it, because it is a child. One should never strike a woman, a brahmin, an ascetic or a child, even at the cost of one’s life, and least of all one who trusts. Eat it now and keep up your strength. In the morning I will bring something else.”

She said, “You did not kill it because it is a child. I cannot destroy it for the sake of my own belly. What ought not to be done is not to be done even when death is near, and what ought to be done is not to be given up. That is the old rule. This one shall be my third son.”

Sanskrit verse 4.41

She fed the cub on her own milk until it thrived. The three young ones grew up together, eating and playing alike, and none of them knew that one was of a different kind.

One day a wild elephant came wandering through that forest. The two lion cubs saw him and started toward him with angry faces. The jackal cub said, “That is an elephant, the enemy of your family. Do not go near him.” Then he ran home.

The two lion cubs lost heart when their elder brother broke away. The text says that one steady fighter who is eager for battle makes a whole army eager, and that when he breaks the army breaks, which is why kings look for strong and spirited fighters and keep cowards away.

Sanskrit verses 4.42, 4.43

The two went home and told their parents how their elder brother had seen an elephant and run from a long way off. He heard it and flew into a rage. His lip shook, his eyes went red and his brow knotted, and he abused them in harsh words.

The lioness took him aside and said, “Child, never talk like that. They are your little brothers.” He grew angrier still. “I am not behind them in courage, looks, learning or skill,” he said. “They have no right to laugh at me. I will kill them both.”

The lioness wanted him to live. She smiled to herself and said, “You are brave, you are accomplished and you are good to look at, my son. But in the family you were born into, no elephant is killed.

Sanskrit verse 4.39

Listen well. You are a jackal’s child. I took pity on you and reared you on my own milk. My two sons are still young and do not know that you are a jackal. Go quickly and live among your own kind before they find out, or they will kill you.” He was terrified. He slipped away quietly and joined his own kind.

What this story shows

The lioness speaks out of care, at the moment when silence would have got him killed.

The cub is not sent away for running from the elephant. He is sent away because he means to fight his brothers, and his foster mother knows how that would end.

In the frame the king uses the tale to say that birth sets a limit. The tale itself shows something gentler alongside that: a lion who will not kill a child, a lioness who will not eat one, and a mother who tells a hard truth to keep her son alive.

  • The lion and the lioness both refuse to harm a child, even when hungry.
  • One fighter who turns back can take the heart out of those beside him.
  • The lioness tells him the truth to save his life, and he believes her.

A lion found a jackal cub one evening when he had caught nothing else. He would not kill a child, and carried it home alive to the lioness.

The lioness would not eat it either. She reared it on her own milk with her two cubs, and the three grew up together, none of them knowing that one was different.

One day a wild elephant came by. The two lion cubs made for it, and the jackal cub warned them off and ran home. The others lost heart and followed.

At home the brothers laughed at him, and he swore to kill them. The lioness took him aside and told him that he was a jackal’s child, and that he must go before they found out. He slipped away to his own kind.
