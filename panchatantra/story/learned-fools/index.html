The four learned fools

The gold-finder tells this to say his friend knew books and not the world.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived four brahmins who were friends. As boys they decided to go to another country and get learning. They went to Kanyakubja, entered a school there and studied with a single mind for twelve years, until all four were masters of their subjects. Then they said, “We know every science now. Let us take leave of our teacher and go home.” They took his leave and their books and set out.

After a while the road divided, and they all sat down. One of them asked which way to take. Just then the son of a merchant had died in the town nearby, and a great crowd was going out to the burning ground. One of the four looked in his book and read, “The way the many go is the way.” So they went with the crowd.

At the burning ground they saw an ass. The second opened his book and read, “He who stands by you at a feast, in trouble, in famine, in danger from enemies, at the king’s gate and at the burning ground is a kinsman.” “Then this is our kinsman,” they said. One of them put his arms round the ass’s neck and another washed its feet.

Sanskrit verse 5.39

Then they looked about and saw a camel, and wondered what it was. The third opened his book and read, “Dharma moves swiftly.” “Then this must be dharma,” he said. The fourth read, “Join what is dear to dharma.” So they tied the ass to the camel’s neck. Someone told the washerman who owned the ass, and he came to give the learned fools a beating, but they ran away.

A little further on they came to a river. A leaf was floating down on the water, and one of the scholars said, “The leaf that comes will carry us across.” He threw himself onto it, and the river began to carry him away. Another scholar caught him by the hair and said, “When everything is about to be lost, the wise give up half and work with the other half, for the loss of everything is hard to bear.”

Sanskrit verse 5.40

The source does not say what he did next. Only three of the four go on from the river.

They went on and came to a village, where the villagers invited them and took each to a different house. One was given noodles with ghee and sugar. He thought it over and said, “The long-drawn-out comes to ruin,” and he left the food and went.

The second was given broad thin cakes. He said, “What is spread too wide does not live long,” and he left too. The third was given a cake full of holes. He said, “Where there are holes, troubles multiply.” So the three scholars, thin with hunger and laughed at by everyone, went home to their own country.

The gold-finder said to the wheel-bearer, “You did not know the ways of the world, and you would not stop when I held you back. That is how you came to this. Those who are skilled in the sciences and know nothing of how the world goes are laughed at by all, like those learned fools.”

Sanskrit verse 5.38

What this story shows

Every saying they quote is sound, and every use they make of it is wrong.

The joke rests on sayings that are true in their place. The many, the kinsman, the swiftness of dharma and the danger of holes all mean something, and none of it is about a funeral crowd, an ass, a camel or a cake.

The scholars never look at what is in front of them. They look it up. The gold-finder says his friend did the same when he walked on past the gold.

  • A rule applied without looking at the case can be worse than no rule.
  • Twelve years of study did not teach them to tell a kinsman from an ass.
  • They end hungry at a table laid for them.

Four friends studied for twelve years and set out for home with their books.

At a fork in the road they read that the way the many go is the way, and followed a funeral crowd. At the burning ground they read that whoever stands by one there is a kinsman, and embraced an ass.

They took a passing camel for dharma, which moves swiftly, and tied the ass to it, since what is dear should be joined to dharma. The ass’s owner came after them and they ran.

At a river one of them read that the leaf that comes will carry one across, and jumped onto a floating leaf. Another caught him by the hair and quoted that the wise give up half to save the rest. Three of the four went on.

In a village each was served a dish and found a saying against it, and all three went home hungry. The gold-finder says his friend, too, knew books and not the world.
