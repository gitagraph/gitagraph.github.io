Yudhishthira the potter

The monkey tells this to tease the crocodile for giving his own plan away.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived a potter. One day he was running fast and carelessly, and he fell on the sharp edge of a broken pot that stood half sunk in the ground. It split his forehead open. Covered in blood, he got up with difficulty and went home. He did not take care of the wound, so it grew ugly and was slow to heal.

Later a famine struck the country. Thin with hunger, the potter went abroad with some men who served a king, and he too entered that king’s service.

The king saw the fearsome scar on his forehead and thought, “This must be a brave man. Only a blow taken face to face leaves such a mark.” So he showed him more favour and honour than any of the warriors of his court. The warriors saw it and were bitterly jealous, but for fear of the king they said nothing.

One day war was at hand. Elephants were being made ready, horses harnessed and soldiers drawn up, and the king was honouring his brave men. In a quiet moment he asked the potter, “Sir, tell me your name and your family, and the battle in which you took that blow on your forehead.”

The potter said, “Lord, no weapon gave me this. I am a potter by birth, and my name is Yudhishthira. There were many broken pots at my house. Once I had been drinking, and I ran out and fell on one of them. This scar is from that fall.”

The king was ashamed. “I have been deceived by a potter who passed for a warrior,” he said. “Put him out at once.” As his men took hold of him the potter said, “Do not do this. Wait and see how quick my hand is in battle.”

The king said, “You have every good quality. Go all the same. You are brave, you are accomplished and you are good to look at, my son. But in the family you were born into, no elephant is killed.” The potter asked what he meant, and the king told him a story.

When he had told it, the king said, “So you too should go quickly, before these warriors learn that you are a potter. If they do, they will make sport of you and it will be your death.” The potter heard him and was gone at once.

The monkey said to the crocodile, “You laid a trap for me, and then, like that Yudhishthira, you undid it yourself by telling the truth. The verse says that one who lets go of his own interest and speaks the truth loses what he was after.”

Sanskrit verse 4.38

What this story shows

A reputation built on someone else’s guess lasts only until the first plain question.

The potter never lies. The king reads the scar for himself, and the favour that follows rests entirely on that reading. One honest answer brings it down.

The monkey uses the tale as a jibe. The crocodile had him on his back in deep water and then told him the truth. The story itself is harder to settle: the king sends away a man he admits is able, and gives his birth as the reason. The telling here leaves that as the king’s view.

  • Favour that rests on a mistaken guess can end with a single question.
  • The potter is dismissed for his birth by a king who grants him every merit.
  • The monkey’s point is narrower: the crocodile spoiled his own plan by talking.

A potter once fell on a broken pot and split his forehead. The wound healed slowly and left a fearsome scar.

In a famine he went abroad and took service with a king. The king saw the scar, took it for a wound won face to face in battle, and honoured him above his warriors.

When war came, the king asked him in which battle he had been wounded. The potter said plainly that he was a potter and had fallen on a pot. The king felt deceived and told him to go, and gave his reason with a story.
