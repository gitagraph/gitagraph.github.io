The monkey chief’s warning

The gold-finder tells this to say that greed makes a mockery of everyone.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain city lived a king named Chandra. His sons loved to play with monkeys and kept a troop of them, well fed every day on all kinds of food. The chief of the troop knew the teachings of Ushanas, Brihaspati and Chanakya, lived by them and taught them to the rest.

In the palace there was also a flock of rams for the young princes to ride. One of the rams was greedy, and day and night he walked boldly into the kitchen and ate whatever he saw. The cooks hit him with whatever came to hand, a piece of wood, a clay dish or a pot of bronze or copper.

The monkey chief saw this and thought, “This quarrel between the ram and the cooks will be the ruin of the monkeys. The ram cannot keep away from the food, and the cooks are hot-tempered and strike with whatever is nearest.

One day, for want of anything else, they will hit him with a burning stick. His thick wool will catch at the smallest flame. He will run burning into the stable next door, and the stable is full of hay and will catch too. The horses will be burnt. And Shalihotra says that burns on a horse are cured with the fat of monkeys. That is how it will go.”

He called all the monkeys aside and said, “Where a ram and the cooks are quarrelling, there the monkeys are sure to be destroyed. One who wants to live keeps well away from a house where there is quarrelling every day for no reason.

Sanskrit verses 5.70, 5.71

Quarrels are the end of great houses, a harsh word is the end of friendship, a bad king is the end of a kingdom and a bad deed is the end of a good name. Let us leave this palace for the forest before we are all dead.”

Sanskrit verse 5.72

The monkeys were proud of their comfort and did not believe him. They laughed and said, “Old age has weakened your wits. A toothless mouth, a chin that is always wet and a mind that lights on nothing belong to a child and still more to the old. We are not going to give up heavenly food from the princes’ own hands to eat sour, bitter, rough fruit in the forest.”

Sanskrit verse 5.73

His eyes filled with tears. “Fools, you do not know where this comfort ends. It is sweet in the tasting and will be poison at the last. I will not stay to watch my family die. I am going to the forest now. They are fortunate who do not see a friend in trouble, their home in the hands of others, their country broken and their family destroyed.” He left them all and went.

Sanskrit verse 5.74

Some days after he had gone, the ram came into the kitchen again. The cook found nothing else to hand and struck him with a half-burnt log. The ram ran bleating, his body in flames, into the stable close by. He rolled on the ground, which was deep in hay, and fire sprang up everywhere. Some of the horses died. Others broke their ropes and ran about half burnt and screaming, and the whole household was thrown into confusion.

The king was dismayed. He sent for the doctors who knew Shalihotra’s science and asked for a cure for the burns. They looked in their books and said, “Lord, the sage Shalihotra says that the harm done to horses by fire is driven off by the fat of monkeys, as darkness is by the sunrise. Have it done quickly, before the horses die.” The king ordered that all the monkeys be killed, and they were killed, every one.

Sanskrit verse 5.75

The monkey chief heard that his sons, his grandsons and all his kin were dead. He was sunk in grief and stopped eating, and he wandered from forest to forest thinking how to pay the king back. He told himself that one who puts up with an outrage done to his family, out of fear or for gain, is the lowest of creatures.

Sanskrit verse 5.76

One day, thirsty from his wandering, he came on a lake covered with lotuses. He looked closely and saw that the tracks of animals and men went into the water and none came out. He thought that some fierce creature must live in it, so he took a lotus stalk and drank through it from a distance.

A demon rose out of the lake with a necklace of jewels round his neck. “Whoever sets foot in this water is my food,” he said. “No one is as sharp as you, who drink in that way. I am pleased with you. Ask for what you wish.”

The monkey asked how many he could eat. The demon said, “Hundreds, thousands, tens of thousands, as many as come into the water. Out of the water even a jackal can get the better of me.”

The monkey said, “I have a deadly quarrel with a certain king. Give me that necklace, and I will talk him into this lake with all his people.” The demon believed him, gave him the necklace and told him to do as he thought fit.

The monkey went back to the city with the necklace round his neck and was seen moving among the trees and the palace roofs. People asked him where he had been so long and where he had found such jewels, which outshone the sun.

He said, “In a certain forest there is a hidden lake that Kubera made. Whoever bathes in it on a Sunday when the sun is half risen comes out, by Kubera’s grace, with a necklace like this one.”

The king heard of it and sent for him and asked whether it was true. The monkey said, “Lord, the proof is round my neck. If you want such necklaces, send someone with me and I will show the place.” The king said, “I will come myself, with my whole household, and we will bring back a great many.”

So the king set out with his wives and his servants, all eager for jewels, and the monkey rode in the king’s own litter, on his lap. The text says here that greed sends even the rich to do what they should not and to wander in wild places, and that when everything else in a person grows old, greed alone stays young.

Sanskrit verses 5.77, 5.79

They reached the lake at dawn. The monkey said, “Lord, the gift comes to those who go in when the sun is half risen, so let all your people go in together. You and I will go in afterward, and I will take you to the place I found and show you necklaces without number.” They all went in, and the demon ate them.

When they did not come back the king asked why his people were so long. The monkey ran up a tree and said, “Wicked king, the demon in the water has eaten your people. I have paid you back for the death of my family. Go. I did not send you in, because you were my master.

A wrong is answered with a wrong and a hurt with a hurt, and I see no fault in dealing badly with the bad. You destroyed my family, and I have destroyed yours.” The king, in helpless anger, went back on foot, alone, the way he had come.

When he had gone, the demon came up out of the water, well fed and pleased, and said, “Your enemy is destroyed, you have made a friend and you have kept the necklace. Drinking through a stalk was well done, monkey.” The gold-finder said to the wheel-bearer, “One who acts out of greed and does not look to the end of it is made a mockery of, like King Chandra.”

Sanskrit verses 5.69, 5.81

What this story shows

The chief saw the whole chain from one thrown pot, and no one would move.

The first half is about foresight. The chief follows a small quarrel through five steps to its end and is laughed at for it. The troop has good food and cannot imagine it stopping.

The second half is his revenge, and the telling here gives it as his own act and his own reasons. He uses the king’s greed as the cooks’ temper was used, and a whole household dies for it. The gold-finder’s stated point is only the last line: greed that does not look ahead ends in mockery.

  • A danger can be plain several steps before it arrives.
  • Comfort made the troop deaf to the one who could see.
  • The king lost his household to the same greed that the tale warns of.

A king’s sons kept a troop of monkeys at the palace. A greedy ram kept getting into the kitchen, and the cooks hit him with whatever was at hand.

The monkey chief foresaw that one day they would strike the ram with a burning stick, that the stable would catch fire, and that monkeys would be killed to treat the burnt horses. He begged the troop to leave. They laughed at him, and he went to the forest alone.

It happened as he had said. The ram ran burning into the stable, the horses were burnt, and the king’s doctors prescribed the fat of monkeys. The whole troop was killed.

The chief grieved and looked for a way to pay the king back. At a lake he saw tracks going in and none coming out, and drank through a lotus stalk. The demon of the lake admired this, and gave him a jewel necklace in return for a promise to bring the king’s people.

He went back wearing the necklace and said that a hidden lake gave such jewels to all who bathed in it at sunrise. The king set out with his whole household, with the monkey on his lap.

The household went into the water, and the demon took them. From a tree the monkey told the king that this was payment for his family, and the king walked home alone. The gold-finder says that greed which does not look ahead ends in mockery.
