The jackal and the bowstring

The poor brahmin tells this to his wife. She has refused to give food away, and he is warning her against wanting too much.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

A hunter went into the forest to hunt. He came upon a great boar, black and tall as a hilltop. He drew his bow to the ear and shot it with a sharp arrow.

The boar turned on him in rage. Its tusk, bright as the new moon, tore his belly open, and the hunter fell dead. Then the boar died too, from the pain of the arrow.

A jackal came wandering to that place. He had gone without food and his own death was close. He saw the boar and the hunter lying there and was overjoyed. “Fate is kind to me,” he thought. “Here is food I never looked for. What a creature did in another life comes back to it, good or bad, without any effort.”

Sanskrit verse 2.81

“I will eat this so that it lasts me many days,” he thought. “What one has earned should be enjoyed slowly, a little at a time, the way the wise take a tonic. So I will begin with the sinew string on the tip of this bow.”

Sanskrit verse 2.83

He took the end of the strung bow in his mouth and began to gnaw the string. The string snapped. The tip of the bow sprang up through the roof of his mouth and came out at the top of his head. He died on the spot.

“So I say, do not want too much,” the brahmin told his wife. “The one who does ends with a crest on his head.”

Sanskrit verse 2.80

What this story shows

With a feast in front of him, the jackal chose to chew a string so the feast would last. That saving cost him his life.

The story shows what sudden plenty can do to someone who has been hungry. The jackal had food before him for many days. He could have eaten. He began with the least of it, the string, so that nothing would be used up too soon.

The brahmin tells it with care. In his words, wanting itself should stay. The harm begins where it goes past what is needed.

  • Wanting is part of life, and too much of it does harm.
  • The jackal had food enough and still held back from it.
  • A plan to make good luck last longer was what ended it.

A hunter in the forest met a great boar and shot it with an arrow. The boar turned on him and killed him, and then died of its wound.

A starving jackal came upon the two of them. He was overjoyed at food he had never looked for. To make it last many days, he decided to begin with the sinew string of the hunter’s bow.

He took the end of the strung bow in his mouth and gnawed. The string snapped, the bow sprang straight, and it killed him. The brahmin told his wife this to say that wanting too much brings harm.
