The golden swans of the Lotus Lake

The brahmin Haridatta tells this to his kinsmen, the day after the snake killed his son. He is saying that those who refuse kindness to one who depends on them lose what they have.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town there was a king named Chitraratha. He had a lake called Padmasaras, the Lotus Lake, which his soldiers guarded well. Many swans lived on it, and they were made of gold. Every six months each of them gave up one feather.

One day a great golden bird came to that lake. The swans told him, “You may not live among us. We hold this lake because we pay for it, one feather each at the end of every six months.” To be brief, a quarrel arose between them.

The bird went to the king for protection. “Lord,” he said, “those birds say this: ‘The king can do nothing to us. We give no one a place to live.’ I told them, ‘You have not spoken well. I shall go and report this to the king.’ That is how matters stand, and it is for Your Majesty to decide.”

The king said to his servants, “Go. Kill all those birds and bring them here quickly.” They set out the moment the order was given.

When the swans saw the king’s men coming with clubs in their hands, one old bird among them said, “Kinsmen, this is bad. We must all be of one mind and fly away.” And so they did.

“That is why I say,” Haridatta finished, “that whoever shows no kindness to those who come to him for shelter loses what he has.”

Sanskrit verse 3.133

What this story shows

The swans had a safe home on good terms. They lost it by turning away one bird who asked to stay.

The swans were secure. Soldiers guarded their lake and the rent was light. Their answer to the newcomer was to shut him out. He carried their words to the king, and the text gives us only his account of what they said.

The king’s order is sudden and severe, and the swans escape only by flying off together on an old bird’s word. Haridatta draws his own lesson from it: his son had struck a creature that lived on their land and under their care. The story is brief in this edition and ends with the flight.

  • The swans held their lake by an agreement, and treated it as theirs alone.
  • One complaint to the king was enough to end their safety.
  • They saved their lives by acting together at once.

A king had a lake guarded by his soldiers, and golden swans lived on it. Every six months each swan gave the king one feather.

A great golden bird came to the lake. The swans told him he could not stay, because they had paid for the lake, and a quarrel began.

The bird went to the king and reported that the swans said the king could do nothing to them. The king ordered his men to kill the swans and bring them in.

An old swan saw the men coming with clubs and told the others to fly, all together. They flew away, and the lake was left empty. The brahmin tells this to say that those who turn away one who seeks shelter lose what they have.
