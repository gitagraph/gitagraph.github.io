The merchant’s son and his one verse

Hiranyaka the mouse tells this to the crow and the tortoise. He has lost his hoard and nearly his life, and this verse is what he holds on to.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

In a certain city lived a merchant named Sagaradatta. His son bought a book for a hundred coins. One verse was written in it and nothing more. “A man gets what is meant to be his. Even a god cannot cross that. So I do not grieve and I do not wonder. What is ours does not go to others.”

Sanskrit verse 2.111

The merchant asked what the book had cost. “A hundred coins,” said his son. “You fool,” said the merchant. “You pay a hundred coins for one verse. How will a mind like that ever earn money? From today you do not enter my house.” And he drove him out.

Hurt, the young man went far away to another country and stopped in a city there. After some days a man of the city asked where he had come from and what his name was. “A man gets what is meant to be his,” he answered. He gave the same answer to everyone who asked. So the city came to know him by those words. His name there was Praptavyamartha, What Is Meant To Be His.

The king of that city had a daughter named Chandravati, young and lovely. On a festival day she was out looking at the city with a friend when a handsome prince came into view. She loved him at first sight. “Find a way for me to meet him today,” she told her friend.

The friend went to the prince. “Chandravati has sent me,” she said. “Since she saw you she is sick with love, and she says she will die if you do not come soon.” The prince asked how he was to get in. “A strong rope will hang from the palace tonight,” said the friend. “Climb up by it.” He agreed, and she went back.

When night came the prince thought it over. This was a great wrong, he saw. A deed that brings disgrace and drags a person down should be left undone. He did not go.

Sanskrit verse 2.113

Praptavyamartha was wandering that night and passed the white palace. He saw the rope hanging there, grew curious, took hold of it and climbed up. The princess took him for the prince. She had him bathed, fed and dressed, and kept him beside her. “I gave you my heart the moment I saw you,” she said. “I will have no husband but you, even in thought. Why do you say nothing to me?” “A man gets what is meant to be his,” he said. She knew then that this was someone else. She had him let down from the palace and sent away.

He went to a ruined temple and lay down to sleep. The city watchman came there to meet a woman in secret and found him. To keep his secret safe the watchman asked who he was. “A man gets what is meant to be his.” “This temple is empty,” said the watchman. “Go to my place and sleep there.”

He went, and in his confusion lay down on the wrong bed. The watchman’s daughter, Vinayavati, was asleep there. She was in love with a man and had told him to come that night. In the thick dark she took the stranger for him. She rose, gave him food and clothes, and married him by her own word, as lovers may. “Why do you still not speak freely with me?” she asked. “A man gets what is meant to be his.” Then she understood. “This is what comes of acting without looking,” she thought, and sadly sent him out.

As he walked down the street a bridegroom named Varakirti came by from another district, with music playing loudly. Praptavyamartha fell in with the wedding party.

The bride, a merchant’s daughter, sat in her wedding dress on a platform by the door of her father’s house, beside the king’s road. The lucky hour was near. Just then an elephant in a rage, which had killed its driver, came charging to that spot with people screaming and running before it. The bridegroom and all his party ran off in every direction.

The girl was left alone, her eyes wide with fear. “Do not be afraid. I will protect you,” said Praptavyamartha. He steadied her, took her right hand in his and, with great daring, shouted the elephant down. By some turn of fate the elephant went away.

Varakirti came back with his friends and family after the lucky hour had passed. He saw the girl’s hand in another man’s. “Father-in-law, this is wrong,” he said. “You promised her to me and have given her to someone else.” “I ran from the elephant as you did,” said the merchant. “I do not know what has happened here.” He turned to his daughter. “Child, this was not well done. Tell me what happened.” “This man saved my life,” she said. “As long as I live, no one else will take my hand.”

The talk went on until the night was over. In the morning a great crowd gathered. The princess heard the news and came. The watchman’s daughter heard it passed from ear to ear, and she came too. Hearing of the crowd, the king himself arrived.

“Speak freely,” the king said to Praptavyamartha. “What has happened here?” “A man gets what is meant to be his,” he said. The princess remembered. “Even a god cannot cross that,” she said. “So I do not grieve and I do not wonder,” said the watchman’s daughter. The merchant’s daughter had listened to it all. “What is ours does not go to others,” she said.

The king promised that no one would be harmed and heard each of them out. When he understood, he gave Praptavyamartha his own daughter with great honour, and a thousand villages with her. “You are my son,” he said, and before the whole city he made him heir to the throne. The watchman also gave him his daughter, with such gifts as he could afford.

Praptavyamartha sent for his father and mother and all his family and brought them to that city with honour. There he lived in comfort among his own people.

“So I say,” Hiranyaka told his friends, “a man gets what is meant to be his.”

What this story shows

A young man owns one verse and answers every question with it. The night keeps handing him what others had arranged for someone else.

The story shows people acting on what they expect to find. The princess and the watchman’s daughter each welcome a stranger in the dark because they are sure of who is coming. One of them says it herself: this is what comes of acting without looking.

It also shows who stays when things turn frightening. The bridegroom runs from the elephant with all his party. The stranger stands by the girl, and after that she will have no one else.

The mouse tells this just after his hoard is taken. The verse is how he carries the loss. What left him was never his to keep, and what is his will come.

  • People who are sure of what they expect often do not look.
  • The one who stayed beside the frightened girl was the one she chose.
  • After a loss, people take comfort in the thought that what is theirs will still come.

A merchant’s son paid a hundred coins for a book that held a single verse: a man gets what is meant to be his. His father called him a fool who would never earn money. He drove him out of the house.

The young man went to a far city. To anyone who asked his name he answered with the first words of his verse. So the city came to call him Praptavyamartha, What Is Meant To Be His.

The king’s daughter saw a handsome prince on a festival day and loved him at once. She sent her friend to tell him to climb to her that night by a rope hung from the palace. The prince agreed.

At night the prince thought it a great wrong and stayed away. The young man, wandering by, saw the rope and climbed it out of curiosity. The princess welcomed him as the prince until he spoke his one line, and then she sent him away.

He lay down to sleep in a ruined temple. The city watchman came there to meet a woman in secret and wanted him gone. He told the stranger to go and sleep at his own place.

There he lay down on the wrong bed in the dark. The watchman’s daughter was waiting for a man she loved, took the stranger for him and married him by her own word. When he spoke his one line she understood, and sadly sent him out.

In the street he fell in with a wedding party. The bride sat waiting by her father’s door when an elephant in a rage came charging. The bridegroom and all his party ran.

The young man stayed, took the frightened girl’s hand and shouted the elephant away. The bridegroom came back and complained that she had been given to another. She said the man who saved her life was the only one who would take her hand.

By morning a crowd had gathered, and the princess, the watchman’s daughter and the king all came. Asked what had happened, the young man spoke the first line of his verse. The three young women each remembered the night and spoke the other three lines in turn.

The king heard each of them out and understood. He gave the young man his daughter and a thousand villages and made him heir to the throne, and the watchman gave his daughter too. The young man brought his parents and family to live there in honour.
