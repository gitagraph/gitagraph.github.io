The brahmin, the snake and the gold coin

Raktaksha, minister to the owl king Arimardana, tells this when the crow Sthirajivi is found lying hurt beneath the banyan. He wants the crow killed at once, and says a friendship broken by a blow cannot be made whole.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

In a certain town lived a brahmin named Haridatta. He worked his land, and season after season it gave him nothing.

One day at the end of the hot season, worn out by the heat, he lay down in the shade of a tree in the middle of his field. Not far off, on top of an anthill, he saw a fearsome snake with its great hood spread wide. He thought, “This must be the deity of the field, and I have never once worshipped it. That is why my farming fails. I will worship it today.”

He begged some milk from somewhere, poured it into a clay bowl and went up to the anthill. “Guardian of the field,” he said, “all this time I did not know that you lived here, and so I offered you no worship. Forgive me now.” He set the milk before the snake and went home.

When he came back in the morning he found a gold dinar in the bowl. After that he came alone every day, gave the snake milk and took away one dinar.

One day the brahmin had to go to another village, and he told his son to carry the milk. The son took it there, set it down and came home. The next day he went again, saw the dinar and took it. He thought, “This anthill must be full of gold dinars. I will kill the snake and take them all at once.”

So the day after that, as he was giving the milk, the brahmin’s son struck the snake with a club. By some turn of fate the snake did not die. In its anger it bit him with its sharp, poisoned fangs, and he died at once. His kinsmen cremated him on a pile of wood not far from the field.

The next day his father came home. He heard from the kinsmen how his son had died, and he judged that it had happened as it should. He said, “Whoever shows no kindness to creatures that have come to him for shelter loses what he has, like the swans of the lotus lake.” The men asked him how that had been, and he told them.

When he had told it, the brahmin took milk again at dawn, went to the anthill and praised the snake in a loud voice. For a long time there was no answer.

Then the snake, keeping itself hidden inside the mouth of the anthill, spoke to him. “You have come here out of greed, and have put aside even your grief for your son. From now on, friendship between you and me is not fitting. Your son struck me in the folly of youth, and I bit him. I cannot forget the blow of that club, and you cannot forget the sorrow of losing a son.”

Having said this, the snake went down into its hole. The brahmin took a jewel and went home, blaming his son’s lack of sense.

“That is why I say,” Raktaksha finished, “look at the burning pyre.”

What this story shows

The snake and the brahmin both lost something that could not be given back. The snake said so plainly and ended the friendship.

The arrangement between the brahmin and the snake was simple and worked for both. The son saw only the coin. He guessed at a hoard, struck, and paid with his life. The father’s first judgment is striking: he does not blame the snake.

When the father returns with milk, the snake names what it will not pretend away. One remembers the club and the other remembers a son. Raktaksha tells this to argue that the wounded crow can never be a true friend to the owls. Inside the story, the snake simply declines to go on as before, and says why.

  • A daily coin was not enough for the son, who wanted the whole hoard.
  • The father held his son responsible for what happened.
  • The snake refused a friendship that both sides would have had to pretend.

A brahmin farmed his field year after year and it gave him nothing. One hot day he saw a great hooded snake on an anthill there and took it for the guardian of the field.

He set a bowl of milk before the snake and asked its pardon. In the morning a gold coin lay in the bowl. After that he brought milk every day and took home a coin.

One day he had to travel and sent his son with the milk. The son found the coin and decided the anthill must be full of gold. He made up his mind to kill the snake and take it all at once.

The next day he struck the snake with a club. The snake lived and bit him, and the boy died. His kinsmen performed his last rites near the field.

The father came home and judged that his son had brought it on himself. At dawn he took milk to the anthill again. From inside, the snake answered that their friendship was over, for it could not forget the club and he could not forget his son.

The snake went down into its hole, and the brahmin went home with a jewel, blaming his son’s folly. The owl minister tells this to say that a friendship broken by a blow does not mend.
