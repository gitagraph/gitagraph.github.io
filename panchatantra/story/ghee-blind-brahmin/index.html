The brahmin who went blind on ghee

The snake Mandavisha tells this to another snake, who has asked why he lets frogs ride on him. He says he is waiting for his moment, as the brahmin waited.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived a brahmin named Yajnadatta. His wife was unfaithful to him, and her heart was set on another man. She was always making cakes rich in ghee and sugar for that man and taking them to him without her husband’s knowledge.

One day her husband saw it. He asked her what she was cooking and where she kept taking it, and told her to tell him the truth.

She was quick-witted and answered with a made-up tale. “Not far from here is a shrine of the blessed Goddess. I keep a fast and take her an offering there, with special foods like these.”

Then, while he looked on, she picked it all up and set out toward the shrine. She reckoned that if the food was offered to the Goddess, her husband would believe her.

She went down into the river to bathe. Meanwhile her husband came round by another path and stood behind the image of the Goddess, where he could not be seen.

The brahmin’s wife bathed and came to the shrine. She bathed the image, offered ointment, garlands, incense and food, bowed to the Goddess and made her request. “Goddess, tell me by what means my husband will go blind.”

The brahmin, standing behind the image, changed his voice and said, “If you keep feeding your husband ghee cakes and food of that kind, he will soon go blind.” She was taken in by the false words, and from then on she gave the brahmin exactly that every day.

After some days the brahmin said, “Wife, I cannot see at all well.” She thought the Goddess’s favour had been granted. Her lover began to come to her every day without fear, thinking that a blind brahmin could do nothing to him.

One day the brahmin saw him come in and come close. He caught hold of him and beat him until he died. Then he cut off his wife’s nose and sent her away.

What this story shows

The brahmin heard his wife’s wish and pretended it had come true. Everyone who relied on his blindness was mistaken.

Each of the two deceives the other. The wife lies about the cakes and prays in secret for her husband’s blindness. The husband answers her prayer in a false voice and then plays the blind man in his own house. He is well fed while he waits.

The ending is violent, and the text gives it in two short sentences without comment. Mandavisha tells the story for one thing only, the waiting: he too is letting others think him harmless. It is a hard little tale, told by one snake to another, and nothing in it is offered as a way to live.

  • The wife’s secret prayer was heard by the one person it was aimed at.
  • The brahmin kept up his pretence until the lover grew careless.
  • Mandavisha takes only one point from it: he is biding his time.

A brahmin’s wife cared for another man and kept making rich ghee cakes for him in secret. One day her husband saw and asked where she took them. She said they were an offering for the Goddess.

She set out for the shrine and went down to the river to bathe. Her husband came by another path and hid behind the image of the Goddess.

She made her offering and asked the Goddess how her husband might be made blind. From behind the image he answered in a changed voice that ghee cakes every day would do it. From then on she fed him exactly that.

After some days he said he could no longer see well. She believed her wish was granted, and the other man began to come to the house without fear.

One day the brahmin seized the man and beat him to death. He cut off his wife’s nose and sent her away. The snake tells this to another snake to say that he too is only waiting for his moment.

What this story shows

The brahmin heard his wife’s wish and pretended it had come true. Everyone who relied on his blindness was mistaken.
