The crab who went along

The wheel-bearer tells this as his last word, sending his friend home with advice not to go alone.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived a brahmin named Brahmadatta. One day he had to go to another village on some business. His mother said, “My child, you should not go alone. Find someone to go with you.” He said, “Mother, do not be afraid. The road is safe, and my business will not wait. I will go alone.”

She saw that he had made up his mind, so she took a crab from the pond nearby and said, “If you must go, child, let this crab be your companion. Take him with you.” To please his mother he picked the crab up in both hands, put it in a packet of camphor, set the packet inside his bowl and went off quickly.

As he walked, the summer heat wore him out. He came to a tree by the road and fell asleep under it. While he slept, a snake came out of a hollow in the tree and made its way to him. It could not resist the camphor and bit into the packet, and the crab, which was inside, killed it.

When the brahmin woke he saw a black snake lying dead beside him, across the packet of camphor. He understood that the crab had killed it. He was glad and said, “My mother was right. A man should take some companion and not go alone. I did as she said, in good faith, and so the crab has saved me from being killed by a snake.

It is well said that the waning moon goes for shelter to the sun, and the full moon swells the sea. Some are helpers in hard times, and others only share in the fortune of the rich. With a spell, a holy place, a brahmin, a god, an astrologer, a medicine or a teacher, the result is as one’s faith is.”

Sanskrit verses 5.97, 5.98

The wheel-bearer said to the gold-finder, “Even a poor sort of companion keeps one safe on the road. A crab that went along as company saved a life. So do not travel alone.”

Sanskrit verse 5.96

What this story shows

He took the crab only to please his mother, and it was enough.

The brahmin is sure the road is safe and thinks the crab a small concession to an anxious mother. The danger comes in a form he had not thought of, while he is asleep, and the smallest possible companion deals with it.

It is the last tale of the book, and it is told by the man who is being left behind. He cannot be freed from the wheel, and his friend is going. What he gives him is this: do not walk home alone.

  • The mother’s worry was right, though the son could not see why.
  • A companion need not be strong to be the one who is awake.
  • The friend who stays behind sends the other off with care for his safety.

A brahmin was setting out alone, and his mother would not have it. When he refused to wait for company she gave him a crab from the pond to take along.

He slept under a tree in the heat of the day. A snake came out of a hollow toward him and went for the packet where the crab was, and the crab killed it.

He woke to find the snake dead beside him and understood what the crab had done. He said his mother had been right that no one should travel alone.

The wheel-bearer tells this as his last advice: even a small companion keeps one safe on the road.
