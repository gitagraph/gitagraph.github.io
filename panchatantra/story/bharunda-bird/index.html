The bird with two heads

The gold-finder tells this to say that those bound together must act together.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

On a certain lake lived a bird called a bharunda, which had one belly and two necks. One day, wandering along the seashore, it found a fruit as sweet as nectar that the waves had thrown up.

One head began to eat it and said, “I have eaten many sweet fruits brought in by the sea, and none has tasted like this. It must have come from one of the trees of heaven, or else fate has dropped some fruit of nectar here unseen.”

The second head said, “If it is as good as that, give me a little, so that my tongue may enjoy it too.” The first head laughed. “We have one belly between us, and it is filled the same either way. There is no point in eating separately. I would do better to please my wife with what is left.”

He gave the rest to his mate. She tasted it and was delighted, and was full of embraces, kisses and loving words for him. From that day the second head was gloomy and resentful.

Some time later the second head found a poisonous fruit. It showed it to the other and said, “You are cruel and mean and care for no one. I have found a poison fruit, and I am going to eat it because of the way you slighted me.”

The first head said, “Fool, do not do it. If you do, we shall both die.” It ate the fruit all the same, out of the hurt it had been nursing, and both of them perished.

The gold-finder said to the wheel-bearer, “Those who share one belly and have separate necks, and eat different fruits without agreeing, are destroyed, like the bharunda bird.”

Sanskrit verse 5.94

What this story shows

The first head was right about the belly and wrong about everything else.

The first head’s reasoning is correct as far as it goes. The fruit feeds the same body either way. What he leaves out is that the other head has a tongue and a temper of its own.

The second head’s answer is ruin for both, and he knows it when he does it. The gold-finder uses the tale against his friend, who went on alone at the gold. The two were one company on the road, and one of them would not act with the other.

  • Being right about the sum did not excuse the slight.
  • Spite made the second head destroy what it shared.
  • The gold-finder’s point is that partners who will not agree go down together.

A bird with one belly and two necks found a fruit as sweet as nectar on the seashore, and one head began to eat it.

The second head asked for a taste. The first laughed that they shared one belly, and gave what was left to his mate. From that day the second head was resentful.

Later the second head found a poison fruit and said it would eat it to answer the slight. The first head begged it not to, since both would die. It ate the fruit, and both perished.

The gold-finder says that those who share one belly and will not agree are destroyed together.

What this story shows
