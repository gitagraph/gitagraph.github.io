The lion and the hare

Damanaka tells this to Karataka, who still fears that the lion is too fierce and the bull too clever to be parted.

In a forest lived a lion named Bhasuraka. He was so full of his own strength that he killed many deer, hares and other animals every day, and he never stopped.

One day the animals of the forest met: antelope, boar, buffalo, hares and the rest. They went to him together. “Lord, why kill so many of us every day? One animal is enough to fill you. Let us make an agreement. From today, stay where you are, and each day one animal will come to you to be eaten, each kind in its turn. You will have your food without trouble, and we will not all be wiped out.”

“A king should take from his people as a herdsman takes milk from a cow, a little at a time, and keep her fed. A king who kills his people as one kills a goat eats once, and never again.”

“What you say is true,” said Bhasuraka. “But if a day passes and no animal comes to me here, I will eat every one of you.”

They gave their word. After that they wandered the forest without fear, and each day at noon one of them went to the lion. It might be one who was old, or tired of living, or struck by grief, or afraid of losing mate and young.

Then the turn came round to the hares, and all the animals sent one hare. He did not want to go. He walked very slowly, trying to think of a way to kill the lion, and let the hour pass.

On his way he came to a well. He looked over the edge and saw his own reflection in the water. “There is a way,” he thought. “I will make Bhasuraka angry, and my wit will put him in this well.”

The day was almost over when he reached the lion. Bhasuraka was thin with hunger and licking the corners of his mouth in rage. “In the morning,” he was thinking, “I will empty this forest of animals.”

The hare came up slowly, bowed and stood before him. “You wretch,” the lion roared. “You are small to begin with, and you are late as well. For this I will kill you, and tomorrow I will finish every herd in the forest.”

“Lord, the fault is not mine, and it is not the animals',” said the hare humbly. “Please hear the reason.” “Be quick,” said the lion, “before you are between my teeth.”

“Today was my turn. Because I am so small, the animals sent other hares along with me. On the way, another great lion came out of his den and told us to remember our gods. I said we were going to our lord Bhasuraka, as agreed.”

“He said, ‘Then this forest is mine, and the animals must keep their agreement with me. This Bhasuraka is a thief. If he is king here, leave the other hares with me as a pledge and go and call him. Whichever of us proves the stronger will be king and eat them all.’ So I have come, lord, as he ordered. That is why I am late.”

“Show me this thief of a lion at once,” said Bhasuraka. “I will turn my anger on him and be at ease.”

“Lord, he lives in a fort,” said the hare. “He came out of it to stop us. An enemy inside a fort is hard to reach. One archer on a wall holds off a hundred.”

“Show him to me, fort or no fort. An enemy, like an illness, must be put down as soon as he appears.”

“That is so,” said the hare. “But he looked strong to me. It is not wise to go without knowing his strength. A moth that does not know the flame flies into it.” “What is that to you?” said the lion. “Show him to me.”

“Then come, lord,” said the hare, and went ahead. He led the lion to the well. “Lord, who can stand against you? The thief saw you from far off and has gone into his fort. Come and look.”

The foolish lion looked down. He saw his own reflection in the water and let out a roar. The echo came back out of the well twice as loud.

He took it for his enemy, threw himself upon it, and died.

The hare went back glad at heart and gave the news to all the animals. They praised him, and he lived on in that forest in comfort.

“That is why I say,” Damanaka finished, “whoever has wit has strength.”

What this story shows

Strength that never stops to think can be led anywhere.

The lion had everything except the habit of checking. He was told that a rival had insulted him, and from that moment he wanted only to be shown where. The hare counted on that. He even warned the lion twice to be careful, knowing that a proud listener hears a warning as a dare.

The animals praise the hare, and the story lets them. He was sent to die and he lied to live. It matters who is telling it, though. Damanaka uses this story to convince Karataka that a clever servant can bring down a king, and the next thing he does is go to the lion with a lie about the bull. The trick that saves a forest here will destroy a friendship there. The story shows that anger makes the strong easy to steer. It leaves the steering to whoever gets there first.

  • When anger arrives, slow down and check what you were told.
  • An insult carried to you by someone else deserves a second look.
  • The loudest answer may be your own voice coming back.
