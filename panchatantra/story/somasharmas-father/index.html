The brahmin and the pot of meal

The wheel-bearer tells this to say that hope makes fools of everyone.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived a brahmin named Svabhavakripana. He had filled a pot with the barley meal left over from what he got by begging. He hung the pot on a peg in the wall, set his bed underneath it and lay looking up at it with a fixed stare.

One night, as he lay there, he began to think. “This pot is full of meal. If a famine comes, it will fetch a hundred silver coins. With those I will buy a pair of she-goats. They will kid every six months, and I shall have a herd.

With the goats I will buy many cows, with the cows buffaloes, and with the buffaloes mares. The mares will foal, and I shall have many horses. I will sell them for a great deal of gold, and with the gold I will have a house with four wings.

Then some brahmin will come to my house and give me his daughter, a grown girl and a beauty. She will bear me a son, and I will name him Somasharma.”

The daydream went on. “When the boy is old enough to crawl, I shall be sitting behind the stable one day with a book, deep in study. Somasharma will see me and crawl from his mother’s lap toward me, right up to the horses’ hooves.

I shall call out angrily to my wife to take the child. She will be busy with the housework and will not hear me. Then I will get up and give her a kick.” He was so lost in the thought that he kicked out as he lay there. The pot broke, and he was left white from head to foot with meal.

The wheel-bearer said to the gold-finder, “One who makes plans about what has not yet come, and cannot come, ends up lying white with meal, like Somasharma’s father.”

Sanskrit verse 5.68

What this story shows

Each step of the plan is possible, and he owns none of them except the pot.

Nothing in the brahmin’s plan is absurd on its own. Goats do breed, and herds are sold for gold. The trouble is that he lives in the last step of it while lying under the first.

The kick is part of the daydream, aimed at a wife who does not exist over a child who has not been born. It is the one thing in the whole plan that actually happens, and it destroys the only thing he has.

  • A long chain of likely steps is still only a wish.
  • He is already angry at a household he does not have.
  • The pot was real, and it is what he lost.

A brahmin had filled a pot with barley meal saved from his begging. He hung it on a peg above his bed and lay looking up at it.

One night he began to plan. The meal would fetch silver in a famine, the silver would buy goats, the goats cows, the cows buffaloes and mares, and the horses gold and a great house. A brahmin would give him a daughter, and they would have a son named Somasharma.

In the daydream his wife did not hear him call, and he got up to kick her. He kicked out as he lay there, the pot broke, and he was left white with meal. The wheel-bearer says this is what comes of planning on what has not happened.

What this story shows
