The sparrow's nest and the monkey

Karataka tells this to Damanaka straight after the last story, still on the danger of advising the wrong listener.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

“And there is this,” said Karataka. “Advice is not to be handed to just anyone. Look how a fool of a monkey left a well-housed bird without a home.” “How was that?” asked Damanaka.

In a forest a pair of wild sparrows had built their nest so that it hung from a branch of a shami tree. One day, as they sat snug inside, a winter cloud began to rain, slowly and steadily.

A monkey came to the foot of that tree and sat down. The wind and the rain had beaten him. His fur stood on end, his teeth chattered and he shook all over.

The hen sparrow looked down at him. “Good sir, you have hands and feet, and you look almost like a man. You are frozen through, you fool. Why do you not build yourself a house?”

The monkey was furious. “Wretch, why can you not keep quiet? The cheek of her. She is laughing at me. A needle-beaked little chatterer, talking like a scholar and afraid of nothing. Why should I not kill her?”

Then he said to her, “Silly thing, what are my troubles to you? Speak to those who want to hear, and most of all to those who ask. Words given to anyone else are like weeping in an empty forest.”

To be brief: she sat in her nest and told him again. He climbed the shami tree and tore her nest into a hundred pieces.

“That is why I say,” Karataka finished, “advice is not to be handed to just anyone.”

“Fool, you were taught and you did not learn,” he went on. “And perhaps the fault is not yours. Teaching does good to the good. It does nothing for the others.”

What this story shows

Advice that shames the listener is heard as an insult.

The sparrow says something true from a dry nest to a creature sitting in the rain, and she puts the word fool in it. Her question has an answer she does not wait for: he has no house because he is a monkey. Advice of this kind is easy to give when you are warm. The monkey's reply is the surprising part. He states the rule himself, speak to those who ask, and then he climbs the tree. He knows how he ought to be spoken to, and he answers with his hands anyway. She had been told to stop, and she said it once more.

Karataka tells this straight after the story of Suchimukha, and it has the same shape. He is speaking to Damanaka while the fight goes on, and he ends by calling him a fool who was taught and did not learn. The verse blames the monkey and warns the adviser in the same breath. The story shows that advice which shames the listener is heard as an insult, however sound it is, and that the one giving it may be the one with something to lose.

  • Advice given from comfort to someone in trouble sounds like mockery.
  • If you are not asked, think twice. If you are told to stop, stop.
  • The one who has a home has more to lose in a quarrel than the one who has none.
