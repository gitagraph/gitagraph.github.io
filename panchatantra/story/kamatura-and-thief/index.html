The old merchant who thanked a thief

The monkey tells this to say the crocodile threw away a friend for a wife’s favour.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

This tale is also told in Book 3. There the thief answers that he sees nothing worth taking. Here the merchant invites him back every night and offers him all he has.

In a certain town lived a rich old merchant named Kamatura. His wife had died, and desire had got the better of his judgment. He paid a great sum to marry the daughter of a poor merchant. She was sunk in misery and could not bear even to look at him. The text says here that white hair is the seat of a man’s humiliation and that the young keep away from it.

Sanskrit verses 4.56, 4.57

One night she was lying on the bed with her face turned from him when a thief got into the house. She saw the thief, and in her fright she threw her arms tightly round her old husband. He was amazed and thrilled. He wondered why she should hold him now. Then he looked carefully and saw the thief standing in a corner, and he understood that she was holding him out of fear.

He said to the thief, “She who always shrinks from me holds me close today. You have done me a kindness, and I wish you well. Take whatever I have.” As the thief was leaving he called after him, “Thief, come every night. All this wealth of mine is yours.”

Sanskrit verse 4.55

The monkey said to the crocodile, “That man gave everything he owned to a thief for the sake of an embrace. You have done much the same.”

What this story shows

He takes a moment of fear for affection and pays for it with all he has.

The merchant knows why his wife is holding him. He has seen the thief. He thanks the thief anyway, because the embrace matters more to him than its cause.

In Book 3 the same tale is told to argue that even a thief can do good. Here the monkey gives it a sharper ending and a different use: a man who will pay any price to be favoured will end with nothing.

  • The merchant sees the true reason and chooses to ignore it.
  • The same tale is used in Book 3 for a different argument.
  • The monkey’s charge is that the crocodile gave up a friend for a favour.

A rich old merchant paid a great sum to marry the young daughter of a poor man. She was unhappy and could not bear to look at him.

One night a thief got into the house. In her fright she held her husband tightly, and he was delighted. Then he saw the thief in a corner and understood why.

He thanked the thief for the favour, told him to take whatever he had, and asked him to come every night. The monkey says the crocodile gave up a friend for no more than that.

What this story shows
