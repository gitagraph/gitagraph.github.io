Devasharma the ascetic

Damanaka tells this to Karataka

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a quiet place there was a monastery, and an ascetic named Devasharma lived in it. Devout people gave him fine cloth, and by selling it he had gathered a good deal of money over the years. Because of it he trusted nobody. He kept the purse tucked under his arm day and night. Wealth is trouble to earn and trouble to keep, the text says, and its loss and its spending are trouble too.

Sanskrit verse 1.174

A thief named Ashadhabhuti noticed the purse under the ascetic's arm. The monastery walls were solid stone and its door was high, so he could not break in. “I will win his trust with pious talk and become his pupil,” he thought. “One day he will let his guard down.”

He went to Devasharma, said “Om, homage to Shiva,” fell flat at his feet, and spoke humbly. “Master, this world is empty. Youth runs past like a mountain stream, and life burns out like a fire of straw. I have seen it all for what it is. Tell me how to cross the sea of this world.”

Devasharma was touched. “My son, you are blessed to have turned away so young.” Ashadhabhuti clasped his feet and begged to be made his pupil. “I will do it,” said Devasharma. “But you must not come inside the monastery at night. An ascetic should keep no company. You will sleep in the grass hut by the gate.” “Your word is my rule,” said Ashadhabhuti.

Sanskrit verse 1.176

So he became a pupil, and he pleased his teacher by rubbing his hands and feet and serving him in every way. Even so, the ascetic never let the purse leave his armpit. Ashadhabhuti began to lose patience.

Then the son of a follower came from a village to invite Devasharma to a ceremony. The ascetic set out gladly with his pupil. On the way they came to a river. Devasharma took the purse from under his arm and hid it inside his patched cloak. He bathed and said his prayers. Then he said, “Ashadhabhuti, I am going to relieve myself. Guard this cloak with care until I come back.”

As soon as he was out of sight, Ashadhabhuti took the purse and was gone.

Devasharma suspected nothing. He was sitting at ease, well pleased with his pupil, when he saw two rams fighting in the middle of a flock. They backed away in fury and charged, and their foreheads met with a crack. Blood ran onto the ground. A jackal, greedy for it, slipped in between them to lick it up.

“What a dull creature,” thought Devasharma. “If he is caught when they meet, he will die.” A moment later the rams charged again. The jackal was between them, still licking, and their heads killed him.

Devasharma was sorry for the jackal. He walked back slowly to his cloak. His pupil was not there. He washed in a hurry, looked inside the cloak, and found no purse. “I am robbed,” he cried, and fell to the ground in a faint. When he came to, he shouted, “Ashadhabhuti, where have you gone with what you stole from me? Answer me.” Then he followed the thief's footprints, slowly, grieving as he went.

By evening he reached a village. A weaver was just leaving it with his wife, on his way to the next town to drink. “Good man,” said Devasharma, “I am a guest brought to you by the setting sun. I know nobody here. Take me in.” The weaver said to his wife, “Go home with our guest. Wash his feet, feed him, give him a bed, and stay in the house. I will bring you back plenty to drink.” And he went on.

Sanskrit verse 1.181

The weaver's wife had a lover named Devadatta. She walked home with the ascetic, smiling to herself and thinking of him. At the house she gave Devasharma a broken cot with no bedding. “Holy man, a friend of mine has come from her village and I must go and greet her. Keep an eye on my house until I am back.”

She dressed in her finery and set out to meet Devadatta. She had not gone far when she saw her husband coming toward her, reeling drunk, his hair loose, stumbling at every step, with a pot of liquor in his hand. She turned and ran home, took off the finery, and sat as she had before.

The weaver had seen her running, dressed as he had never seen her. He had already heard talk about her, and had kept it to himself. Now he was sure. He came in raging. “Where were you going?” “I have been nowhere since I left you,” she said. “That is the drink talking.” He looked at her changed clothes and did not believe her. He beat her with a stick, tied her tightly to a post, and fell into a drunken sleep.

Her friend, a barber's wife, was the go-between who carried her messages. She learned that the weaver was asleep and came in. “Devadatta is waiting for you at the usual place. Come quickly.” “Look at the state I am in,” said the weaver's wife. “How can I go?” “He is drunk and will not wake until the sun touches him,” said the barber's wife. “I will untie you. Tie me up in your place, go to Devadatta, and come back fast.”

So it was done. Some time later the weaver woke, sober and less angry. “You with the sharp tongue,” he said to the woman at the post, “if you promise never to leave the house again and never to answer me back, I will untie you.” The barber's wife was afraid her voice would give her away, and said nothing. He said it again and again, and still she did not answer. He lost his temper, took a sharp blade, and cut off her nose. Then he fell asleep again.

Devasharma lay awake on his broken cot, hungry and sick at heart over his money, and saw all of it.

The weaver's wife came back from her lover. “Are you all right? Did that wretch wake while I was gone?” “All right, except for my nose,” said the barber's wife. “Untie me before he sees me, and let me go home.” They changed places again.

The weaver woke once more. “Still nothing to say? Shall I cut off your ears next?” This time his wife answered, in a fury. “Fool. Nobody can disfigure a faithful wife like me. Let the guardians of the world hear me. If I am faithful, and have never wanted another man even in thought, let the gods make my nose whole again. If I have, let them burn me to ash.” Then she said, “Look, you wretch. My faithfulness has given me back my nose.”

He lit a brand and looked. Her nose was whole, and there was a great pool of blood on the floor. He was amazed. He untied her, laid her on the bed, and begged her pardon with a hundred sweet words. Devasharma, watching, was amazed as well. The night seemed very long to him.

The barber's wife went home without her nose, wondering how she could hide such a thing. Her husband had spent the night working at the palace. At dawn he stood at the door and called, “Bring me my razor case, quickly. I have customers waiting.” She stayed inside, took a single razor out of the case, and threw it toward him. The barber was in a hurry and wanted the whole case. Annoyed, he threw the razor back into the house.

At that she ran out into the street with her arms in the air, screaming. “Look, all of you. This wicked man has cut off my nose, and I have done nothing. Save me.” The king's men came running. They beat the barber with sticks, tied him up, and took him to court with his wife. “This barber has maimed his good wife for no reason,” they told the judges.

“Barber, why did you do this to your wife?” asked the judges. “Did she go with another man, or try to kill you, or steal?” The barber was so badly beaten that he could not speak. The judges took his silence for guilt. A man who has done wrong, they said, shows it in his face and his voice. They sentenced him to death.

Sanskrit verse 1.210

Devasharma saw the barber being led away to die. He went to the judges and said, “Sirs, this poor man is being killed for nothing. He has done no wrong. Hear me. The jackal was undone by the rams' fight, I by Ashadhabhuti, and the go-between by another woman's affair. All three of us did it to ourselves.” The judges asked what he meant, and he told them all three stories from the beginning.

Sanskrit verse 1.173

They were astonished. They set the barber free. “This woman lost her nose by her own doing,” they said, and as the king's punishment they had her ears cut off as well. As for Devasharma, he grieved no more for his money, and he went back to his monastery. “That is why I say it,” said Damanaka. “Three faults, and each one self-made.”

What this story shows

Each of the three walked into harm that was plain to see.

Three creatures in this story are hurt, and none of them by surprise. The jackal can see the rams. The ascetic knows he trusts no one, and hands his cloak to a stranger of a few weeks. The barber's wife ties herself to another woman's post. Each wanted something badly enough to stop looking: blood, a devoted pupil, a share in a friend's secret.

The court gets it wrong as well. It reads a beaten man's silence as guilt and nearly kills him. Only the ascetic, who watched from a broken cot because he could not sleep, knows what happened. Damanaka tells this just after admitting that he brought the bull to court himself. He names his own mistake clearly, and then goes straight on to plan a worse one.

  • People are seldom ruined by something they could not see coming.
  • Wanting a thing badly makes its danger hard to notice.
  • Silence under pressure is easily taken for guilt.

The ascetic Devasharma had grown rich by selling the fine cloth people gave him. He trusted nobody and kept his purse under his arm day and night. A thief named Ashadhabhuti saw it.

The monastery could not be broken into, so the thief came with pious talk and begged to be made a pupil. He served the ascetic well. The purse still never left the old man's arm.

On a journey they came to a river. The ascetic hid the purse in his cloak, told his pupil to guard it, and went aside. The thief took the purse and was gone.

The ascetic, suspecting nothing, watched two rams fight. A jackal crept between them to lick the blood, and their heads killed him. Then the ascetic found his purse gone and followed the thief's tracks.

At evening a weaver on his way to drink sent the ascetic home with his wife as a guest. She had a lover, and set out to meet him. She met her drunken husband on the road and ran back.

The weaver had heard talk about her, and now he was sure. He beat her, tied her to a post, and fell asleep. The ascetic lay awake and watched.

Her friend, a barber's wife who carried her messages, took her place at the post so she could go to her lover. The weaver woke and spoke to the woman at the post. She dared not answer, and in his anger he cut off her nose.

The wife came back and changed places again. When her husband woke, she swore that if she was faithful the gods would make her nose whole. He lit a brand, saw her nose whole and blood on the floor, and begged her pardon.

At dawn the barber asked for his razor case. His wife threw him one razor, and he threw it back in annoyance. She ran into the street crying that he had cut off her nose, and the king's men seized him.

The barber, beaten, could not speak, and the judges sentenced him to death. The ascetic came forward and told them all three stories: the jackal, himself, and the go-between, each undone by their own doing. The barber was freed, the woman's ears were cut as well, and the ascetic went home at peace.

  • Silence under pressure is easily taken for guilt.
