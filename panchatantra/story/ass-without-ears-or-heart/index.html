The ass with no ears and no heart

The crocodile will not leave, and the monkey answers that he is no Lambakarna.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

In a forest lived a lion named Karalakesara, and a jackal named Dhusaraka followed him everywhere as his servant. One day the lion fought an elephant and was so badly hurt that he could not move a step. Since the lion could not hunt, the jackal grew thin with hunger.

At last he said, “Master, hunger is wearing me out. I can hardly walk, and I am of no use to you like this.” The lion said, “Go and find some animal that I can kill even as I am.”

The jackal searched until he came to a village nearby. By a pond he saw an ass named Lambakarna, picking with difficulty at a few blades of grass. He went up to him and said, “Uncle, accept my greeting. It is long since I saw you. Tell me how you have grown so thin.”

The ass said, “Nephew, there is little to tell. The washerman has no pity. He loads me until I ache and does not give me a handful of fodder. I eat nothing but grass mixed with dust, so my body cannot thrive.”

The jackal said, “Uncle, in that case there is a lovely place by a river, thick with grass as green as emerald. Come and live there, and we will pass the time in good talk.” Lambakarna said, “That is kindly meant, nephew. But we village animals are food for those who live in the forest, so a fine place is no use to me.”

The jackal said, “Do not say that. I guard that place myself, and no one else can enter it. And there is more. Three she-asses live there with no master, for a washerman ill-used them too. They have grown sleek and are in the pride of youth, and they asked me to go to some village and bring them a fit husband. That is why I am taking you.”

At these words desire took hold of the ass, and he told the jackal to lead the way. The text remarks here that nothing is nectar and nothing is poison as a woman is, since a man lives by her company and dies of parting from her, and that the mere name of women stirs desire before one has seen or met them.

Sanskrit verses 4.34, 4.35

So he came with the jackal to where the lion lay. The lion, in pain, began to get up, and at the sight of him the ass turned and ran. The lion struck at him with a paw as he fled, and the blow missed.

The jackal was angry. “That was a poor sort of blow. An ass has got away from under your nose, so I do not see you fighting an elephant. I have seen your strength now.” The lion gave an embarrassed smile and said, “I had not gathered myself to spring. When I have, even an elephant does not get away.”

The jackal said, “I will bring him to you once more. Be ready to spring this time.” The lion said, “He has seen me with his own eyes and gone. He will not come here again. Look for some other animal.” The jackal said, “Leave that to me. You have only to be ready.”

The jackal followed the ass’s tracks and found him grazing in the same place. The ass said, “Nephew, that was a fine place you took me to. I was nearly killed. Tell me what creature it was whose paw came at me like a thunderbolt.”

The jackal laughed. “Good sir, that was the she-ass. She saw you coming and rose to embrace you, and you ran away out of shyness. She put out her hand only to hold you back. She cannot live without you now. She has sat down to fast for your sake, and she says that if Lambakarna will not be her husband she will go into fire or water.

Be kind and come, or a woman’s death will be on your head, and the god of love will be angry with you. Those who turn away from love are fools, and the god himself strikes them down and leaves them naked, shaven, in red rags, with matted hair or carrying skulls.”

The ass believed him and set out with him again. Even one who knows better does what is blameworthy when fate drives him, the text says, for no one would choose such a deed otherwise. This time the lion was ready, and he killed Lambakarna. He set the jackal to guard the kill and went to the river to bathe. The jackal could not hold back his greed, and he ate the ass’s ears and heart.

Sanskrit verse 4.37

The lion bathed, worshipped the gods and made his offerings to his ancestors. When he came back, the ass had no ears and no heart. He turned on the jackal in fury. “Wretch, this was not fit to do. You have eaten the ears and the heart and made my food your leavings.”

The jackal answered humbly, “Master, do not say so. This ass never had ears or a heart. That is how he could come here, see you, go away, and come back again.” The lion took his word for it, shared the meat with him and ate without a doubt in his mind.

“He came and went, and having gone he came again, a fool with no ears and no heart, and there he died,” said the monkey. “I am not that ass.”

Sanskrit verse 4.33

What this story shows

Having seen the danger once is no protection for one who wants to be persuaded.

The ass has every piece of knowledge he needs. He has seen the lion and felt the paw. What brings him back is a story that fits what he wants, told by someone who calls him uncle.

The jackal’s last lie is the neatest. He explains his own theft by the ass’s foolishness, and the lion accepts it because the ass’s return can hardly be explained any other way.

  • A second invitation from the one who led you into danger deserves no hearing.
  • Flattery and a promise aimed at one’s wishes can outweigh what one has seen.
  • The clever servant in this story cheats his master as easily as his victim.

A lion was hurt in a fight with an elephant and could not hunt. His servant the jackal grew thin with hunger, and the lion sent him to find some animal that could be killed even so.

By a village pond the jackal found an ass, worn out by a hard master. He called him uncle and promised a place of green grass by a river, with three she-asses waiting for a husband. The ass went with him.

At the sight of the lion the ass turned and ran, and the lion’s blow missed. The jackal scolded his master and promised to bring the ass once more.

The jackal found the ass grazing where he had been. He said the paw had been a she-ass reaching out to embrace him, and that she would die without him. The ass believed it and went back.

This time the lion was ready, and the ass did not escape. While the lion bathed, the jackal ate the ears and the heart. He told his angry master that this ass never had ears or a heart, or he would not have come back, and the lion believed him.

What this story shows

Having seen the danger once is no protection for one who wants to be persuaded.

  • A second invitation from the one who led you into danger deserves no hearing.
  • Flattery and a promise aimed at one’s wishes can outweigh what one has seen.
  • The clever servant in this story cheats his master as easily as his victim.
