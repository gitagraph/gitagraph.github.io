The scholars who made a lion

The gold-finder tells this to his friend, who went on past the gold and now carries the wheel.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived four brahmin’s sons who were close friends. Three of them had mastered every science and had no sense. The fourth had sense and no taste for books. One day the friends said to one another, “Learning is no use if one does not go abroad, please kings with it and earn a fortune. Let us go to the eastern country.”

When they had gone some way, the eldest said, “One of us four is a dunce. He has sense and nothing else, and no king gives gifts for sense without learning. We will not share with him what we earn. Let him go home.”

The second said, “Subuddhi, go home, since you have no learning.” The third said, “This is not right. We have played together since we were children. Let him come, and he shall have an equal share of what we earn.

Fortune kept for oneself alone, like a wife, is of little use. It should be like a courtesan, open to every traveller. The small-minded count who is their own and who is a stranger, and to the generous the whole earth is family. Let him come too.”

Sanskrit verses 5.36, 5.37

So they went on together, and in a forest they saw the bones of a dead lion. One of them said, “Now we can put our learning to the proof. Here lies some dead creature. Let us bring it to life by the power of our knowledge. I will put the bones together.” He did so eagerly. The second added skin, flesh and blood.

The third was about to give it breath when Subuddhi stopped him. “Wait, sir. This is a lion you are making. If you bring him to life he will kill every one of us.” The third said, “Fool, I will not let my learning go to waste.” Subuddhi said, “Then wait a moment while I climb this tree.”

He climbed it. The lion was brought to life, and he rose up and killed all three. Subuddhi came down from the tree and went home.

The gold-finder said to the wheel-bearer, “That is why I say that sense is better than learning and stands above it. Those who have no sense perish, like the men who made the lion.”

Sanskrit verse 5.35

What this story shows

The one without learning asks the only question that matters, and then climbs a tree.

Each scholar does his own part perfectly. None of them asks what the finished thing will be. The fourth has no skill to add, and he is the only one who looks ahead.

He also does not waste time arguing. When he cannot stop them, he gets out of the way. The gold-finder’s point to his friend is the same: knowing how to do a thing is different from knowing whether to do it.

  • Skill without judgment built the thing that killed them.
  • The friend they nearly sent home was the one who saw the danger.
  • When a warning is refused, stepping aside is also sense.

Four friends set out to earn a fortune from kings. Three were learned and had no sense, and the fourth had sense and no learning. Two wanted to send him home, and the third insisted he should share alike.

In a forest they found the bones of a lion. One scholar joined the bones, one added flesh and skin, and the third prepared to give it life. The fourth warned them and was called a fool, so he asked them to wait while he climbed a tree.

The lion came to life and rose up, and the three scholars did not survive it.

The fourth came down from the tree and went home. The gold-finder says that sense stands above learning.
