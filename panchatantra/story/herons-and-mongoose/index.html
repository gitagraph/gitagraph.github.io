The herons and the mongoose

The king's officers tell this to Dharmabuddhi, after Papabuddhi's plan has ended in the burning tree.

“A wise person thinks of the plan, and thinks of what could go wrong with it too,” said the king's officers. “The foolish heron looked on while the mongoose ate his young.” “How was that?” asked Dharmabuddhi.

In a forest stood a banyan tree full of herons. A black snake lived in a hollow of its trunk. It spent its days eating the heron chicks, even before their wings had grown.

One heron saw that his young had been eaten. Sick with grief, he went down to the edge of a lake and stood there with his head bowed and his eyes full of tears.

A crab saw him. “Uncle, why are you weeping like this today?”

“Friend, what am I to do? I have no luck. The snake that lives in the hollow has eaten my children, and I weep for them. Tell me if there is any way to destroy it.”

The crab thought, “This creature is the born enemy of my kind. I will give him advice that is half true and half false, so that all the other herons are destroyed as well. Speak to an enemy with a voice like butter and a heart without pity, and advise him so that he dies with all his line.”

“Uncle,” he said aloud, “if that is so, drop pieces of fish in a trail from the mouth of a mongoose's burrow to the snake's hollow. The mongoose will follow the trail and finish that wicked snake.”

The heron did it. The mongoose followed the fish and killed the black snake. Then, one by one, it ate every heron that lived in that tree.

“That is why we say,” the officers finished, “think of the plan, and think of what could go wrong with it too.”

What this story shows

A plan can do exactly what you asked and still destroy you.

The crab's advice worked. That is what makes it dangerous. The trail of fish brought the mongoose, and the mongoose killed the snake, just as promised. The heron had asked one question, how to be rid of the snake, and he got a true answer to it. He did not ask what a mongoose does next, or why a crab should care about a heron's children. He was grieving, and he took help from the first one who offered. The crab says in so many words what he is doing: a soft voice, a hard heart, and advice shaped to ruin.

The king's officers tell this to Dharmabuddhi over the body of Papabuddhi's father. They mean Papabuddhi, whose plan worked at every step until it put his father inside a burning tree. It is worth setting beside the earlier story of the crows, who had the same snake in the same kind of tree. They asked a friend, and their helpers went home when the job was done. The story shows that the same plan is safe or deadly depending on who gave it to you.

  • Ask what your helper will do after the job is done.
  • Advice from someone with a reason to hate you needs checking, most of all when it is good.
  • Grief is a bad hour for choosing an adviser.
