The brahmin who gave half his life

The monkey tells this after the crocodile admits he acted at his wife’s word.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived a brahmin whose wife was dearer to him than his life. She quarrelled with his family every day and gave them no rest. He could not bear the quarrelling, and out of love for her he left his family and set out with her for a distant country.

In the middle of a great forest she said, “My lord, I am tormented by thirst. Find some water.” He went, and when he came back with water he found her dead.

He loved her dearly, and as he wept he heard a voice from the sky. “Brahmin, if you give half of your own life, your wife will live.” He purified himself and gave half his life with a threefold word. As he spoke, she came back to life. They drank the water, ate fruit from the forest and went on.

In time they came to the edge of a city and went into a flower garden. The brahmin said, “My dear, wait here until I come back with food,” and he went into the city.

In the garden a lame man was working the water wheel and singing in a voice of rare sweetness. She heard him and was seized with desire. She went up to him and told him that if he did not love her, her death would be on his head.

He said, “I am a sick man. I am no use to you.” She said that made no difference and that she must be with him, and he did as she wished. Afterward she said, “From today I have given myself to you for life. Come with us.” He agreed.

The brahmin came back with food and sat down to eat with her. She said, “This lame man is hungry. Give him a little too.”

When that was done she said, “You have no companion, and when you go off to another village I have no one even to talk to. Let us take this lame man with us.” He said, “I can hardly carry myself, let alone him.” She said, “I will carry him in a basket.” Her words took him in, and he agreed.

Another day the brahmin was resting beside a well. His wife, whose heart was now with the lame man, pushed him, and he fell in. She took up the basket and went on into a city.

The king’s men, who were on the watch for goods carried past the toll, saw the basket on her head, took it from her by force and brought it to the king. The king opened it and found the lame man. The woman had followed, weeping, and the king asked her what this meant.

She said, “This is my husband. He is sick, and his kinsmen drove him out. I love him, so I carried him to your city on my head.” The king said, “You are a sister to me. Take two villages, and live in comfort with your husband.”

By good fortune a kind man pulled the brahmin out of the well, and his wandering brought him to that same city. His wife saw him and told the king, “This man is my husband’s enemy.” The king ordered him put to death.

The brahmin said, “Lord, this woman is holding something of mine. If you love justice, have her give it back.” The king told her to return whatever she had of his. She said she had taken nothing. The brahmin said, “I gave you half my life with a threefold word. Give that back.”

In fear of the king she repeated, there and then, that she returned the half-life given with a threefold word, and with the words her life left her. The king was amazed and asked what had happened, and the brahmin told him the whole story.

The monkey said to the crocodile, “He gave up his family and half his life for her, and she left him all the same. You began this business for a wife’s sake too.”

What this story shows

What he gave could be asked for again, and when it was, the truth came out.

The brahmin gives up his family and then half his life, and each time he asks for nothing back. His wife’s wishes are the only measure he uses, and they lead him to the edge of a well.

The ending turns on a plain claim made in front of a judge. He does not accuse her. He asks for what is his, and her own answer settles the matter. The monkey draws a wider lesson about wives from this. The story itself is about one wife and one husband.

  • He gives everything away without once weighing what he is told.
  • A lie told to a king holds only until the wronged person is allowed to speak.
  • The monkey applies the tale to the crocodile, who also acted at a wife’s word.

A brahmin loved his wife more than his life. She quarrelled with his family every day, and for her sake he left them and set out with her for a distant country.

In a forest she died of thirst before he could bring water. A voice from the sky said she would live if he gave half his own life, and he gave it with a threefold word. She came back to life.

In a garden near a city she heard a lame man singing at the water wheel, and she gave her heart to him.

When her husband came back with food, she asked him to take the man along, and she carried him in a basket.

One day, as her husband rested beside a well, she pushed him in and went on with the basket. The king’s men seized it and took it to the king. She said the man inside was her sick husband, and the king gave her two villages.

A kind man pulled the brahmin out, and he came to that city. His wife called him her husband’s enemy, and the king ordered his death. The brahmin asked only for the half-life he had given her, and as she spoke the words to return it she died.

The king was amazed, and the brahmin told him the whole story. The monkey tells this to the crocodile, who also acted at his wife’s word.
