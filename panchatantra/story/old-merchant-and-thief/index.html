The old merchant, his young wife and the thief

Diptaksha, the third of the owl king’s ministers to speak, tells this to Arimardana. He argues that the wounded crow should be spared, since even an enemy can do one a good turn.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived an old merchant named Kamatura. His wife had died. Desire had carried off his good sense, and he married the daughter of a poor merchant, giving a great deal of money for her.

The girl was sunk in unhappiness and could not bear even to look at the old merchant. The text says this is no wonder, and adds two hard sayings about old age. The whiteness of the hair on a man’s head is where contempt for him settles, and young women keep as far from him as people keep from an outcaste’s well marked with a piece of bone.

Sanskrit verse 3.188

In old age the body shrinks, the step falters, the teeth are lost, the sight wanders, the looks are spoiled and the mouth dribbles. His relations stop doing what he says, his wife does not wait on him, and even his son looks down on a man overcome by age.

Sanskrit verse 3.189

One night she was lying on the same bed with her face turned away from him when a thief came into the house. She saw the thief, was thrown into fear and clung tightly to her husband, old as he was.

The merchant was amazed, and a thrill went through his whole body. He wondered why she was holding him today. He looked carefully and saw the thief in a corner of the house. Then he understood that she was surely holding him out of fear of that man.

Knowing this, he said to the thief, “She who always shrinks from me holds me close today. You have done me a kindness, and I wish you well. Take whatever I have.”

Sanskrit verse 3.186

The thief answered, “I see nothing of yours worth taking. If there is ever something to take, I will come again, provided she is not holding you.”

Sanskrit verse 3.187

“So people think well even of a thief who does them a good turn,” said Diptaksha. “How much more of one who has come asking for shelter.”

What this story shows

The merchant got one embrace, and he knew exactly why. He thanked the thief anyway.

The marriage is told in two lines and the text does not soften it. A rich old man paid for a bride from a poor house, and she was miserable. The sayings about old age are harsh, and they are given as the reason for her turning away.

The joke is gentle by comparison. The husband sees at once that it was fear that moved her, and he is grateful all the same. Diptaksha uses it in the owls’ debate: a good turn counts, whoever does it. The thief’s dry answer keeps the story honest about what the merchant really had.

  • The wife had no say in the marriage, and her unhappiness is stated plainly.
  • The merchant understood why she held him and did not fool himself.
  • The thief was offered everything and found nothing he wanted.

An old merchant whose wife had died married the daughter of a poor merchant, paying a great deal of money for her.

The girl was unhappy and could not bear even to look at him. At night she lay with her face turned away.

One night a thief came into the house. In her fright she clung tightly to her husband. He was amazed, until he looked round and saw the thief in the corner.

He thanked the thief and told him to take whatever he liked. The thief answered that there was nothing worth taking, and that he would come again if she ever stopped holding him. The owl minister tells this to say that even a thief can do one a good turn.
