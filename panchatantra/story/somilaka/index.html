Somilaka the weaver and his gold

Mantharaka the tortoise tells this to Hiranyaka the mouse, who has lost his hoard. He wants the mouse to stop grieving over wealth he was never going to enjoy.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

In a certain town lived a weaver named Somilaka. He made cloth with many kinds of fine work in it, fit for kings. Even so, he never earned more than his food and clothes. Other weavers in the town, who could make only coarse cloth, were rich. Seeing them, he said to his wife, “Look at these makers of coarse cloth and all their gold. This place does not feed me. I am going elsewhere to earn.”

“It is a mistake to think wealth is found by going away and cannot be found at home,” said his wife. “What is not meant to happen does not happen. What is meant to happen comes without effort. A calf finds its mother among a thousand cows, and in the same way a deed done before finds the one who did it.”

Sanskrit verses 2.129, 2.130

“That is not right, my dear,” said the weaver. “No deed bears fruit without effort. One hand cannot clap. Even food that fate puts on the plate will not reach the mouth unless the hand lifts it. Deer do not walk into the mouth of a sleeping lion. I must go to another country.”

Sanskrit verses 2.133, 2.134, 2.136

He went to the city of Vardhamanapura, stayed three years and earned three hundred gold coins. Then he set out for home. Halfway there, in a forest, the sun went down. For fear of wild animals he climbed onto the thick trunk of a banyan tree and slept. At midnight he heard two fearsome figures talking to each other.

“Doer,” said one, “you know very well that this Somilaka is to have nothing beyond food and clothes. Why did you give him three hundred gold coins?” “Deed,” said the other, “I have to give to those who work. What becomes of it afterwards is in your hands.” The weaver woke and looked at the knot where he kept his gold. It was empty.

Wealth earned with such hard work had gone just like that. He could not show his face to his wife and friends with empty hands. So he went back to the same city, and in a single year he earned five hundred gold coins and set out again. Halfway, in the forest, the sun went down once more. This time he was afraid of losing the gold. Tired as he was, he did not rest and walked on fast.

Then he saw the same two figures coming and heard them talk. “Doer, why did you give him five hundred gold coins? You know he has nothing beyond food and clothes.” “Deed, I have to give to those who work. What follows is in your hands. Why blame me?” Somilaka looked at his knot. The gold was gone.

He was crushed. He saw no use in living without wealth, and there under the banyan he decided to end his life. He was about to do it when a figure standing in the sky spoke. “Somilaka, do not do this rash thing. It is I who take your wealth. I will not let you keep one cowrie beyond food and clothes. Go home. Still, your daring has pleased me, and seeing me should not be for nothing. Ask for whatever boon you wish.”

“Then give me great wealth,” said Somilaka. “What will you do with wealth you cannot enjoy?” asked the figure. “Let me have it all the same,” said Somilaka. “A man with a store of wealth is served by everyone, even if he is mean and of low birth. And a jackal once said this. Loose, yet firmly fixed. Will they fall or will they not? I have watched them, my dear, for fifteen years.” “What is that?” asked the figure.

“Then go back to Vardhamanapura,” said the figure. “Two merchants’ sons live there. One is named Guptadhana, Hidden Wealth. The other is named Upabhuktadhana, Enjoyed Wealth. See how each of them lives, and then ask to be like one of them. You may have wealth that is kept and never used, or wealth that is given and enjoyed.” With that he vanished.

Somilaka went back to Vardhamanapura in wonder. He reached the city tired, at evening, asked his way to the house of Guptadhana and found it with difficulty after sunset. Guptadhana, his wife and his son scolded him, but he pushed his way in and sat down. At mealtime he too was given a little food, without any kindness. He ate and slept there.

At midnight he saw the same two figures talking. “Doer, why have you put this extra cost on Guptadhana, that he had to feed Somilaka? That was wrong.” “Deed, the fault is not mine. I have to see that a man receives what comes to him. What follows is in your hands.” In the morning Guptadhana was doubled up with a sickness of the stomach, and the next day he had to go without food because of it.

That morning Somilaka left and went to the house of Upabhuktadhana. This man rose to greet him and honoured him with food and clothes. Somilaka slept in his house on a fine bed.

At midnight the two figures were there again. “Doer, this one has spent a great deal on Somilaka, and he borrowed all of it from the moneylender. How is the debt to be paid?” “Deed, that is my work. What follows is in your hands.” At dawn a king’s man came with money, a gift of the king’s favour, and handed it to Upabhuktadhana.

Somilaka saw this and thought, “He has no savings, and still he is better off than that miser Guptadhana. The use of wealth is to give it and enjoy it. Let fate make me one whose wealth is given and enjoyed. I have no use for hidden wealth.” And that is what Somilaka became.

Sanskrit verses 2.139, 2.150

“So I say,” the tortoise told the mouse, “an unlucky man may earn wealth and still not get to enjoy it, as Somilaka found in the great forest.”

Sanskrit verse 2.127

What this story shows

Somilaka could earn gold and could not keep it. Shown a hoarder and a giver side by side, he chose to be the one who gives and enjoys.

The story sets two ways of thinking side by side. Somilaka’s wife holds that what is not meant for a person will not come. Somilaka holds that nothing comes without work. The story lets both be right. His work does earn the gold, and the gold still goes.

It then shows two men with money. One keeps it buried and grudges a guest his supper. The other spends borrowed money on a stranger, and the king’s gift arrives in the morning. Somilaka had asked simply for a great deal of wealth. After seeing the two houses he changes what he asks for.

  • Effort earned the gold both times, and something else decided whether he kept it.
  • Wealth that stayed hidden and unused left its owner poor in every way that showed.
  • After seeing both houses, Somilaka asked for wealth to give and enjoy.

Somilaka wove cloth fit for kings and never earned more than his food and clothes. Makers of coarse cloth in the same town were rich. He told his wife that the place did not feed him and he would go elsewhere to earn.

His wife said that what is meant for a person comes without effort, and what is not meant does not come. Somilaka said no deed bears fruit without effort, as one hand cannot clap. He left for another country.

In three years he earned three hundred gold coins and set out for home. At night in a forest he slept in a banyan tree and heard two fearsome figures, Doer and Deed. Deed asked why Somilaka had been given gold, Doer said he must give to those who work, and when the weaver woke the gold was gone.

He could not go home with empty hands. He went back, earned five hundred gold coins in a year and set out again. This time he did not stop to rest in the forest.

The same two figures came by and said the same words. He looked, and the gold was gone again. Somilaka was crushed and saw no use in living without wealth.

There under the banyan he decided to end his life. A figure in the sky stopped him and said that it was he who took Somilaka’s wealth and would allow him nothing beyond food and clothes. He was pleased by the weaver’s daring all the same, and offered him a boon.

Somilaka asked for great wealth, even wealth he could never enjoy. The figure sent him back to the city to see two merchants’ sons, Hidden Wealth and Enjoyed Wealth. He was to watch how each lived and then ask to be like one of them.

The man called Hidden Wealth scolded him and gave him a little food without kindness. That night Doer and Deed spoke of the cost of that meal. In the morning the host was sick and had to go without food the next day.

The man called Enjoyed Wealth welcomed him with food, clothes and a fine bed, all on borrowed money. That night Deed asked how the debt would be paid. At dawn a king’s man arrived with a gift of money from the king.

Somilaka saw that the man with no savings was better off than the miser. He asked to be one whose wealth is given and enjoyed, and so he became. The tortoise told the mouse this to say that an unlucky man may earn wealth and still not enjoy it.
