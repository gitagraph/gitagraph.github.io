Dharmabuddhi and Papabuddhi

Karataka tells this to Damanaka during the fight, to show him what comes of a plan that counts only the gain.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

“I know them both, Dharmabuddhi and Papabuddhi,” said Karataka. “A son was too clever for his own good, and his father died in the smoke.” “How was that?” asked Damanaka.

Sanskrit verse 1.429

Two friends lived in a certain town. One was Dharmabuddhi, whose name means a mind set on what is right. The other was Papabuddhi, a mind set on wrong.

One day Papabuddhi thought, “I have no brains and no money. Let me take Dharmabuddhi abroad and earn a fortune on the strength of his. Then I will cheat him as well, and live in comfort.”

So he said to his friend, “When you are old, what will you have to remember? If you have never seen another country, what will you have to tell anyone? A man who has not wandered the earth and learned its many tongues and ways was born for nothing.”

Sanskrit verse 1.430

Dharmabuddhi was delighted. He took leave of his elders, and on a lucky day the two set out. Abroad, thanks to Dharmabuddhi's ability, Papabuddhi made a great deal of money. Then, rich and happy, they turned eagerly for home.

As they came near their town, Papabuddhi said, “Friend, we should not carry all this money home. Our families and relatives will ask for it. Let us bury it here in the thick of the forest and take only a little with us. When we need more, we will come together and fetch it. A wise man shows his wealth to nobody. Even a sage's mind wavers at the sight of it.”

Sanskrit verse 1.433

“Let us do that,” said Dharmabuddhi. They buried the money, went home and lived in comfort.

Then one midnight Papabuddhi went into the forest, took every coin, filled the pit again and went home.

Some days later he came to Dharmabuddhi. “Friend, we both have large families and we are short of money. Let us go and fetch a little.” “Let us do that,” said Dharmabuddhi.

They went to the place and dug. The pot was empty. Papabuddhi beat his head. “Dharmabuddhi, you took this money. Nobody else could have. Look, the pit was filled in again. Give me my half, or I will go to the king's court.”

“Wicked man, do not say such a thing. I am Dharmabuddhi. I do not do a thief's work. One whose mind is set on what is right looks on another's goods as a clod of earth.”

Sanskrit verse 1.435

Still quarrelling, they went before the judge and accused each other. The officers of the court were about to put them to the ordeal when Papabuddhi spoke. “This is not the proper way. In a dispute one looks first for a document, then for witnesses. Only when there are no witnesses do the wise call for an ordeal. And I have witnesses: the spirits of the trees. They will say which of us is the thief.”

Sanskrit verse 1.436

“That is well said,” the officers answered. “Where there is any witness at all, an ordeal is out of place, and all the more when the witnesses are forest spirits. We are curious to see this ourselves. At dawn you will both come with us to that place.”

Sanskrit verse 1.437

Papabuddhi went home and spoke to his father. “Father, I have stolen a great sum from Dharmabuddhi. One word from you will make it safe. Without it, the money will be lost and our lives with it.” “Son, tell me quickly what to say,” said his father.

“There is a great shami tree at that spot, with a large hollow in it. Go and get inside it now. At dawn, when I call on the truth, you must say: Dharmabuddhi is the thief.”

So it was arranged. At dawn Papabuddhi bathed and went to the shami tree, with Dharmabuddhi ahead of him and the officers of the court. He called out in a loud voice. “Sun and moon, wind and fire, sky, earth and water, the heart and the god of death, day and night and both twilights: these know what a man has done. Blessed spirit of the forest, say which of us two is the thief.”

Sanskrit verse 1.438

From inside the hollow his father answered, “Dharmabuddhi took the money.”

The king's men stared in wonder. They were turning to the law books for the penalty that fitted Dharmabuddhi's theft when Dharmabuddhi piled dry stuff round the hollow and set it alight.

As the hollow blazed, Papabuddhi's father came out of it, half burnt, his eyes ruined, crying pitifully. “What is this?” they all asked. “All this wrong was Papabuddhi's doing,” he said, and died.

The king's men hanged Papabuddhi from a branch of that shami tree. They praised Dharmabuddhi.

“It is well said,” they told him. “A wise person thinks of the plan, and thinks of what could go wrong with it too. The foolish heron looked on while the mongoose ate his young.” “How was that?” asked Dharmabuddhi. And they told him.

“So it is with you, fool,” said Karataka. “Like Papabuddhi, you thought of your plan and never of the harm in it. You are no good man. You are a Papabuddhi. I know it, because you have put our master's life in danger.”

What this story shows

Think through where a clever plan can end, before someone else pays for it.

Every step of Papabuddhi's scheme sounds like good sense. Do not carry all the money home. Do not show wealth to anyone. Look for witnesses before an ordeal. He quotes the right rule each time, and each rule moves the money closer to him. The officers are no better. They are ready to believe a voice from a tree, and they reach for the law books before they have looked inside the trunk. Dharmabuddhi does not argue. He tests the witness with fire, and the text lets him: a father dies and a son is hanged, and the court praises the man who lit it.

Karataka tells this to Damanaka while the lion and the bull are tearing at each other. His charge is exact. Damanaka, like Papabuddhi, thought only of how his plan would work, and never of where it could end. Notice what Karataka is afraid of. It is the lion's life, and their own place beside him. The bull, who is dying of the same plan, does not come into it.

  • Ask what a plan will cost if it works, as well as whether it will work.
  • A rule quoted by the person it benefits deserves a second look.
  • Before you believe a witness, find out who is standing behind it.

Papabuddhi was poor. He took his friend Dharmabuddhi abroad to grow rich on his friend's ability, and meant to cheat him afterwards.

They made their fortune. Near home Papabuddhi said, “Let us bury it in the forest and take only a little with us.”

At midnight he came back alone and took it all. Later, at the empty pit, he accused Dharmabuddhi of the theft and took him to court.

Before the judges he named the spirits of the trees as his witness. That night he hid his own father in the hollow of the tree.

At dawn he called out, “Spirit of the forest, which of us is the thief?” A voice from the tree said, “Dharmabuddhi.”

Dharmabuddhi set fire round the hollow. The father came out, said it was all Papabuddhi's doing, and died. The king's men hanged Papabuddhi from that tree.
