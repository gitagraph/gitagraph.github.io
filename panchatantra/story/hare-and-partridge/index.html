The hare, Kapinjala and the cat

The same crow tells this to the birds, as something he saw himself. He is warning them against putting a mean ruler over themselves.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

The crow who tells this story once lived in a certain tree. Below him, in a hollow of the same tree, lived a sparrow named Kapinjala. Every evening at sunset the two came home, and the hours passed in great happiness. They traded fine sayings, told the old stories of the sages of the gods, the brahmin sages and the royal sages, and described the many strange things each had seen on his wanderings.

One day Kapinjala went off with other sparrows to look for food in another region, where the rice stood ripe. Night came and he had not returned. The crow grew anxious and sad at being parted from him. He wondered why Kapinjala had not come, and whether he had been caught in a snare or killed by someone. He was sure that if his friend were well he would not stay away.

Many days went by in this worry. Then one evening at sunset a hare named Shighraga, Swift-Goer, came and went into the hollow. The crow had given up hope of Kapinjala, so he did not stop him.

Some time later Kapinjala remembered his home and came back, very plump from eating rice. It is well said that living beings are not as happy even in heaven as they are in their own country, their own town and their own house, even in poverty.

Sanskrit verse 3.92

He saw the hare inside the hollow and spoke sharply. “Hare, you have not done well. You have come into my dwelling. Get out at once.” The hare said, “This house is mine. It is yours no longer. Do not say harsh things that are untrue.”

“The rule is this. Once a pond, a well, a tank, a temple or a tree has been given up, the old owner cannot claim it again. Where someone has openly used a field or the like for ten years, the use itself is the proof, and neither a witness nor a written deed is needed. That is the rule the sages gave for humans. For beasts and birds, a place is theirs only for as long as they live in it. So this house is mine.”

Sanskrit verses 3.93, 3.94

Kapinjala said, “If you go by the law books, come with me. We will ask someone who recites the law, and whoever he gives it to shall have it.” The hare agreed, and they set out. The crow wondered how it would end and wanted to see the judgment, so he followed them out of curiosity.

Meanwhile a wild cat named Tikshnadamshtra, Sharp-Tooth, had heard of their quarrel. He went to the riverbank beside their road and took up a holy man’s pose. He held a blade of sacred grass, shut his eyes, raised his arms, stood with only half a foot touching the ground, turned his face to the sun and began to preach.

“This world has no substance,” he said. “Life breaks in a moment. The company of those we love is like a dream, and the ties of family are like a conjuror’s show. There is no refuge except right living. Bodies do not last, wealth does not stay and death is always near, so one should gather merit. A person whose days come and go without right living breathes like a blacksmith’s bellows and is not alive. Learning without it is as useless as a dog’s tail, which neither covers the dog decently nor drives off the flies. Those who do not live by it are like empty husks among grain, winged ants among birds and mosquitoes among living creatures.”

Sanskrit verse 3.96

“Flower and fruit are better than the tree, ghee is better than curd, oil is better than the oilcake, and right living is the best part of a human being. Those who live without it are made only to eat and to pass what they eat, and they serve others’ ends like cattle. The wise praise taking one’s time in every task, but right living meets many obstacles and should not be put off. It can be said in a few words. To help others is merit, and to hurt others is sin. Hear the whole of it and keep it in mind: do not do to others what you would not want done to yourself.”

Sanskrit verses 3.100, 3.101

The hare heard this sermon and said, “Kapinjala, there is an ascetic on the riverbank who speaks of right living. Let us ask him.” Kapinjala said, “He is our enemy by nature. Let us ask from a distance, in case his vow gives way.” So they stood far off and called, “Holy teacher, we have a dispute. Settle it for us by the law books. Whoever is in the wrong, you may eat.”

“Good sirs, do not say such a thing,” said the cat. “I have turned back from violence, which is the road to hell. Doing no harm is the path of right living. It rests on care for every creature, so even lice, bugs and gnats should be protected. One who harms even creatures that do harm is without pity and goes to a dreadful hell, and one who harms the gentle fares worse still.”

Sanskrit verses 3.105, 3.106

“Even the priests who kill animals in sacrifices are fools,” he went on. “They do not know the true meaning of the scripture. It says to sacrifice with aja. That word means rice seven years old. It is not the name of an animal. If cutting down trees, killing animals and making a mud of blood were the way to heaven, there would be no way left to hell.”

Sanskrit verse 3.107

“So I will eat neither of you. I will only decide who wins and who loses. But I am old and cannot hear well from far away. Come close and state your case in front of me. Then I can understand it truly and give a ruling that does not harm me in the next world. A judge who rules wrongly out of pride, greed, anger or fear goes to hell. A lie about an animal destroys five of one’s kin, a lie about a cow ten, a lie about a girl a hundred, and a lie about a man a thousand. One who sits in the court and does not speak plainly, or does not declare the truth, should be shunned from afar. So trust me, and tell it clearly close to my ear.”

To be brief, that mean creature won their trust so completely that they came and sat in his lap. In the same instant he pinned one with his paw and caught the other in the saw of his teeth. They died, and he ate them.

“That is why I say,” the crow told the birds, “that the hare and Kapinjala went looking for justice, came before a mean lord, and both were destroyed.”

Sanskrit verse 3.91

What this story shows

Both had a fair case to argue. They carried it to a judge who was their natural enemy, and neither came back.

The quarrel itself is an ordinary one. One left his home empty for a long time, and the other moved in. Each quotes a rule, and each rule has some sense in it. The trouble begins when they look for someone to decide.

The cat says everything a good judge should say. His words about harmlessness and truth are sound, and he means none of it. Kapinjala saw the danger and said so, and the two still walked closer, a step at a time. The crow’s warning to the birds is about whom they give power over themselves.

  • Both sides had rules to quote, and neither rule saved them.
  • Kapinjala named the danger and then set his own caution aside.
  • The cat’s teaching was true, and he used it as bait.

A crow lived in a tree, and a sparrow lived in a hollow at its foot. Every evening the two friends sat and talked. One day the sparrow flew off to feed where the rice was ripe, and did not come back.

Many days passed, and the crow gave up hope of his friend. One evening a hare came by and made the empty hollow his home.

The sparrow came home, fat from the rice, and told the hare to get out. The hare said that a home left empty belongs to whoever is living in it. They agreed to take the quarrel to someone who knew the law.

A wild cat heard of the quarrel. He stood by the river like a holy man, arms raised to the sun, and preached kindness to every creature. The crow followed to see what would happen.

The two stood far off and asked him to judge between them. The cat said he had given up all harm, and that he was too old to hear from so far. He asked them to come close and speak into his ear.

Little by little they trusted him. In the end the hare and the sparrow were sitting in the cat’s lap.

In one moment he caught them both, and that was the end of them. The crow saw it all. He tells the birds that this is what comes of taking a mean creature for a judge.
