The lion, the jackal and the talking cave

Raktaksha tells this to his own followers. The owl king will not listen to him, and he is asking them to leave the cave with him before harm comes.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain part of the forest lived a lion named Kharanakhara, Sharp-Claws. Once he wandered this way and that, his throat thin with hunger, and found no creature at all.

At sunset he came to a large cave in the hillside and went in. He thought, “Some creature is sure to come to this cave at night. I will stay here quietly, out of sight.”

Meanwhile the owner of the cave, a jackal named Dadhipuccha, Curd-Tail, came home. He looked, and saw a line of lion’s tracks going into the cave and none coming out. He thought, “I am done for. There must be a lion inside. I need some way to find out for certain.”

So he stood at the entrance and began to call, “Ho, cave. Ho, cave.” Then he fell silent, and after a while he spoke again in the same way. “You seem to have forgotten the agreement between us. When I come back from outside I am to call to you, and you are to invite me in. If you do not call me, I shall go to another cave.”

The lion heard this and thought, “This cave must always call out to him when he comes home. Today it says nothing for fear of me. It is well said that when the mind is shaken by fear, hands and feet will not work, speech will not come and the trembling grows. I will call him myself. He will come in at the sound and be my meal.”

Sanskrit verse 3.218

Having settled this, the lion called to him. The cave filled with the echo of the lion’s voice, and it frightened forest creatures even far away. The jackal ran, and as he ran he recited this verse: “One who acts before trouble comes does well, and one who does not comes to grief. I have grown old living in this forest, and I have never once heard a cave speak.”

Sanskrit verse 3.217

“Take this to heart,” said Raktaksha, “and come away with me.”

What this story shows

The jackal did not know what was inside. He put a question to the cave that only an intruder would answer.

The jackal reads the ground before he goes in. The tracks tell him enough to be afraid and too little to be sure, so he finds a safe way to test it. He invents a custom and waits to see who will keep it.

The lion is undone by being clever. He reasons that the cave must be silent out of fear, and he supplies the answer himself. Raktaksha tells the story as he walks away from a king who will not be warned. His point is the jackal’s: act before the danger arrives, and trust small signs.

  • Tracks that went in and did not come out were enough to stop the jackal.
  • He tested his fear from a safe distance before acting on it.
  • The lion gave himself away by answering for the cave.

A hungry lion had found nothing to eat all day. At sunset he came to a cave and hid inside, sure that some creature would come home to it at night.

The jackal who lived there came back and saw a lion’s tracks going in and none coming out. He stood outside and called to the cave. He said it always answered him, and that he would go elsewhere if it did not.

The lion thought the cave was silent only for fear of him, so he called out himself. His roar filled the cave and frightened creatures far away.

The jackal ran, saying that in all his years in the forest he had never heard a cave speak. The owl minister tells this to his own followers and asks them to leave with him before harm comes.
