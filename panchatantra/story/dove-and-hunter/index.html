The dove, his mate and the hunter

Kruraksha, another of the owl king’s ministers, tells this to Arimardana. He is answering Raktaksha: one who comes asking for shelter is not to be killed.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

There was once a fowler, mean in his ways and like death itself to living things, who roamed a great forest. He had no friend, no relation and no kinsman. All of them had left him because of his cruel work. People who are cruel and take the lives of creatures are feared by every being, as beasts of prey are feared.

Sanskrit verses 3.135, 3.136, 3.137

Every day he took a cage, a snare and a club and went into the forest, doing harm to every creature. One day as he wandered there a female dove fell into his hands, and he put her in the cage.

Sanskrit verses 3.138, 3.139

While he was still in the forest, every quarter of the sky turned black with clouds. A great wind and rain came down, as if the world were ending. He was frightened. Shivering again and again and looking for shelter, he reached a great tree.

Sanskrit verses 3.140, 3.141

For a moment the sky cleared and showed its stars. He came under the tree and said, “Whoever lives here, I come to him for shelter. Let him protect me. I am broken by the cold and faint with hunger.”

Sanskrit verses 3.142, 3.143

On a branch of that tree a dove had lived for a long time. That night he was without his wife, and he was lamenting in great sorrow. “There has been a great storm of wind and rain, and my dear one has not come. Without her this house of mine is empty today.

Sanskrit verses 3.144, 3.145

A man whose wife is devoted to him, lives for him and cares for his good is blessed on earth. A house is not what people call a home. The wife is called the home, and a house without her is thought no better than a wilderness.”

Sanskrit verses 3.146, 3.147

His wife, in the cage, heard his sorrowful words and was glad of them. She said, “She is no wife whose husband takes no joy in her. When her husband is pleased with a woman, all the gods are pleased with her. A father gives in measure, a brother gives in measure and a son gives in measure. A husband gives without measure, and every wife honours him.”

Sanskrit verses 3.148, 3.149, 3.151

She went on, “Listen well, my dear, to what I say for your good. One who has come to you for shelter must be protected, even at the cost of your life. This fowler lies here under your roof, suffering from cold and hunger. Do him honour.

Sanskrit verses 3.152, 3.153

It is said that when a guest arrives in the evening and is not honoured as well as one is able, he leaves his own wrongs with the householder and carries off the householder’s merit.”

Sanskrit verse 3.154

“And do not hate him because he has caught the one you love. I am bound by my own deeds from before. Poverty, sickness, sorrow, captivity and misfortune are the fruits of the tree of our own faults. So let go of the anger my capture has caused you. Set your mind on what is right, and honour him as a guest should be honoured.”

Sanskrit verses 3.155, 3.156, 3.157

The dove heard her words, which were full of right and reason. He went up to the fowler humbly and said, “Welcome, good sir. Tell me what I may do for you. Do not be troubled. You are in your own house.” The fowler answered, “Dove, I am very cold. Give me some protection from the cold.”

Sanskrit verses 3.158, 3.159, 3.160

The dove went and brought a live coal, and soon had a fire burning in dry leaves. When it was blazing well he said to his guest, “Warm your limbs here, at ease and without fear.”

Sanskrit verses 3.161, 3.162

“We who live in the forest live on whatever comes our way. I have no wealth with which to end your hunger. One person feeds a thousand, another a hundred, another ten. I have little merit and am a small creature, and I can hardly feed myself.

Sanskrit verses 3.163, 3.164

If someone cannot give food to even one guest, there is little point in his living in a house so full of troubles. So I will use this body, which lives in sorrow, in such a way that I never again have to say ‘there is nothing’ when someone in need arrives.”

Sanskrit verses 3.165, 3.166

He blamed only himself and did not blame the fowler. “I will satisfy your hunger,” he said. “Wait a moment.” With that, the righteous bird, glad at heart, walked round the fire and entered it as though it were his own home.

Sanskrit verses 3.167, 3.168

The fowler saw it and was struck through with pity. As the dove lay in the fire he said, “A person who does evil surely has no love for himself, for the evil one does is suffered by oneself. My mind is evil and I have always taken pleasure in evil work. I shall fall into a dreadful hell, there is no doubt of it.”

Sanskrit verses 3.169, 3.170, 3.171

“This noble dove, giving his own flesh, has held up a mirror to my cruelty. From today I will give up every pleasure and let this body dry away, as summer dries a little water. I will bear cold, wind and heat, go thin and unwashed, keep many kinds of fasts and live by the highest law.”

Sanskrit verses 3.172, 3.173, 3.174

Then the fowler broke his stick, his rod, his net and his cage, and set the poor dove free.

Sanskrit verse 3.175

Freed by the fowler, she saw her husband fallen in the fire. She cried out in pain, her heart burning with grief. She wept long and bitterly, and then she, who had been true to him, entered that same blazing fire.

Sanskrit verses 3.176, 3.179

The story says that she then saw her husband seated in a heavenly chariot. She herself was dressed in heavenly clothes and adorned with heavenly ornaments. He too had a divine body, and he told her she had done well to follow him. From then on the two lived in happiness day after day, and this was the fruit of the good they had done before.

Sanskrit verses 3.180, 3.181, 3.183

The hunter, glad at heart, went into a deep forest. He had given up harming living things and was weary of the world. There he saw a forest fire and, wanting nothing more, entered it. The story says his wrongs were burnt away and he reached the happiness of heaven.

Sanskrit verses 3.184, 3.185

“That is why I say,” Kruraksha finished, “that a dove, it is told, honoured an enemy who came to him for shelter as a guest should be honoured, and offered him his own flesh.”

Sanskrit verse 3.134

What this story shows

The dove’s mate was in the hunter’s cage, and she was the one who asked her husband to treat him as a guest. What the dove did next changed the hunter’s whole life.

This is an old story of hospitality, and the source tells it almost wholly in verse. At its centre is the rule that someone who asks for shelter is to be protected, even an enemy. The caged wife is the one who states the rule, and she asks her husband to put away his anger at her capture.

The ending is harsh, and the text tells it as an act of the dove’s own world and time. Three lives end in fire. The story does not hold that out as a path for anyone. What stays with a reader is the hunter: a man everyone had given up on, who is changed by being treated as a guest.

  • The wife in the cage asked for kindness toward the man who had caught her.
  • The dove blamed himself for having nothing to give, and never blamed his guest.
  • The hunter broke his cage and snare after seeing how he had been received.

A fowler, abandoned by everyone for his cruelty, went into the forest every day with cage, snare and club. One day he caught a female dove and shut her in his cage.

A great storm broke over the forest. Cold, hungry and afraid, the fowler reached a tree and asked whoever lived there for shelter.

A dove lived on that tree and was grieving that his mate had not come home. She heard him from the cage. She told him to put his anger aside and honour the fowler as a guest who had asked for shelter.

The dove went to the fowler, welcomed him and asked what he could do for him. The fowler said he was cold.

The dove fetched a live coal and lit a small fire in dry leaves for his guest. He said he had no food to offer, and he blamed himself for it.

The story says the dove then gave his own body so that his guest could eat. The fowler saw it and was filled with pity, and with horror at his own life.

He broke his stick, his net and his cage and set the female dove free. He vowed to give up his cruel work.

The freed dove grieved for her mate and followed him into death. The story says the two were together afterwards in heaven.

The fowler gave up hunting and went deep into the forest, where he too met his end. The story says he reached heaven. The owl minister tells this to say that even an enemy who asks for shelter is to be protected.

What this story shows

The dove’s mate was in the hunter’s cage, and she was the one who asked her husband to treat him as a guest. What the dove did next changed the hunter’s whole life.

  • The dove blamed himself for having nothing to give, and never blamed his guest.
  • The hunter broke his cage and snare after seeing how he had been received.
