Ekata, Dvita and Trita

The hawk tells this to the sage Shalankayana, who has just knocked a mouse out of his claws. He is saying that a sage should have kept silent.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

On the bank of a certain river lived three brothers named Ekata, Dvita and Trita. All three were sages and practised austerities.

By the power of their austerities, their washed cloths, still wet, hung in the air with nothing to hold them while they bathed, so that they would not touch the ground.

One day a vulture carried off a small frog by force, as I carried off the mouse. The eldest brother saw her caught. His heart melted with pity and he cried out, as you did, “Let go, let go.” At that moment his washed cloth fell from the air to the ground.

The second brother saw it fall and was afraid of the same thing. He said, “Do not let go.” No sooner had he spoken than his cloth fell too.

The third brother saw both cloths lying on the ground and kept silent.

“That is why I say,” the hawk finished, “that one called ‘Let go, let go’ and fell, and a second called ‘Do not let go’ and fell. The sight of both falling shows that silence serves every purpose.”

Sanskrit verse 3.205

What this story shows

One spoke for the frog and one spoke against the first. Only the brother who said nothing kept what he had.

The story is a few lines long and works like a small puzzle. The first brother speaks from pity and loses his power. The second sees this, says the opposite and loses his as well. Speaking at all was the fault, whichever way the words went.

The hawk uses it for his own case, and he has an interest in the sage’s silence. The sage laughs in reply and says that rule belonged to another age. So the story is offered and then answered inside the larger one. It is a reminder that a saying in praise of silence can be used by someone who gains from it.

  • The brothers’ power showed in a small thing, their cloths staying off the ground.
  • Pity and its opposite cost the two speakers the same.
  • The hawk who praises silence is the one who wants the mouse.

Three brothers, all sages, lived by a river. Such was the power of their austerities that when they bathed, their wet cloths hung in the air and did not touch the ground.

One day a vulture carried off a small frog. The eldest brother cried out to it to let go, and at once his cloth fell to the ground.

The second brother, afraid of the same thing, called out not to let go. His cloth fell too.

The third saw both cloths lying on the ground and said nothing. The hawk tells this to the sage to say that silence serves best.

What this story shows

One spoke for the frog and one spoke against the first. Only the brother who said nothing kept what he had.
