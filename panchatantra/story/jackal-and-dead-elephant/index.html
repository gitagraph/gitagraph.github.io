The jackal who kept an elephant

The crocodile asks how to win back his home, and the monkey answers with this.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain country lived a jackal named Mahachaturaka. One day in the forest he found an elephant that had died of itself. He walked round and round it, but he could not get through the tough hide.

Just then a lion came wandering that way. The jackal put his head to the ground, folded his paws and said humbly, “Master, I am your guard, and I have been keeping this elephant for you. Please eat.” The lion said, “I never eat what another has killed. I make you a present of this elephant.”

The jackal was delighted. “That is how a master should treat his own servants,” he said. “The great keep the ways of a lord even at the last, as a conch stays white even after it has been through the fire.”

Sanskrit verse 4.79

When the lion had gone, a tiger arrived. The jackal thought, “I got rid of one dangerous beast by bowing. This one is bold, and nothing will move him but setting him against another. Where soft words and gifts will not serve, division is the means to use, for even one with every quality can be caught by it. A pearl is flawless, and it is strung only because it has been pierced.”

Sanskrit verses 4.80, 4.81

He went to meet the tiger with his neck held high and said in alarm, “Uncle, you have walked into the jaws of death. A lion killed this elephant. He set me to guard it and went to the river to bathe.

As he left he told me to let him know quietly if a tiger came, because he means to clear this forest of tigers. A tiger once ate from an elephant he had killed while he was away, and he has been furious with tigers ever since.” The tiger was terrified. “Nephew, grant me my life,” he said. “However long it is before he comes, tell him nothing about me.” And he ran.

After the tiger a leopard came. The jackal thought, “This one has strong teeth. I will get him to cut the hide for me.” He said, “Nephew, it is long since I saw you, and you look hungry. Be my guest. A lion killed this elephant and set me to guard it. Still, eat your fill of the meat before he comes back, and then be off quickly.”

The leopard said, “Uncle, in that case I want none of it. One who stays alive sees a hundred good things. I will go.” The jackal said, “Do not be so timid. Eat in peace. I will warn you while he is still far off.”

The leopard began. As soon as the jackal saw that the hide was cut through, he said, “Go, nephew. The lion is coming.” The leopard fled and was soon far away.

The jackal had just begun to eat through the opening when another jackal came up, very angry. He saw that this one was his equal in strength. He remembered the saying that the great are won by bowing, the brave by division, the low by a small gift and an equal by force.

Sanskrit verse 4.78

He went straight at the other jackal, tore him with his teeth and drove him off, and then he ate elephant meat in comfort for a long time.

The monkey said to the crocodile, “You must do the same. The enemy in your house is one of your own kind. Beat him in a fight and drive him out. If you let him take root, he will be the end of you.”

What this story shows

He treats each visitor according to what that visitor is, and saves force for his equal.

The jackal has one prize and four rivals, each of a different strength. He bows to the lion, frightens the tiger with a story, feeds the leopard to get the hide cut, and fights only the one he can beat.

The lies in the tale are the jackal’s, and the text reports them as they are told. The monkey takes one thing from it for the crocodile, whose rival is another crocodile: against an equal, there is nothing left but to fight.

  • The jackal reads each rival before he decides how to meet him.
  • He uses the leopard’s teeth for the one thing he cannot do himself.
  • The monkey’s advice to the crocodile is the last part of the saying: fight your equal.

A jackal found an elephant that had died of itself, and he could not get through the hide. A lion came by, and the jackal bowed and offered it to him. The lion ate only his own kills, and made him a present of it.

Next came a tiger. The jackal said a lion had killed the elephant and had sworn to rid the forest of tigers. The tiger begged him to say nothing and ran.

Then came a leopard with strong teeth. The jackal invited him to eat before the lion returned and promised to keep watch. As soon as the hide was cut through, he cried that the lion was coming, and the leopard fled.

At last another jackal came, his equal in strength. This one he fought and drove off, and then he ate in peace for a long time.

The monkey tells the crocodile to do the same. The rival in his house is one of his own kind, and an equal has to be fought.
