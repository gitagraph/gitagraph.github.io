The jackal who followed the bull

Somilaka the weaver says this to the figure in the sky who takes his wealth. He has been asked why he wants riches he will never enjoy.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

A great bull named Tikshnavishana, Sharp Horns, grew so proud of his strength that he left his herd. He lived in the forest as he pleased, tearing at the riverbanks with his horns and grazing on grass as green as emerald.

A jackal named Pralobhaka lived in the same forest. One day he was sitting at ease on the riverbank with his wife when the bull came down to the sand to drink. The jackal’s wife looked at the bull. “Husband, see those two lumps of flesh hanging from him,” she said. “They will drop in a moment, or in a few hours at most. Follow him.”

“My dear, no one knows whether they will ever fall,” said the jackal. “Why send me on a useless errand? Here I can stay with you and eat the mice that come to drink. This is their path. If I go off behind that bull, someone else will take this spot. Whoever leaves the sure thing to chase the unsure one loses the sure thing, and the unsure one was lost already.”

Sanskrit verse 2.142

“You have a small spirit,” said his wife. “You are happy with whatever scrap comes to you. A little stream is quickly full, and so are a mouse’s cupped paws. Luck stays where there is drive and no laziness. No oil comes from sesame without pressing. Besides, I am sick of mouse meat. Those two lumps look ready to fall. Do as I say.”

Sanskrit verses 2.143, 2.144, 2.145

The jackal heard her out. He left the place where the mice came and followed the bull.

He and his wife wandered behind the bull for a long time, and nothing fell. In the fifteenth year the jackal gave up. “Loose, yet firmly fixed. Will they fall or will they not? I have watched them, my dear, for fifteen years,” he said. “They will not fall after this either. Let us go back to our own place.”

Sanskrit verse 2.141

“That is why I said it,” Somilaka told the figure in the sky. “Loose, yet firmly fixed.”

What this story shows

The jackal gave up a sure meal for a prize that always looked one step away.

The story shows how easily people leave something sure for something that only looks close. The jackal had a place by the water and a meal every day. The prize looked ready to drop at any moment, and it looked that way for fifteen years.

It also shows how hard it is to stop. Each day spent following becomes a reason to follow for one more. The jackal himself had named the risk before he set out.

  • What looks about to fall can stay where it is for years.
  • Leaving the sure thing for the unsure one can lose both.
  • The jackal knew the risk, said it aloud, and went anyway.

A great bull named Sharp Horns grew proud of his strength and left his herd. He lived alone in the forest, tearing at the riverbanks with his horns and grazing as he pleased.

A jackal and his wife sat by the river when the bull came down to drink. The wife saw two lumps of flesh hanging from the bull. She was sure they would drop within hours and told her husband to follow him.

The jackal said no one knew whether they would ever fall, and that he would be giving up a sure meal of mice by the water. His wife called him small in spirit and said she was sick of mouse meat. He gave in.

They followed the bull for fifteen years, and nothing fell. Then the jackal gave up and they went back to their own place. Somilaka told this to the figure in the sky when he was asked why he wanted wealth he could not enjoy.

What this story shows
