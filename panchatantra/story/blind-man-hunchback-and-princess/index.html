The blind man, the hunchback and the princess

The wheel-bearer tells this to say that fate decides how things turn out.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In the north country there is a city named Madhupura, and a king named Madhusena once ruled there. A daughter was born to him with three breasts. When the king heard of it he told his chamberlains, “Take this child far away into a forest and leave her, so that no one knows of it.”

The chamberlains said, “Great king, such a birth is held to bring misfortune. Even so, you should send for the brahmins and ask them, so that nothing is done against this world or the next.

One who keeps asking, listening and remembering finds his understanding opening like a lotus in the sun. A wise person should always be one who asks. Long ago a brahmin who had been seized by a demon was set free by a question.” The king asked how that had been, and they told him.

When he had heard it, the king sent for the brahmins and asked whether anything could be done for his daughter. They quoted their books to say that the sight of such a daughter was held to harm her father. They advised him not to look on her, and to give her to any man who would marry her, on condition that he left the country.

The king had it proclaimed everywhere by beat of drum that whoever married the princess would receive a hundred thousand gold pieces and must go abroad. A long time passed and no one took her. She grew up, kept out of sight in a guarded place.

In that same city lived a blind man, and a hunchback named Mantharaka who walked in front of him and held his stick. The two heard the drum and talked it over. “Let us touch the drum. If by some chance we win the girl, we win the gold too, and with gold life is easy. And if her ill luck brings death, that will at least be the end of this misery of being poor.

Modesty, affection, a sweet voice, good sense, the bloom of youth, love, the regard of one’s kin, freedom from sorrow, pleasure, duty and learning all come to a person once the belly is full.” So the blind man went and touched the drum and said, “I will marry the girl, if the king will give her to me.”

Sanskrit verse 5.91

The king’s men went and reported that a blind man had touched the drum, and asked the king’s pleasure. The king said that whoever the man was, he could take the girl and the gold and go abroad.

So they took him to the river bank, married the princess to him with a hundred thousand gold pieces, put them in a boat and told the boatmen to set the blind man down in some town in another country, with his wife and the hunchback. It was done. The three bought a house in a foreign town and lived in comfort. The blind man lay on his couch, and Mantharaka ran the house.

As time went on, the princess and the hunchback became lovers. One day she said to him, “If this blind man could be got rid of somehow, you and I could live happily. Look for poison somewhere, and I will give it to him.”

Some time later the hunchback found a dead black snake. He brought it home in delight and said, “Here is a black snake. Cut it up, cook it with plenty of ginger and spices and give it to him as fish. He is always asking for fish, and it will kill him quickly.” Then he went out. She cut the snake in pieces, put it in a pot of buttermilk and set it on the fire.

She was busy about the house, so she said politely to her husband, “My lord, I have got you the fish you are always asking for, and it is on the fire. Take the ladle and stir it for a moment while I see to the housework.”

He was delighted. He got up at once, licking his lips, took the ladle and began to stir. As he stirred, the steam, heavy with poison, reached his eyes, and the dark film over them began to melt away. He found that it did him good and held his eyes in the steam on purpose. His sight came back.

He looked into the pot and saw nothing but pieces of black snake. He thought, “She told me this was fish, and it is a black snake. I must find out exactly what she is about, and whether this plan to kill me is hers, or the hunchback’s, or someone else’s.” He hid what had happened and went on behaving as a blind man.

Presently the hunchback came in and, thinking himself unobserved, began to embrace and kiss the princess. The husband saw it. He looked for a weapon and found none, and he was beside himself with anger.

He went across to them as he used to, seized the hunchback by the feet, swung him round above his head with all his strength and struck the princess on the chest with him. The blow drove her third breast into her chest, and the hunchback’s back was straightened by the swinging.

The wheel-bearer said to the gold-finder, “That is why I say that the blind man, the hunchback and the three-breasted princess all came right by a wrong road, because fate was on their side.”

Sanskrit verse 5.86

What this story shows

Nothing that happens in this house is meant well, and all of it turns out to someone’s good.

The wheel-bearer chooses this tale because it defeats every rule. A wife plots murder, a servant helps, a husband strikes in rage, and each of the three comes out of it changed in the way the tale counts as good fortune.

The telling here keeps to what the source says and does not share its view of the three bodies. The king sends his daughter away on the word of his books, and the tale treats a blind man and a hunchback as people to whom anything may be done. What it offers as argument is narrow: the wheel-bearer says results do not always follow from conduct, and the gold-finder will answer him.

  • The wheel-bearer’s case is that outcomes can come by the wrong road.
  • The princess is sent abroad because of a belief about her body, and the tale reports that belief.
  • No one in the house acts well, which is the teller’s whole point about fate.

The brahmins said it was held that the sight of such a daughter harmed her father. The king had it proclaimed by drum that whoever married her would get a great sum in gold and must leave the country. No one came, and she grew up out of sight.

A blind man and the hunchback who led him decided to take the offer, since gold would end their poverty. The blind man touched the drum and married the princess. The three were sent abroad by boat, bought a house and lived in comfort.

In time the princess and the hunchback became lovers and planned to be rid of her husband. The hunchback found a dead snake, and she cooked it as fish.

She asked her husband to stir the pot. The poisoned steam reached his eyes and cleared them, and he saw what was cooking. He said nothing and went on as before.

When he saw the two together he struck out in fury, swinging the hunchback against the princess. The blow left the hunchback’s back straight and the princess with two breasts. The wheel-bearer says all three came right by a wrong road, because fate was with them.
