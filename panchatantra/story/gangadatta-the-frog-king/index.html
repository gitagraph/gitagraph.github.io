Gangadatta the frog king

The monkey tells this to the crocodile, who is coaxing him to come back.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

A frog king named Gangadatta lived in a well. His own kinsmen gave him no peace, and one day he climbed up the buckets of the water wheel and got out. He thought about how to pay them back. A man is born again, he told himself, once he has paid back the one who harmed him in trouble and the one who laughed at him in hard times.

Sanskrit verse 4.17

As he was thinking, he saw a black snake going into its hole. He decided to take the snake to the well and make an end of all his kinsmen. An enemy is to be set against an enemy, he thought, and a stronger one against a strong one, so that their fall costs oneself nothing. A sharp enemy is pulled out by a sharp enemy, as a thorn is drawn with a thorn.

Sanskrit verses 4.18, 4.19

He went to the mouth of the hole and called, “Come out, Priyadarshana, come out.” The snake heard him and thought, “Whoever is calling me is not one of my kind, for that is no snake’s voice, and I have no dealings with anyone else in this world. I will stay inside my fort until I know who it is.

Brihaspati said not to keep company with one whose character, family and home are unknown. It may be a charmer or a herb doctor who means to bind me, or someone with a grudge who wants me to bite his enemy.” So he asked who was there.

Sanskrit verse 4.20

“I am Gangadatta, lord of the frogs,” came the answer. “I have come to make friends with you.” The snake said, “That is not to be believed. Grass does not make friends with fire. A creature does not go near the one born to kill it, even in a dream.”

Sanskrit verse 4.21

Gangadatta said, “That is true. You are our enemy by nature. But others have humiliated me, and so I have come to you. When everything is being lost and life itself is in danger, one saves life and goods even by bowing to an enemy.”

Sanskrit verse 4.22

The snake asked who had humiliated him. “My kinsmen,” said Gangadatta. The snake asked where he lived, and he said it was a well lined with stone.

“We snakes have no feet,” said the snake. “I cannot get into such a place, and once in, I would have nowhere to lie while I killed your kinsmen. Go away. One who wants to prosper eats only what can be swallowed, what can be digested and what is good for him in the end.”

Sanskrit verse 4.23

Gangadatta said, “Come with me. I will get you in by an easy way. Inside, near the water, there is a pleasant hollow. You can lie there and kill my kinsmen at your ease.”

The snake thought, “I am old. Some days I catch one mouse, with luck. This disgrace to his family has shown me an easy living, and I will go and eat those frogs. One whose strength is failing and who has no helpers does well to find the easiest way to live.”

Sanskrit verse 4.24

He told Gangadatta to lead the way. Gangadatta said, “I will take you there and show you the place. You are to eat only the ones I point out.” The snake said, “You are my friend now. Have no fear. I will eat your kinsmen at your word.”

He came out of his hole and embraced him, and the two set off together. At the well, Gangadatta himself brought the snake down by the buckets of the water wheel to his own home.

When the snake had done what he was brought for, Gangadatta said, “Good sir, you have done what a friend should. Now go back the way you came, by the water wheel.” The snake said, “That is not well said, Gangadatta. I cannot go back. Someone else will have taken my hole by now. I am staying here, and you will give me one frog of your own people each day. If you do not, I will eat them all.”

Gangadatta was sick at heart. “What have I done, bringing this snake here,” he thought. “If I refuse, he will eat every one of us. It is rightly said that one who makes a friend of an enemy stronger than himself is eating poison of his own accord. I will give him one of my friends each day.

Sanskrit verse 4.25

The wise quiet an enemy who is set on taking everything by giving him a little, as the ocean quiets the fire beneath it. A weak one who will not give a small thing gently when the stronger asks ends by giving a great measure. When everything is about to be lost, the wise give up half and work with the other half, and they do not lose much for the sake of a little.”

Sanskrit verses 4.26, 4.27, 4.28, 4.29

So he named one frog each day. The snake ate that one, and behind Gangadatta’s back he ate others too. One who has begun to lose what he has does not guard what is left, the text says, as a person in soiled clothes sits down anywhere.

Sanskrit verse 4.30

One day, after eating other frogs, the snake ate Gangadatta’s own son Yamunadatta. Gangadatta cried aloud and would not stop. His wife said, “There is no use in crying now. You are the one who destroyed your own people, and with our people gone we have no one to protect us. Think even now of a way out for yourself, and of a way to kill him.”

Sanskrit verse 4.31

In time the whole clan of frogs was eaten, and only Gangadatta was left. Priyadarshana said, “Gangadatta, I am hungry. The frogs are all finished. You brought me here, so give me something to eat.” Gangadatta said, “Friend, do not worry about that while I am here. If you let me go, I will win the trust of the frogs in other wells and bring them to you.”

The snake said, “I would not eat you in any case, for you are like a brother to me. Do this and you will be like a father.” Gangadatta climbed onto a bucket of the water wheel, with vows to many gods, and got out of the well.

Priyadarshana waited and waited. When Gangadatta did not come back, he spoke to a lizard who lived in another hollow of the well. “Good lady, do me a small favour. You have known Gangadatta a long time. Find him in whatever pond he is in and give him my message.

Tell him to come quickly, alone if the other frogs will not come, for I cannot live here without him. Tell him I stake all my merit that I will do him no harm.” The lizard soon found Gangadatta and gave him the message.

Gangadatta said, “There is no sin a hungry creature will not do, and those who are wasting away have no pity. Good lady, tell Priyadarshana that Gangadatta does not come back to the well.” With that he sent her away. “And I, like Gangadatta, will never go to your house,” the monkey told the crocodile.

Sanskrit verse 4.16

What this story shows

A helper stronger than oneself does not leave when the job is done.

Gangadatta’s plan works exactly as he meant it to, and that is the trouble. The snake he brings in is stronger than his rivals and stronger than he is, and once the rivals are gone it has no reason to go.

The story is also about knowing when to stop trusting. Gangadatta escapes only because he leaves and stays away, whatever promises follow him. The monkey makes the same choice with the crocodile.

  • An enemy brought in against other enemies stays to be fed.
  • Paying a little each day to a stronger party ends with paying everything.
  • After one betrayal, a promise from the same mouth is no reason to return.

A frog king lived in a well, and his own kinsmen gave him no peace. He climbed out by the buckets of the water wheel, saw a black snake going into its hole, and thought of a way to be rid of them.

He called the snake out and asked for his friendship. The snake did not believe that prey would come to him of its own will. The frog king said his kinsmen had driven him to it, and promised an easy way into the well and a hollow to lie in.

The snake was old and saw an easy living in this. The frog king led him to the well and took him down by the buckets himself, to eat only the frogs he pointed out.

When the work was done, the frog king asked the snake to go. The snake said his own hole would be taken by now, so he would stay and be given one frog each day. The frog king saw he had brought in an enemy stronger than himself.

The snake ate the frog he was given each day and others besides, and one day the king’s own son. In time the well was empty and only the frog king was left. He promised to bring frogs from other wells, and got out by the buckets.

The snake sent a lizard after him with a promise of safety. The frog king answered that the hungry stop at nothing, and that he would not come back to the well. The monkey says he will not go back to the crocodile either.

What this story shows

A helper stronger than oneself does not leave when the job is done.

  • An enemy brought in against other enemies stays to be fed.
  • Paying a little each day to a stronger party ends with paying everything.
  • After one betrayal, a promise from the same mouth is no reason to return.
