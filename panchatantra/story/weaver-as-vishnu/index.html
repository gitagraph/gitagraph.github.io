The weaver who came as Vishnu

Damanaka tells this to Karataka

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town there lived a weaver and a carpenter who built chariots. They had been friends since childhood and went everywhere together.

One day there was a great festival at a temple in the town, crowded with players, dancers and singers and with people from many lands. The two friends were walking through it when they saw the king's daughter. She was a grown young woman, not yet married, and she had come on an elephant to see the god, with the servants of the palace around her.

The weaver took one look at her and fell to the ground as if he had been poisoned. The carpenter had him carried home and brought doctors to him. It was a long time before he opened his eyes.

“Friend, what happened to you?” asked the carpenter. “If you love me,” said the weaver, “build my funeral pyre, and forgive me any wrong I have done you.” The carpenter wept. “Tell me what is hurting you. If it can be mended, I will mend it, and if it cannot, I will walk into the fire with you.” “It is the king's daughter,” said the weaver. “Since I saw her I cannot bear to live.”

The carpenter smiled. “Then our trouble is over. You will be with her this very night.” “Nothing enters her rooms except the wind,” said the weaver. “They are guarded day and night. Do not mock me.” “Watch what my wits can do,” said the carpenter.

He set to work and built a wooden Garuda, the great bird that carries the god Vishnu, and he built it so that it flew when its pegs were turned. He made a second pair of arms, and the conch, the discus, the mace and the lotus that Vishnu holds, and the god's crown and jewel. He sat the weaver on the bird, dressed him as Vishnu, and showed him how to work the pegs. “Go to her at midnight in this form,” he said. “She sleeps alone at the top of the palace of seven floors. She will take you for the god.”

The weaver flew to her window that night. “King's daughter, are you awake? For your sake I have left the goddess Lakshmi and come from the ocean. Be my wife.” She saw him on the great bird, with four arms and the god's weapons, and stood up in wonder. “Lord, I am only a mortal woman. You are the one who makes the three worlds pure. How can this be right?”

“You were my wife Radha in an earlier life,” said the weaver. “That is why I have come.” “Then ask my father,” she said. “He will give me to you gladly.” “I do not show myself to mortals, and I do not speak with them,” said the weaver. “Give yourself to me by the rite that needs no witness. If you refuse, I will curse your father and burn him and his whole house to ash.”

She was frightened, and she agreed. He stayed until just before dawn and flew home unseen. After that he came to her every night.

In time the servants who guarded her rooms saw signs that a man had been visiting her. They went to the king. “We do not know how, lord, but someone is getting into your daughter's rooms.” The king was sick with worry. A daughter, he thought, is a care from the day she is born: whom to give her to, and whether she will be happy. He sent the queen to find out the truth.

The queen went to her daughter in a rage. “You have shamed this house. Who is the man who comes to you?” The king's daughter bowed her head. “Mother, it is the god Narayana himself. He comes every night on Garuda. If you do not believe me, hide and see him with your own eyes at midnight.”

The queen's anger turned to joy. She hurried to the king. “My lord, you are blessed. The god Narayana comes to our daughter every night and has made her his wife. Tonight you and I must watch from the window. He does not speak with mortals.”

That day seemed to the king as long as a hundred years. At midnight he and the queen hid at the window, and saw Narayana come down out of the sky on Garuda, with the conch, the discus, the mace and the lotus in his hands. The king felt as if he had been bathed in nectar. “Nobody in the world is as fortunate as you and I,” he said. “The god loves our child. With such a son-in-law I will bring the whole earth under my rule.”

So he broke his agreements with every king on his borders. They saw what he was doing, joined forces, and made war on him.

The king sent word to his daughter through the queen. “With you for a daughter and the god Narayana for a son-in-law, it is not right that every king should be at war with me. Speak to your husband tonight, and have him destroy my enemies.” She asked the weaver humbly that night. “Your father's enemies are a small matter,” he said. “Do not worry. I will cut them to pieces with my discus in a moment.”

Time passed, and the enemy took the whole country. The king had nothing left except the walls of his city. He still did not know that his son-in-law was a weaver. Every day he sent him camphor, musk, fine clothes, flowers, food and drink. At last he sent this message through his daughter: “Lord, the city will fall in the morning. The fodder and the firewood are gone. Every man I have is wounded or dead. Do whatever is fitting.”

The weaver thought it over. “If the city falls I will lose her. I will mount my bird and show myself in the sky with my weapons. They may take me for Vishnu and lose heart, and the king's men may kill them. Even a snake without venom should spread its hood wide. Venom or none, the hood is frightening. And if I die fighting for this place, that is better still.”

At dawn he said to her, “I will not eat or drink until every enemy is dead. Tell your father to come out of the city this morning with all his army and fight. I will be in the sky above them and take their strength away, and he can kill them easily.” She told her father, and he believed it. He marched out at sunrise. The weaver took up a bow, mounted the wooden bird, and flew out to the battle, ready to die.

Now the god Narayana knows all that has been, is, and will be. He thought of the real Garuda, who came at once, and he laughed. “Garuda, do you know that a weaver is riding a wooden copy of you, in my form, and has made a king's daughter his wife?” “Lord, I know everything he has done. What shall we do?”

“Today that weaver has gone out to battle, ready to die,” said the god. “The warriors will certainly strike him down. Then people will say that Vishnu and Garuda were beaten, and nobody will worship you or me again. So go quickly and enter the wooden bird. I will enter the weaver's body, and he will destroy the enemy. Our glory will grow by it.”

Garuda agreed, and it was done. By the god's power the weaver in the sky, with conch, discus, mace and bow, took the strength from every enemy warrior in a moment, as if in play. The king and his army fell on them and killed them all. And people everywhere said that the king had destroyed his enemies through the power of his son-in-law Vishnu.

The weaver saw them dead and came down from the sky, well pleased. The king, his ministers and the townspeople looked at him and saw a weaver from their own town. “What is this?” they asked. He told them everything from the beginning.

The king had his victory, and the weaver's daring pleased him. He gave him his daughter in marriage before all the people, and land as well. The weaver lived with her in comfort for the rest of his days. “That is why it is said,” Damanaka finished, “that not even the creator sees to the bottom of a deception that is well made.”

Sanskrit verse 1.218

What this story shows

The lie held because everyone who could have tested it wanted it to be true.

The carpenter's bird is a fine piece of work, and the story is clear that craft alone did not carry the trick. The king's daughter is threatened into silence. Her mother stops being angry the moment the visitor is said to be a god. Her father sees a figure in the night sky and at once plans to conquer the earth. Each of them could have asked one more question, and each had a reason to leave it unasked.

The cost falls on other people. A kingdom is overrun and its soldiers die for a king's belief about his son-in-law. In the end the god makes the lie come true, and the text says why: his own name would suffer if the false Vishnu fell. Damanaka tells this to show Karataka that a deception can succeed. What the story shows as well is how much has to go wrong around a lie before someone powerful finds it easier to rescue it than to expose it.

  • A claim is believed most easily by those who gain from it.
  • A threat can pass for consent when nobody asks how the consent was given.
  • A lie that grows large enough draws in people who then need it to hold.

Her servants told the king that someone was visiting her. She told her mother it was the god himself. The king and queen watched from a window, saw the figure come down from the sky, and believed it.

With a god for a son-in-law, the king broke faith with every neighbour, and they made war on him together. He lost the whole country except his city walls, and begged his son-in-law to act.

The weaver flew out to the battle ready to die, hoping the enemy would take him for Vishnu. The real Vishnu saw that people would stop worshipping him if the false one fell. So he entered the weaver's body, and Garuda entered the wooden bird.

The enemy lost all strength in a moment and the king's army destroyed them. The weaver came down and was seen for what he was, and he told the whole story. The king, pleased with his daring and his victory, gave him his daughter and land.
