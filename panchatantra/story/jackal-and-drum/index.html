The jackal and the drum

Damanaka tells this to the lion Pingalaka, who is ready to leave his forest because of a sound he cannot explain.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

“My lord, a sound alone is nothing to fear,” Damanaka said to the lion. “There is a verse: At first I thought it was full of fat. Then I went inside, and found only hide and wood.”

“How was that?” asked Pingalaka.

A jackal named Gomayu was wandering in the forest, his throat thin with hunger. He came to a field where two armies had fought.

A drum lay fallen there. Whenever the wind blew, the tips of a creeper's branches struck it, and it boomed.

The jackal's heart shook. “I am finished,” he thought. “Before the one who makes this sound sets eyes on me, I should be somewhere else.”

Then he checked himself. “That is too hasty. Whoever stops to think when fear or joy arrives, and does not act in a rush, has nothing to regret. Let me first find out whose sound this is.”

He gathered his courage and crept closer, step by step, until he saw the drum. He knew it for what it was. He went up to it and, out of curiosity, struck it himself.

Now he was glad. “At last a great meal has come my way. It must be packed with meat, fat and blood.”

The drum was covered in tough hide. He tore at it until he had made a hole in one place, and went happily inside. He broke his teeth on the hide in doing it.

Inside there was only wood. His hope gone, he looked at it and said the verse: “At first I thought it was full of fat. Then I went inside, and found only hide and wood.”

“So,” said Damanaka, “a sound alone is nothing to fear.”

“Wait here, my lord,” he went on. “I will go and find out what this sound really is, and come back.”

What this story shows

A loud sound tells you very little about what is making it.

The jackal was wrong twice. First he took the drum for a monster, then he took it for a feast. Both guesses came from the sound, and both fell apart when he reached the thing itself. His one good move was the pause between them, when he decided to look before running.

Damanaka tells this to calm a frightened king, and it is good advice. It also serves Damanaka. A king who stays, and who owes his calm to one jackal, is a king that jackal can use. The story shows how fear and hope both fill an empty thing with whatever we expect to find.

  • Find out what is making the noise before you run from it.
  • The same pause that checks fear should check hope too.
  • A big sound can come from a hollow thing.
