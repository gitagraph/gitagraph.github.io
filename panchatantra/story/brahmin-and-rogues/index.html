The brahmin, the goat and the three rogues

The old minister Sthirajivi tells this to Meghavarna, king of the crows. He is about to try a ruse against the owls, and this is his example of how many voices can mislead one person.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived a brahmin named Mitrasharma, who kept the sacred fire. One day in the month of Magha, when a soft wind was blowing, clouds covered the sky and a light rain was falling, he went to another village to ask for an animal for sacrifice.

He said to a patron there, “On the coming new-moon day I am to hold a sacrifice. Please give me one animal.” The man gave him a fat goat, of the kind the scriptures call for. It was strong and kept running this way and that, so the brahmin lifted it onto his shoulder and set off quickly for his own town.

On the road three rogues came toward him, thin with hunger. They saw the fat animal riding on his shoulder and said to one another, “If we could eat that animal, today’s bitter cold would be nothing to us. Let us trick him out of it and keep ourselves warm.”

One of them changed his dress, came round by a side path and met the brahmin face to face. “Fire-keeper, you simple man,” he said, “you are doing something ridiculous that goes against all custom. You are carrying an unclean dog on your shoulder. The rule says that a dog, a cock and an outcaste are equally unclean to touch, and a donkey and a camel even more, so one must not touch them.”

Sanskrit verse 3.117

The brahmin was furious. He asked whether the man was blind, to call a goat a dog. “Do not be angry, brahmin,” said the rogue. “Go on as you please.”

He had gone a little farther when the second rogue came up to him. “Brahmin, what a pity, what a pity,” he said. “This dead calf may have been dear to you, and still it is wrong to put it on your shoulder. The rule says that a fool who touches a dead animal or a dead person is made clean only by the five products of the cow or by the fast that follows the moon.”

Sanskrit verse 3.118

The brahmin answered angrily and asked if he too was blind, to call a living animal a dead calf. “Sir, do not be angry,” said the rogue. “I spoke out of ignorance. Do as you think best.”

He had gone a short way into the forest when the third rogue, in yet another dress, came to meet him. “This is not right,” he said. “You are carrying a donkey on your shoulder. Put it down. The rule says that anyone who touches a donkey, knowingly or unknowingly, must bathe with his clothes on to wash away the fault. Drop it before anyone else sees you.”

Sanskrit verse 3.119

Now the brahmin believed the animal was a donkey. In fear he threw it to the ground and ran for home. The three rogues met, took the goat and ate it at their ease.

“That is why I say,” Sthirajivi finished, “that rogues with many wits and sound knowledge can cheat even the strong, as they cheated the brahmin of his goat.”

Sanskrit verse 3.116

What this story shows

The brahmin trusted his own eyes twice. The third time, he trusted three strangers who all said the same kind of thing.

The brahmin trusts his own eyes the first time and the second time. Each rogue comes alone, from a new direction, in new clothes, and each one touches the same fear, that he is carrying something unclean. By the third meeting the fear is stronger than what he can see.

Sthirajivi tells this as someone planning a deception of his own against the owls, and the frame will show what comes of that. Read from the brahmin’s side, the story is about how doubt is built. The three never agreed on what the animal was. They agreed only that he was wrong.

  • The brahmin was right twice and gave way the third time.
  • The rogues worked on his fear of impurity, which mattered more to him than the goat.
  • Their three accounts did not even match one another.

A brahmin who kept the sacred fire went to another village to ask for an animal for his sacrifice. A patron gave him a fat goat. He lifted it onto his shoulders and set off for home.

Three hungry rogues saw him coming along the road. They wanted the goat and agreed to trick him out of it.

The first rogue came up in disguise and said the brahmin was carrying a dog, an unclean thing. The brahmin was angry and walked on. A little farther the second rogue said it was a dead calf, and got the same answer.

In the forest the third rogue met him and said it was a donkey. He told him to drop it before anyone saw.

Now the brahmin believed it. He threw the goat down in fear and ran home, and the three rogues carried it off and ate it. The old crow minister tells this to show how clever cheats can fool even the strong.
