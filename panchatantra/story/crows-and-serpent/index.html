The crows and the black snake

Damanaka the jackal tells this to Karataka, who doubts that a jackal can part a lion and a bull.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

A pair of crows lived in a great banyan tree. Every time their eggs hatched, a black snake came up out of a hollow in the trunk and ate the young.

Worn down by grief, they went to a jackal who lived under a tree nearby. He was a dear friend. “What are we to do?” they asked. “That snake comes out of the hollow and eats our children. Tell us some way to protect them.”

“A man whose field lies on a river bank can never rest,” said the crow. “Nor can one who shares his house with a snake. We live in fear every day.”

“Do not lose heart,” said the jackal. “That greedy creature cannot be killed without a plan. A plan wins what weapons cannot, and someone small who knows the way is not beaten by the strong. Think of the heron who ate so many fish, and died of his greed in the grip of a crab.”

“How did that happen?” they asked. And the jackal told them.

When the story was done, the crow asked, “Then tell us how this snake will die.”

“Fly to a city where a king lives,” said the jackal. “Find someone rich and careless. Take a gold chain or a necklace and drop it into the snake's hollow. Whoever comes to fetch it will deal with the snake.”

The two crows flew off at once. The she-crow came to a lake where the women of a king's household were bathing. They had left their gold chains, pearl necklaces and clothes at the water's edge. She picked up a gold chain and flew for home.

The king's attendants saw it go. They snatched up their clubs and ran after her. She dropped the chain into the hollow and settled on a branch far off to watch.

The attendants climbed the tree and looked into the hollow. There sat the black snake with its hood spread. They killed it with their clubs, took the gold chain and went away.

After that the two crows lived in peace.

“That is why I say,” Damanaka finished, “what a plan can do, force cannot.”

What this story shows

Even the small can bring down the strong, with a plan and a friend.

The crows could not fight the snake, and they did not try. They found something a stronger party wanted and put it where the snake was. The king's men did the rest, and never knew they had been used.

It is worth remembering who tells this story. Damanaka is about to use the same thinking against a friendship. A plan is a tool. The story shows that it works. It does not say what it should be used for.

  • When force will not work, look for another way.
  • Ask someone who sees more clearly than you.
  • Let the stronger side's own power do the work.
