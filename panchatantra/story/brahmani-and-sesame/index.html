Hulled sesame for unhulled

The visiting monk tells this to his host Tamrachuda. He wants to show that the mouse’s high leap must have a reason behind it.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

One rainy season the visiting monk had asked a brahmin for a place to stay and keep his vow. The brahmin took him in and looked after him. Early one morning the monk lay awake and heard the brahmin talking with his wife.

“Tomorrow at dawn the sun turns south,” said the brahmin. “A gift given on that day bears fruit without end. I am going to the next village to receive gifts. You must feed one brahmin here, in the Sun’s name.”

His wife answered him harshly. “Where would a man as poor as you find food to give? You should be ashamed to talk like this. Since the day I took your hand I have had no comfort, no sweet thing to eat and no ornament to wear.”

The brahmin was afraid of her, but he spoke gently. “That is no way to talk. Even from one mouthful, half can go to someone who asks. No one ever has as much as they would like. A small giver is loved more than a rich miser. People go to the well for its sweet water and leave the sea alone. So even the poor should give a little, at the right time, to the right person.”

Sanskrit verses 2.72, 2.74, 2.79

“Do not give up wanting altogether, and do not want too much,” he said. “The one who wants too much ends with a crest on top of his head. A jackal found that out.”

“Life, deeds, wealth, learning and death,” he went on. “These five are fixed for a person while still in the womb.” His wife gave way. “Then there is a little sesame in the house,” she said. “I will hull it and feed a brahmin with ground sesame.” The brahmin set off for the village.

Sanskrit verse 2.84

She rubbed the sesame in hot water, pounded off the husks and spread it in the sun. While she was busy with the housework, a dog came and urinated on it. “See how clever fate is when it turns against you,” she thought. “This sesame cannot be eaten now. I will take it to some house and bring back unhulled sesame in exchange. Anyone will agree to that.”

The monk had gone to a house that day to beg for food. The brahmin’s wife came into the same house with her sesame. “Hulled sesame, in exchange for unhulled,” she called. “Let anyone who wants it take it.”

The woman of the house was about to make the exchange. Her son, who had studied Kamandaki’s book on statecraft, spoke up. “Mother, do not take this sesame. No one gives hulled for unhulled without a reason. There is something behind it.” She listened to him and left the sesame alone.

Sanskrit verse 2.71

“That is why I say nothing happens without a reason,” the monk told Tamrachuda. “The mouse’s leap has one too.”

What this story shows

When someone offers the better thing in exchange for the worse, there is a reason they are not saying.

The story shows that people rarely give up the better thing for the worse without a cause. The brahmin’s wife had spoiled sesame to get rid of, so she dressed it as a good bargain. The boy could not tell what was wrong with it. He could tell that such an offer does not come for nothing.

It also shows two people in one poor house looking at giving in different ways. The husband wants to give even from very little. The wife counts what she has gone without. Both are speaking from the same empty kitchen.

  • An offer that is better than it should be has a reason behind it.
  • The boy stopped the exchange by asking why, without knowing the answer.
  • The brahmin held that even a poor house can give a little.

The brahmin said even the poor should give a little, and that wanting too much brings harm. He told her of a jackal who learned this. His wife gave way and said she would hull their bit of sesame and feed a brahmin with it.
