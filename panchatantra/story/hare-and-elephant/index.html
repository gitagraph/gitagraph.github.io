The hare, the elephant and the Moon Lake

A crow tells this to the birds who are about to crown the owl as their king. He says the name of a great lord is protection enough, and they already have Garuda.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain forest lived a great elephant named Chaturdanta, Four-Tusks, the lord of a herd. Once a great drought came and lasted many years. The tanks, lakes, ponds and pools all dried up.

The elephants went to their king. “Lord,” they said, “the calves are tormented by thirst. Some are nearly dead and some have died. Find us some water where they can drink and be well again.” He thought for a long time. Then he said, “There is a great lake in a lonely place, set in the middle of dry land. It is always full, fed by the Ganga that flows under the earth. Let us go there.”

They walked for five nights and reached the lake. They plunged in and bathed as they liked, and came out at sunset. All around the lake, in the soft ground, were the burrows of hares beyond counting. The elephants, wandering here and there, broke them all. Many hares had their feet, heads and necks crushed. Some died, and some were left barely alive.

When the herd had gone, the hares came together. Their homes had been trampled. Some had broken legs, some were bruised and covered in blood, and some, whose young had been killed, could hardly see for tears.

“We are ruined,” they said. “This herd will come every day, because there is no water anywhere else. It will be the end of us all. They say an elephant kills with a mere touch, a snake with a mere sniff, a king with a smile and a wicked person with a show of respect. Let us think of some way out.”

One of them said, “Let us leave this country. There is nothing else to do. Manu and Vyasa have said that one person may be given up for the family, the family for the village, the village for the country, and the whole earth for one’s own life.

Sanskrit verse 3.83

A king should give up even safe and fertile land, rich in grain and cattle, to save himself, and not think twice. Wealth is kept for hard times, a wife is protected even at the cost of wealth, and one’s own self is to be protected always, even at the cost of wife and wealth.”

Sanskrit verses 3.84, 3.85

Others answered, “The home of our fathers and grandfathers cannot be dropped in a moment. Let us frighten the elephants somehow, so that with luck they stay away. Even a snake with no poison should spread a wide hood. Poison or no poison, the spread hood is what frightens.”

Sanskrit verse 3.86

Then others said, “If so, there is one great fright that will keep them away, and it needs a clever envoy. Our lord, the hare Vijayadatta, lives in the disc of the Moon. Let us send a false envoy to the leader of the herd, to say that the Moon forbids him to come to this lake because the Moon’s own people live all around it. If it is said in a way he can believe, he may turn back.”

Some said, “Then there is the hare called Lambakarna, Long-Ears. He knows how to put words together and he knows an envoy’s work. Send him. A king’s envoy should be pleasant to look at, free of greed, a good speaker, learned in many subjects and able to read another’s mind. Whoever sends a fool, a greedy person or, worst of all, a liar to a king’s door finds that his business fails.

Sanskrit verse 3.87

Let us see whether there is a way out of this trouble for us.” The rest agreed. “This is right,” they said. “There is no other way for us to live. Let it be done.”

So Lambakarna was chosen, and he went. He reached the path the elephants used and climbed to a high place they could not get to. From there he called to the elephant king, “You wicked elephant. You come to this Moon Lake at your ease and without fear. You must not come here. Turn back.”

The elephant was astonished and asked who he was. “I am the hare Lambakarna,” he said, “and I live in the disc of the Moon. The blessed Moon has now sent me to you as his envoy.

You know the rule. An envoy who says what he was told to say is not to be blamed, for every king speaks through the mouth of his envoy. Even when weapons are raised and kinsmen have been killed, a king does not kill envoys, however harsh their words.”

Sanskrit verse 3.89

“Then tell me the blessed Moon’s message, hare,” said the elephant, “so that it can be carried out at once.” Lambakarna said, “Yesterday, when you came with your herd, you struck down a great many hares. You must know that they are under my care. If you value your life, do not come to this lake again for any reason at all. That is the message.”

The elephant asked where the blessed Moon was at present. “Here in the lake,” said the hare. “He has come to comfort the hares your herd trampled, those who are left alive. I was sent to you.” “If that is so,” said the elephant, “show me the lord, so that I may bow to him and go somewhere else.” “Come with me, alone,” said the hare, “and you shall see him.”

At night the hare led him to the edge of the lake and showed him the round image of the moon lying in the water. “There is our lord,” he said, “sitting in deep meditation in the middle of the water. Bow to him quietly and go. If his meditation is broken, he will be far more angry than before.”

The elephant was frightened. He bowed to the moon in the water and went away. From that day the hares lived in comfort in their own homes with their families.

“That is why I say,” the crow finished, “that the name of the great is enough to bring success. The hares live in peace because they named the Moon.”

Sanskrit verse 3.81

What this story shows

The hares could not fight the herd. Their envoy used the Moon’s name, and the elephant king believed him and left.

The hares are small and the herd is vast. They talk through their choices in order: leave the land, stay and bluff, or send someone who can speak. Each view is given its reasons, and the council settles on the one that needs skill and a steady voice.

The crow tells the story for one point, that those who live under a great name are sheltered by it. The envoy’s message was untrue, and the text says so plainly when the hares call him a false envoy. What the story shows is how fear of a greater power can turn a strong one aside.

  • The herd did its harm without meaning to, and the hares still had to answer it.
  • The hares heard every view before they chose one.
  • The elephant king turned back out of respect for a power greater than his own.

A long drought dried up every pool, and the elephants were dying of thirst. Their king remembered a lake that never ran dry and led the herd toward it.

After five nights the herd reached the lake and bathed until sunset. All around it were the burrows of hares, and the elephants trampled them. Many hares were hurt or killed.

The hares who were left met in council, sure the herd would come every day. One said to leave the land, and others said to stay and frighten the elephants off. They settled on sending an envoy who would speak in the Moon’s name.

The hare chosen as envoy climbed a high rock out of reach and called to the elephant king. He said the Moon had sent him. The hares were the Moon’s own people, and the herd must not come to the lake again.

The elephant king asked to see the Moon. At night the hare led him to the water and showed him the moon’s reflection, saying the lord sat there in meditation and must not be disturbed. The elephant bowed in fear.

The elephant king went away, and the herd did not come back. From that day the hares lived safely in their own homes. The crow tells this to the birds to show how a great name shelters the small.
