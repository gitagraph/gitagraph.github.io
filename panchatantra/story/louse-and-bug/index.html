The louse and the bug

Damanaka tells this to the lion Pingalaka, to make him doubt the bull he has taken in.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

“Do not give shelter to one whose nature you do not know,” Damanaka said to the lion. “Through a bug's fault, the louse Mandavisarpini was killed.” “How was that?” asked Pingalaka.

A certain king had a beautiful bed. Between its two white sheets lived a louse named Mandavisarpini. She fed on the king's blood and passed her days in comfort.

One day a bug named Agnimukha came wandering into the bed. The louse's face fell when she saw him. “Agnimukha, why have you come to a place where you do not belong? Leave quickly, before anyone finds out.”

“Lady, that is no way to speak to a guest, even a bad one,” said the bug. “A guest should hear: Come in, sit down, it has been so long, are you well.”

“Besides, I have tasted the blood of many people. It was always sharp or bitter or sour from what they ate. I have never once tasted sweet blood. This king eats fine food of every kind, and his blood must be sweet. Let my tongue have that pleasure. A hungry guest has come to your house. It is not right that you should feed on this king alone.”

“I feed on him only when he has fallen asleep,” said Mandavisarpini. “You are Agnimukha, the fire-mouthed, and you are hasty. If you will drink when I drink, then stay, and taste the blood you long for.”

“Lady, I will do as you say. I swear by the gods and by my teacher that I will not taste the king's blood until you have tasted it first.”

While they were still talking, the king came to his bed and lay down. The bug's greedy tongue could not wait. He bit the king while he was still awake.

Nature is not changed by advice. Water grows cold again, however well it is heated.

The king sprang up from the bed as if a needle had pricked him. “Look for it. There is a bug or a louse in this bedding, and it has bitten me.”

The servants of the bedchamber took up the bedding and searched it closely. The bug was quick, and slipped into a crack of the bed frame. Mandavisarpini was found in a fold of the cloth, and they killed her.

“That is why I say,” Damanaka finished, “do not give shelter to one whose nature you do not know.”

“Knowing this, you must kill the bull. If you do not, he will kill you.”

What this story shows

A promise does not change what someone is.

The louse knew the danger. She told the bug to leave, and she named his fault to his face: he was hasty. Then he talked about manners and hunger, and swore an oath, and she let him stay. The oath lasted until the king lay down. The story's own comment is that advice does not change a nature, any more than heating changes water for long.

The one who paid was the careful one. The bug was quick and got away. The louse was slow, was found, and died for a bite she did not take. Damanaka tells this to the lion so that he will see the bull as the bug. The bull has done nothing of the kind. The story is true about hasty guests, and Damanaka is using a true story to carry a lie.

  • Know someone's habits before you share your place with them.
  • An oath sworn in hunger is tested the moment food arrives.
  • When a rule is broken, the slow one is often the one who is caught.
