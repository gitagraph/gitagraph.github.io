The brahmin’s wife and the mongoose

The judges tell this to the merchant after the barber’s crime.

The cards tell it quickly, in pictures. The full story below has the voices and the verses, and is worth the time.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In a certain town lived a brahmin named Devasharma. His wife gave birth to a son, and on the same day a she-mongoose gave birth to a young one and died. The brahmin’s wife loved children, and she reared the little mongoose like a son, with her own milk, with oil and with rubbing.

But she did not trust him. Love for one’s own child goes beyond every other love, and she was always afraid that the fault of his kind would one day make him harm her boy. The text says here that even a bad son gladdens the heart, that the touch of a son is cooler than sandal paste, and that no bond holds people as the bond of a son does.

Sanskrit verses 5.18, 5.19, 5.20

One day she laid her son on the bed, took up the water pot and said to her husband, “I am going to the pond for water. Guard the boy from the mongoose.” When she had gone, the brahmin too went off somewhere to beg for alms and left the house empty.

The source skips what happened next. From what follows it is plain that a black snake came toward the sleeping child, that the mongoose fought it and killed it, and that he then ran out to meet the mother with blood on his mouth.

The mother saw his blood-stained mouth and her heart misgave her. She was sure the wretch had eaten her child, and in her anger she threw the water pot down on him. So she killed the mongoose.

She came into the house wailing, and there was her son asleep just as she had left him, and beside him a black snake cut to pieces. She began to beat her head and her breast in grief for the son she had killed.

Just then the brahmin came back with his alms. His wife, burning with grief, cried out, “Greedy man. Greed got the better of you and you did not do as I asked. Now taste the fruit of this tree of sorrow, the death of a son.” She said that greed should be kept within measure, and when he asked what she meant she told him a tale.

What this story shows

She had the truth one room away and did not look.

The mongoose does exactly what a brother would do. The mother sees one thing, the blood, and reads it by the fear she has always carried about him. The child is asleep a few steps away.

The judges use the tale against the barber, who saw one event and never asked what lay behind it. The tale is harder on the husband than on the wife. She asked him to stay, and he went out for alms.

  • One sight, read through an old fear, was enough for her to kill.
  • The check she needed would have taken a moment.
  • The husband left his post, and she names that as the cause.

A brahmin’s wife had a baby son, and she reared an orphaned mongoose alongside him like a second child. She loved the mongoose and never quite trusted him.

One day she went for water and asked her husband to watch the child, and he went out too. While they were away a snake came for the baby, and the mongoose killed it. He ran to the door to meet her.

She saw his mouth and was sure he had eaten her son. In her anger she threw the water pot down on him, and he died.

Inside, the baby was asleep and unharmed, and the snake lay dead beside him. She wept for the mongoose and told her husband that his greed for alms had caused it.

What this story shows

She had the truth one room away and did not look.
