A king, three princes and a promise

How the five books came to be told.

First time here: this story is from the Panchatantra. See what that is.

In the south of the country there was a city called Mahilaropya. Its king was Amarashakti. He was generous and accomplished, and he had three sons: Vasushakti, Ugrashakti and Anekashakti.

The three princes would not study. Books bored them. Advice slid off them. The text says plainly that they were very slow and had turned their backs on learning.

One day the king called his ministers. “You know my sons,” he said. “When I look at them, this whole kingdom gives me no joy. What use is a cow that gives neither calf nor milk? What use is a son with neither learning nor devotion? Find a way to wake up their minds.”

Five hundred scholars lived on the king's support. One of them spoke. “Your Majesty, grammar takes twelve years. Then come the books on duty, the books on wealth and rule, and the books on love. After all that, the mind wakes up.”

Twelve years, and that was only the start.

Then a minister named Sumati spoke. “Life is short. The study of words has no far shore, and there are many obstacles. So take the heart of it and leave the rest, as the swan is said to draw milk out of water. There is a Brahmin here called Vishnusharma. He knows every science, and students speak his name with respect. Give the princes to him.”

The king sent for Vishnusharma. “Make my sons second to none in the art of ruling, and I will give you a hundred grants of land.”

Vishnusharma's answer is the best moment in the story.

“Hear the plain truth, Your Majesty. I do not sell learning, even for a hundred grants. If in six months your sons do not know the art of wise conduct, I will give up my name.”

Six months, against twelve years. The king and his ministers were astonished and delighted. The princes went with their new teacher.

Vishnusharma composed five books of stories and taught the princes with them.

In one, a lion listens to the wrong adviser. In another, a crow, a mouse, a tortoise and a deer become friends. Crows go to war with owls. A monkey trusts a crocodile. A man acts before he looks. Inside each story someone stops to tell another story, and sometimes there is one more inside that.

The princes kept listening, because they wanted to know what happened next. In six months they were all that their teacher had promised.

Since then, the text says, these five books have travelled the earth to teach the young.

That was long ago. They are still travelling, and now they have reached you.

What this story shows

A rule tells you what to do. A story lets you watch what happens.

The scholars offered the princes twelve years of rules. Vishnusharma offered them a lion, a jackal and a monkey, and they could not stop listening.

A story does its teaching quietly. You follow someone into trouble, you see how it ends, and you remember it because you were there. These stories have since been retold in more than fifty languages.

  • Take the heart of a subject first. The rest can wait.
  • A good teacher is known by what the students become.
  • What holds your attention is what stays with you.

On a minister's advice the king sent for Vishnusharma and offered him a hundred grants of land. “I do not sell learning,” said Vishnusharma. “Six months, or I give up my name.”

He made five books of stories and taught the princes with them.

In six months the princes were all he had promised. The five books have been travelling ever since.
