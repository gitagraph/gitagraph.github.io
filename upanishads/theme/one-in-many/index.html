The one in the many

Salt in water, a seed in a fig: one reality under many forms.

  • Chandogya Upanishad · 6.1–6.16Shvetaketu: 'You are that'

    A proud young scholar comes home, and his father teaches him with salt and a seed.

    For a graduate proud of a new degree, or anyone curious about what 'self' really means.
  • Mundaka Upanishad · 3.1.1–7Two birds on one tree

    One bird eats the fruit and frets; the other only watches. Which one are you?

    For anyone who has noticed a quiet part of them watching their own thoughts.
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad · 2.4 and 4.5Maitreyi asks for what does not die

    Offered a share of the household, Maitreyi asks: will any of this keep me from dying?

    For anyone curious about why we love the people we love.
  • Chandogya Upanishad · 4.5–4.9Satyakama's four teachers

    Alone with a herd for years, a young man learns from a bull, fire and birds.

    For anyone who has learned as much from quiet, ordinary hours as from any classroom.
  • Isha Upanishad · 1–2, 4–8, 15–16Everything is wrapped in the Lord

    Two short lines that tell you how to hold everything you own.

    For anyone setting up a first home or a first job, and wondering how to hold what they have.
  • Mundaka Upanishad · 1.1.1–7, 1.2.12–13Shaunaka's question: two kinds of knowing

    A wealthy householder asks for the one thing that makes everything else make sense.

    For a student wondering whether all this studying adds up to wisdom.
  • Chandogya Upanishad · 5.11–5.18Six scholars learn from King Ashvapati

    Six famous scholars each knew one piece of the self. A king showed them the whole.

    For anyone curious how the sky, the sun and the earth could all be part of one self.
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad · 3.6, 3.8Gargi questions Yajnavalkya

    Silenced once before a hall of scholars, Gargi rises again with two questions like arrows.

    For a student who loves asking "and what is that resting on?"
  • Mandukya Upanishad · 1–12Om and the four states

    Waking, dreaming, dreamless sleep: the shortest Upanishad points to what stays through all three.

    For anyone who has wondered where "I" go in deep, dreamless sleep.
