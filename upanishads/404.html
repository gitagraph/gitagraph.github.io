Upanishads for all

For curious minds — read, explore, reflect. Stories from India's oldest philosophy, retold from the Sanskrit for first-time readers and for those who know them well.

All stories

  • Katha Upanishad · 1.1–1.2Nachiketa and Death

    Given to Death by his angry father, a boy asks what happens after we die.

    For someone young weighing what is worth wanting, or anyone wondering what outlasts death.
  • Chandogya Upanishad · 4.4Satyakama tells the truth

    A boy who does not know his father tells a stranger exactly that, and is accepted.

    For a student starting somewhere new, or anyone who has wondered whether to tell the plain truth about themselves.
  • Chandogya Upanishad · 6.1–6.16Shvetaketu: 'You are that'

    A proud young scholar comes home, and his father teaches him with salt and a seed.

    For a graduate proud of a new degree, or anyone curious about what 'self' really means.
  • Chandogya Upanishad · 8.7–8.12Indra, Virochana and the reflection in water

    Two kings look into a pan of water. One goes home satisfied; the other keeps asking.

    For anyone who has looked in a mirror and wondered who is doing the looking.
  • Mundaka Upanishad · 3.1.1–7Two birds on one tree

    One bird eats the fruit and frets; the other only watches. Which one are you?

    For anyone who has noticed a quiet part of them watching their own thoughts.
  • Taittiriya Upanishad · 3.1–3.6Bhrigu finds bliss

    A son asks his father what is real. Again and again, the father sends him back.

    For a student wondering what really lies at the center of a life.
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad · 2.4 and 4.5Maitreyi asks for what does not die

    Offered a share of the household, Maitreyi asks: will any of this keep me from dying?

    For anyone curious about why we love the people we love.
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad · 5.2–5.3What the thunder says: Da, Da, Da

    Three kinds of students, one teacher, one syllable, and three different lessons.

    For anyone who has stood under a monsoon sky and listened to the thunder.
  • Kena Upanishad · 3–4The Yaksha, the gods and Uma

    The fire god boasts he can burn everything. A single blade of grass will not burn.

    For anyone who has wondered where their own talent and strength really come from.
  • Katha Upanishad · 1.3.3–15The chariot of the self

    Death shows a boy a chariot, wild horses, and a rider who is none of them.

    For anyone curious about who is really in charge when they make a choice.
  • Chandogya Upanishad · 4.5–4.9Satyakama's four teachers

    Alone with a herd for years, a young man learns from a bull, fire and birds.

    For anyone who has learned as much from quiet, ordinary hours as from any classroom.
  • Chandogya Upanishad · 4.1–4.3Raikva, the man with the cart

    A famously generous king overhears two swans say a man with a cart outshines him.

    For anyone who has done well in life and quietly wonders what it all adds up to.
  • Chandogya Upanishad · 7.1–7.26Narada learns that names are not enough

    He had studied every subject there was, and he was still unhappy. He asked why.

    For anyone who loves learning and wonders where all of it finally leads.
  • Taittiriya Upanishad · 1.11The teacher's farewell

    A teacher's parting words to a student about to go out into the world.

    For a student finishing school or college and stepping into a life of their own.
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad · 2.1Proud Balaki and King Ajatashatru

    A scholar comes to teach a king, runs out of answers, and learns from a sleeper.

    For someone proud of their degree, and curious about what it still leaves out.
  • Chandogya Upanishad · 5.1The quarrel of the senses

    Speech, sight, hearing and mind each walked out for a year. Then breath stirred.

    For anyone who has ever argued about who on a team matters most, or simply wondered what keeps us alive.
  • Chandogya Upanishad · 4.10–4.15Upakosala and the fires

    Twelve years of service, no teaching, a heartsick student. Then the fires start to speak.

    For anyone who has wondered who is really looking out through their own eyes.
  • Chandogya Upanishad · 1.10–1.11Ushasti in the famine

    A starving scholar eats a stranger's leftovers, then halts the singing at a king's sacrifice.

    For anyone who does their work by routine and sometimes wonders what it is really for.
  • Isha Upanishad · 1–2, 4–8, 15–16Everything is wrapped in the Lord

    Two short lines that tell you how to hold everything you own.

    For anyone setting up a first home or a first job, and wondering how to hold what they have.
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad · 1.3.28From what is not to what is

    Three lines murmured during an ancient chant, and the Upanishad's own key to what they mean.

    For anyone who has recited these three lines at school or at home and wondered where they come from.
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad · 4.3.1–6Janaka asks: what is a person's light?

    The sun sets, the moon sets, the fire dies, the voices stop. What light is left?

    For anyone who has sat in a dark room and wondered how they still know they are there.
  • Mundaka Upanishad · 1.1.1–7, 1.2.12–13Shaunaka's question: two kinds of knowing

    A wealthy householder asks for the one thing that makes everything else make sense.

    For a student wondering whether all this studying adds up to wisdom.
  • Chandogya Upanishad · 5.11–5.18Six scholars learn from King Ashvapati

    Six famous scholars each knew one piece of the self. A king showed them the whole.

    For anyone curious how the sky, the sun and the earth could all be part of one self.
  • Prashna Upanishad · 1.1–1.5, 6.1–6.8Six seekers come to Pippalada

    Six learned men bring their questions to a sage. He tells them to wait a year.

    For anyone starting out with a new teacher, coach or mentor, and eager for answers.
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad · 3.6, 3.8Gargi questions Yajnavalkya

    Silenced once before a hall of scholars, Gargi rises again with two questions like arrows.

    For a student who loves asking "and what is that resting on?"
  • Mandukya Upanishad · 1–12Om and the four states

    Waking, dreaming, dreamless sleep: the shortest Upanishad points to what stays through all three.

    For anyone who has wondered where "I" go in deep, dreamless sleep.
